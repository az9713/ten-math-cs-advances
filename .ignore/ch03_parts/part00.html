0. Motivation — infinite symmetry, finite shuffles

Can every infinite group be simulated, as accurately as you like, by shuffling a large finite deck? Make the question precise in the humblest possible way. A group is its multiplication table. Pick any finite fragment of that table — finitely many elements and their pairwise products. Ask for a large finite set $Y$ and, for each chosen group element, a permutation of $Y$, such that (i) the permutations compose the way the table says they should at almost every point of $Y$, and (ii) every nonidentity element's permutation moves almost every point. Groups admitting such simulations at every precision are called sofic (Fig. 1). Gromov introduced the idea in 1999 in his work on symbolic dynamics; Weiss named it and asked the innocent-sounding question: is there a group that is not sofic? For a quarter century the question — the soficity conjecture, in its optimistic phrasing “every countable group is sofic” — absorbed attacks from group theory, dynamics, operator algebras, and quantum information, and every candidate obstruction either failed or remained conditional. Chapter 3 answers it. There is a nonsofic group, and it is startlingly concrete: the group of invertible elements of a ring on four generators over the field of two elements.

Here is the villain. Let $\mathbb F_2$ be the field with two elements, and let $$R \;=\; L_{\mathbb F_2}(1,2) \;=\; \mathbb F_2\bigl\langle\, s_0, s_1, t_0, t_1 \;\big|\; t_i s_j = \delta_{ij},\; s_0 t_0 + s_1 t_1 = 1 \,\bigr\rangle \tag{1}$$ be the binary Leavitt algebra: the ring on four noncommuting generators, coefficients mod $2$, subject to five short relations ($\delta_{ij}$ is $1$ if $i = j$, else $0$). Write $R^\times$ for its group of invertible elements. The relations encode something a linear-algebra eye recognizes instantly: $R$ is a ring isomorphic, as a module over itself, to two copies of itself — a violation of dimension-invariance that no field, and no ring of matrices over a field, can commit. That self-similarity is the engine of everything below.

Theorem 1.1 (proved in §8). The unit group $L_{\mathbb F_2}(1,2)^\times$ is not sofic.

The proof is a three-act collision of machines built by different communities:

  • Act one (rigidity). Kazhdan's property (T) — a rigidity property of infinite groups — forces any sofic simulation of a property-(T) group to organize itself, after negligible edits, into a disjoint union of expander graphs (Kun's theorem). A companion theorem of Kun and Thom says: if the simulation is a single expander, then any group commuting with the property-(T) group must be LEF — simulable by finite groups exactly, not just approximately. The gap between “union of expanders” and “one expander” is genuine, and closing it is the chapter's analytic contribution (§4): a matching argument shows that a suitable contracting conjugation ($t\Gamma t^{-1} \le \Gamma$) forbids the simulation from fragmenting.
  • Act two (self-similarity). Inside $R^\times$, the binary prefix structure of the Leavitt algebra manufactures exactly the objects Act one needs: a finitely generated property-(T) group $G$ (an elementary matrix group made internal to $R$), a property-(T) subgroup $\Gamma$ acting on “half the space”, contracting units $u, v$, and — crucially — a commuting copy $J$ of Thompson's group $V$, an infinite finitely presented simple group (§5–§7).
  • Act three (contradiction). A finitely presented LEF group must be residually finite; an infinite simple group cannot be. So $V$ is not LEF (§2) — yet soficity of $G$ would force it to be. Hence $G$, and with it $R^\times$, is not sofic (§8).

The plan: §1 and §2 build the two ends of the trap (soficity, and the non-LEF group $V$) from nothing, proving all the foundational lemmas. §3 is a self-contained primer on property (T) and expanders — written to be reusable by Chapter 4's explainer — stating the two imported expander theorems and showing, by a known example, why the gap between them is real. §4 proves the bridging Proposition 2.3 in full. §5–§7 build the Leavitt configuration, proving every algebraic identity. §8 assembles the theorem and maps what it settles; §9 re-verifies all of §5–§7 by machine; §10 is the honest scorecard.
