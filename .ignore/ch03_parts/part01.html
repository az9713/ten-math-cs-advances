1. Sofic groups from zero

Notation first. A group $H$ is a set with an associative multiplication, an identity $1$, and inverses; it is countable if its elements can be listed, and finitely generated if some finite subset generates it (every element is a finite product of generators and their inverses). For a nonempty finite set $Y$, $\mathrm{Sym}(Y)$ is the group of permutations (bijections $Y \to Y$), and the normalized Hamming distance between two permutations counts the disagreement fraction: $$d_{\mathrm H}(p, q) \;=\; \frac{\bigl|\{z \in Y : pz \neq qz\}\bigr|}{|Y|} \qquad (p, q \in \mathrm{Sym}(Y)).$$ Thus $d_{\mathrm H}(p, q) = 0$ means $p = q$, and $d_{\mathrm H}(p, \mathrm{id}) = 1$ means $p$ moves every point.

Definition (sofic approximation; sofic group). A sofic approximation of a countable group $H$ is a sequence of maps $p_n : H \to \mathrm{Sym}(Y_n)$, for nonempty finite sets $Y_n$, satisfying $p_n(1) = 1$ and $$d_{\mathrm H}\bigl(p_n(gh),\, p_n(g)p_n(h)\bigr) \longrightarrow 0 \quad (\text{every } g, h \in H), \qquad d_{\mathrm H}\bigl(p_n(g),\, 1\bigr) \longrightarrow 1 \quad (\text{every } g \neq 1).$$ The maps need not be homomorphisms — that is the whole point: multiplication must hold at almost every point of $Y_n$ (asymptotically), and each nonidentity element must move almost every point. $H$ is sofic if it admits a sofic approximation. Replacing each $Y_n$ by many disjoint copies of itself (with the same permutation on each copy) changes no distances, so we may always assume $|Y_n| \to \infty$.

Some calibration, with proofs, before the machinery arrives. First: exact models are allowed, and two huge classes of groups have them.

Toy 1 — finite groups are sofic, exactly. For finite $H$ take every $Y_n = H$ and $p_n(g) = $ left multiplication by $g$. Multiplication holds at every point ($g(hz) = (gh)z$ is associativity), and for $g \neq 1$, $gz = z$ would force $g = 1$, so $p_n(g)$ moves every point. Both defects are exactly $0$ and $1$ — no asymptotics needed.
Definition (residually finite). $H$ is residually finite if for every $g \neq 1$ there is a homomorphism $\varphi$ from $H$ onto a finite group with $\varphi(g) \neq 1$: every nonidentity element survives into some finite quotient. Examples: free groups, $\mathbb Z^d$, and $\mathrm{SL}_d(\mathbb Z)$ are residually finite (reduce matrices mod $m$).
Proposition A. Every residually finite group is sofic.

Let $H$ be residually finite and first suppose it is finitely generated, with generating set $S$. For each $n$, the ball $B_n \subseteq H$ of radius $n$ in the generators is finite, so there is a homomorphism $\varphi_n$ onto a finite group $F_n$ injective on $B_n$ (take the product of finitely many quotients, one killing each bad coincidence $\varphi(x) = \varphi(y)$ for distinct $x, y \in B_n$ — a finite product of finite groups is finite). Let $p_n(g)$ be left multiplication by $\varphi_n(g)$ on $Y_n = F_n$. Multiplication is exact ($\varphi_n$ is a homomorphism), and for fixed $g \neq 1$: once $n$ exceeds the length of $g$, injectivity on $B_n$ gives $\varphi_n(g) \neq 1$, whose left multiplication moves every point. So $p_n$ is a sofic approximation. The general countable case follows from Proposition C below, since every finitely generated subgroup of a residually finite group is residually finite (restrict the homomorphisms). ∎

Second: where does the “almost” genuinely work? The simplest showcase is $\mathbb Z$ approximated without exploiting its finite quotients (Fig. 2): shift the interval $\{1, \dots, n\}$ one step right. The shift is only partially defined — the last point has nowhere to go — so patch it arbitrarily (send it to the front). The patch breaks multiplicativity at a vanishing fraction $O(k/n)$ of points for the word test $p(1)^k$ vs $p(k)$; the same recipe with Følner sets in place of intervals proves every amenable group sofic, which we note but do not need.

Third: two closure properties we will use, with one-line proofs, and a longer list we will not.

Proposition B. Every subgroup of a sofic group is sofic.

Restrict a sofic approximation of the big group to the subgroup: the defining conditions quantify over elements one at a time (or in pairs) and survive restriction verbatim. ∎

Proposition C. If every finitely generated subgroup of a countable group $H$ is sofic, then $H$ is sofic.

List $H = \{h_1, h_2, \dots\}$ and let $H_k = \langle h_1, \dots, h_k\rangle$. Since $H_k$ is sofic, there are a finite set $Y_k$ and a map $q_k : H_k \to \mathrm{Sym}(Y_k)$ with $q_k(1) = 1$ whose multiplicative defect on every pair from $\{h_1, \dots, h_k\}^{\pm 1}$-products of length $\le k$… more simply: whose defects satisfy $d_{\mathrm H}(q_k(gh), q_k(g)q_k(h)) \le 1/k$ and $d_{\mathrm H}(q_k(g), 1) \ge 1 - 1/k$ for all $g, h$ in the finite set $\{h_i h_j, h_i : i, j \le k\} \setminus \{1\}$ (pick a term far enough along $H_k$'s sofic approximation; only finitely many conditions are imposed). Define $p_k : H \to \mathrm{Sym}(Y_k)$ by $p_k(g) = q_k(g)$ for $g \in H_k$ and $p_k(g) = 1$ otherwise. For any fixed $g, h \in H$, all of $g, h, gh$ lie in $\{h_i, h_ih_j : i,j \le k\}$ for every large $k$, so both limits hold. ∎

Why a nonsofic group was so elusive. Soficity survives essentially every standard construction: subgroups and direct limits (above), direct and free products, extensions with amenable quotient, amalgams over amenable subgroups, inverse limits, graph products… (see Pestov's survey [Pes08] for the full list). Every group anyone can write down by combining familiar pieces is therefore sofic. Earlier routes to a counterexample — Bowen–Burton via flexible stability of $\mathrm{PSL}_d(\mathbb Z)$ [BB20], Gohla–Thom and Chapman–Dikstein–Lubotzky via central extensions of $p$-adic lattices [GT24, CDL24] — were all conditional on unproved stability hypotheses. The related (strictly stronger) Aldous–Lyons conjecture about unimodular random networks was disproved in 2024–25 by Bowen, Chapman, Lubotzky, and Vidick [BCLV24, BCV25] using the quantum-information machinery behind $\mathsf{MIP}^* = \mathsf{RE}$; but a non-co-sofic invariant random subgroup need not be concentrated on a normal subgroup, so that breakthrough did not produce a nonsofic group. Theorem 1.1 needs no unproved hypothesis and no quantum machinery. (§8 returns to this landscape.)

2. The obstruction: LEF groups and Thompson's group $V$

To prove a group is not sofic we need a consequence of soficity that some concrete group visibly lacks. The consequence will be an exact finite approximation property:

Definition (LEF). A group $J$ is locally embeddable into finite groups (LEF) if for every finite subset $F \subseteq J$ there are a finite group $B$ and an injection $\phi : F \to B$ with $$\phi(xy) = \phi(x)\phi(y) \qquad \text{whenever } x, y, xy \in F.$$ The multiplication table of $F$ embeds exactly; only the receiving finite group may vary with $F$.

LEF sits strictly inside sofic (exact beats approximate):

Proposition D. Every LEF group is sofic. In particular every residually finite group is LEF and sofic (take $B$ = a finite quotient injective on $F$).

Fix a finite $F_n \subseteq J$ increasing to $J$ (countability; for uncountable $J$ soficity is defined via countable subgroups, which we do not need). We may assume $1 \in F_n$; note $\phi(1) = \phi(1)\phi(1)$ forces $\phi(1) = 1$ when $1, 1\cdot 1 \in F_n$. Let $\phi_n : F_n \to B_n$ be a local embedding and let $p_n(g)$ be left multiplication by $\phi_n(g)$ on $Y_n = B_n$ for $g \in F_n$, and the identity otherwise. For fixed $g, h$: eventually $g, h, gh \in F_n$, and then $p_n(g)p_n(h) = p_n(gh)$ exactly; for fixed $g \neq 1$: eventually $\phi_n(g) \neq \phi_n(1) = 1$ by injectivity, and left multiplication by a nonidentity element moves every point. ∎

The wedge that will crack the conjecture is a pair of rigidity facts pulling in opposite directions (Fig. 4):

Proposition E (finitely presented + LEF $\Rightarrow$ residually finite). Suppose $J = \langle x_1, \dots, x_d \mid \rho_1, \dots, \rho_r\rangle$ is a finitely presented LEF group (finitely many generators and finitely many defining relations $\rho_i$, words in the generators whose consequences are all relations of $J$). Then $J$ is residually finite.

Fix $1 \neq g \in J$ and write $g$ as a word of length $\ell$ in the generators. Let $F \subseteq J$ be the ball of radius $L = \max(\ell, \max_i |\rho_i|)$ in the generators — a finite set containing $1$, the generators, $g$, and every prefix-evaluation of every relator. Take a local embedding $\phi : F \to B$. Define a homomorphism $\psi$ from the free group on $x_1, \dots, x_d$ to $B$ by $\psi(x_i) = \phi(x_i)$. For any word $w$ of length $\le L$, all prefixes of $w$ evaluate inside $F$, and multiplying one generator at a time, $\phi$'s partial multiplicativity gives $\psi(w) = \phi(\overline w)$, where $\overline w \in J$ is the evaluation of $w$. In particular $\psi(\rho_i) = \phi(\overline{\rho_i}) = \phi(1) = 1$ for every relator, so $\psi$ descends to a homomorphism $\overline\psi : J \to B$; and $\overline\psi(g) = \phi(g) \neq \phi(1) = 1$ by injectivity of $\phi$ on $F$. Thus every nonidentity $g$ survives in a finite quotient. ∎

Proposition F (infinite simple $\Rightarrow$ not residually finite, hence not LEF if finitely presented). A group is simple if its only normal subgroups are $\{1\}$ and itself. An infinite simple group $J$ has no nontrivial homomorphism to any finite group, so it is not residually finite; if moreover $J$ is finitely presented, Proposition E shows $J$ is not LEF.

Let $\varphi : J \to B$ be a homomorphism to a finite group. Its kernel is normal, hence $\{1\}$ or $J$. Kernel $\{1\}$ would embed the infinite $J$ into the finite $B$ — impossible. So the kernel is everything: $\varphi \equiv 1$, and no nonidentity element survives into any finite quotient. ∎

2.1 Thompson's group $V$

Now the concrete group that will play the role of $J$. Work with the space of infinite binary strings $\omega \in \{0,1\}^{\mathbb N}$. For a finite binary word $a$, the cylinder $[a]$ is the set of infinite strings beginning with $a$. A prefix code is a finite list $E = (a_1, \dots, a_q)$ of words, none a prefix of another (so the cylinders $[a_i]$ are pairwise disjoint); it is complete if the cylinders $[a_i]$ partition all infinite strings. Splitting a cylinder $[a]$ into its children $[a0], [a1]$ repeatedly produces complete prefix codes with any prescribed number of words.

Definition (prefix replacement; Thompson's group $V$). Take two complete prefix codes $E = (a_1, \dots, a_q)$, $E' = (b_1, \dots, b_q)$ with a bijection $a_i \mapsto b_i$. Every infinite string is uniquely $a_i\omega$ with $a_i \in E$ and $\omega$ an infinite tail; the prefix replacement acts by $$g(a_i \omega) = b_i \omega$$ — swap the initial word, keep the tail. Completeness of $E'$ makes this a bijection of $\{0,1\}^{\mathbb N}$ (the inverse replaces $b_i$ by $a_i$). Thompson's group $V$ is the group of all prefix replacements. For a finite word $l$, $V_l \cong V$ denotes the copy acting the same way inside the cylinder $[l]$ (replace prefixes after $l$) and fixing everything outside it.

$V$ is visibly infinite (the swaps of $[0^k 1]$ with $[0^{k+1}1]$, say, are distinct for all $k$; distinct replacements differ on some cylinder). Everything else we need about it is classical and deep, and we import it:

Import 4 (Thompson's group $V$) [CFP96; BQ17]. $V$ is finitely presented, infinite, and simple. Moreover $V$ is generated by its cylinder swaps: the involutions $\tau_{\rho,\sigma}$ exchanging two disjoint cylinders $[\rho] \leftrightarrow [\sigma]$ (prefix-incomparable $\rho, \sigma$) and fixing everything else [BQ17, Thm. 1.1]. (Discovered by Richard Thompson in 1965; $V$ was among the first known infinite finitely presented simple groups.)
Corollary G (the obstruction). Thompson's group $V$ is finitely generated but not LEF. (Proof: $V$ is finitely presented, infinite, simple [Import 4]; by Proposition F it is not residually finite and, being finitely presented, not LEF by Proposition E.)
