3. Property (T), expanders, and the two big imports (a reusable primer)

This section is the analytic toolbox: expander graphs, Kazhdan's property (T), one fully proved bridge between them (Proposition H), and the two deep imported theorems — Kun's expander decomposition and the Kun–Thom expander-centralizer theorem — whose gap §4 will close. Nothing here mentions the Leavitt algebra; the primer is written to stand alone (Chapter 4's explainer reuses it).

3.1 Expanders: graphs with no bottlenecks

All graphs here are finite multigraphs: parallel edges are allowed and counted with multiplicity, and loops (edges from a vertex to itself) are allowed but contribute nothing to any boundary. For a graph $L$ and a vertex subset $U \subseteq V(L)$, the edge boundary $\partial_L U$ is the multiset of edges with one endpoint in $U$ and the other in $V(L) \setminus U$. If $C$ is a connected component, $\partial_C U$ means the boundary of $U \subseteq C$ inside the induced graph on $C$.

Definition ($\gamma$-expander). A finite connected multigraph $C$ is a $\gamma$-expander ($\gamma \gt 0$) if $$|\partial_C U| \;\ge\; \gamma \,\min\{|U|,\, |C \setminus U|\} \qquad (U \subseteq C). \tag{3}$$ Every subset, however chosen, leaks at least $\gamma$ boundary edges per vertex on its smaller side: the graph has no bottlenecks at any scale. A family of bounded-degree graphs is an expander family if one $\gamma \gt 0$ works for all of them.

The definition is quantitative, so a computed contrast is the fastest way to calibrate it (Fig. 5). Take two graphs on $16$ vertices with maximum degree $3$. The first is two densely wired $8$-vertex blobs joined by a single bridge edge; brute force over all $2^{16}$ vertex subsets (figgen/ch03_expander_data.py) finds its worst ratio at exactly the blob: $|\partial U|/|U| = 1/8 = 0.125$. The second is the Möbius–Kantor graph (a classical symmetric cubic graph); the same brute force gives $0.75$ — six times better, and no subset does worse. As the bridge picture scales up, the ratio of the bottleneck cut tends to $0$; expander families are exactly the graphs for which this never happens.

3.2 Generator graphs: how a sofic approximation becomes a graph

A sofic approximation $p_n : H \to \mathrm{Sym}(Y_n)$ turns each finite generating set into a sequence of graphs, and all the analysis of §4 happens on those graphs. Throughout, a finite symmetric generating set $S = S^{-1} \subseteq H$ is one closed under inverses and containing the identity $1$. Normalize the approximation so that inverse labels get inverse permutations and the identity label acts exactly as the identity (this changes only a vanishing fraction of values, by [ES06, Lemma 2.1]).

Definition (generator graph). The $S$-generator multigraph $X_n$ of $p_n$ has vertex set $Y_n$ and, for every $z \in Y_n$ and $s \in S$, an arc from $z$ to $p_n(s)z$. Pair each arc with the arc of the inverse label to get an undirected multigraph; fixed points (including the whole identity label) become loops. Parallel edges keep their multiplicity. Since $|S|$ is fixed, these graphs have uniformly bounded degree. The identity loops change no boundary $|\partial U|$; they are kept because they make the random walk on the graph lazy, a technical convenience in Kun's spectral argument.

If $p_n$ were an exact transitive action, $X_n$ would be a Schreier graph of $H$ — connected, and shaped by $H$'s geometry. A sofic approximation is only an almost-action, so $X_n$ can be a mess: near-copies of pieces of $H$'s Cayley graph, wired together by the small fraction of defective edges, possibly in many fragments. The two imported theorems say that property (T) tames this mess.

3.3 Kazhdan's property (T)

Property (T) is a rigidity property of a group's unitary representations. A unitary representation of a countable group $H$ on a (complex, possibly infinite-dimensional) Hilbert space $\mathcal H$ is a homomorphism $\pi$ from $H$ to the group of unitary (inner-product-preserving, invertible) linear operators on $\mathcal H$. A vector $\xi \in \mathcal H$ is invariant if $\pi(g)\xi = \xi$ for all $g$.

Definition (property (T)). Let $S$ be a finite generating set of $H$. The group $H$ has property (T) if there is a constant $\kappa \gt 0$ (a Kazhdan constant for $S$) such that every unitary representation $\pi$ of $H$ without nonzero invariant vectors satisfies $$\max_{s \in S}\, \bigl\| \pi(s)\xi - \xi \bigr\| \;\ge\; \kappa \|\xi\| \qquad (\xi \in \mathcal H).$$ Equivalently: if some unit vector is moved less than $\kappa$ by every generator (an almost-invariant vector), then an exactly invariant vector exists. Whether the property holds does not depend on the choice of finite generating set (changing $S$ changes only $\kappa$).

Two poles calibrate the definition. Finite groups have property (T): given an almost-invariant unit vector $\xi$, the average $\eta = \frac{1}{|H|}\sum_{g} \pi(g)\xi$ is exactly invariant, and a short estimate shows $\eta \neq 0$ when $\xi$ is moved little enough. At the opposite pole, the freest infinite groups fail it — and it is worth seeing the failure concretely, because the mechanism (spread-out bump vectors) is exactly what expansion forbids:

Toy 2 — $\mathbb Z$ does not have property (T). Let $\pi$ be the shift representation of $\mathbb Z$ on $\ell^2(\mathbb Z)$ (square-summable sequences), $(\pi(k)\xi)(x) = \xi(x - k)$, with generating set $S = \{0, \pm 1\}$. There is no nonzero invariant vector: an invariant $\xi$ is a constant sequence, and a nonzero constant is not square-summable. But take the normalized bump $\xi_n = n^{-1/2}\,\mathbf 1_{\{1,\dots,n\}}$: shifting by one changes it in exactly two coordinates, so $\|\pi(\pm 1)\xi_n - \xi_n\|^2 = 2/n \to 0$. Almost-invariant vectors exist at every quality, invariant ones do not — property (T) fails. (This is the “wide bump” trick; compare the interval approximation of Fig. 2, which is the same geometry in permutation form.)

Between the poles, the landmark examples are the higher-rank lattices: $\mathrm{SL}_d(\mathbb Z)$ has property (T) for every $d \ge 3$ (Kazhdan), while $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb Z)$ does not. What this chapter needs is the ring-theoretic analogue of that fact — property (T) for elementary matrix groups over an arbitrary finitely generated ring, stated as Import 3 in §5. For now, keep only the intuition: property (T) means no almost-invariant vectors without invariant ones, uniformly over all representations at once.

The bridge to expanders is completely provable in the case of exact finite actions, and seeing it proved makes the imported theorems believable:

Proposition H (property (T) $\Rightarrow$ exact finite actions expand). Let $H$ have property (T) with Kazhdan constant $\kappa \gt 0$ for the finite symmetric generating set $S$. Let $Y$ be a finite transitive $H$-set with $|Y| \ge 2$ (an exact action, every point reachable from every other). Then the $S$-generator graph on $Y$ satisfies $$|\partial U| \;\ge\; \frac{\kappa^2}{4}\, \min\{|U|,\, |Y \setminus U|\} \qquad (U \subseteq Y),$$ i.e., it is a $\frac{\kappa^2}{4}$-expander.

Let $\ell^2_0(Y)$ be the space of complex functions on $Y$ summing to zero, with the action $(\pi(g)\xi)(y) = \xi(g^{-1}y)$. This is a unitary representation. It has no nonzero invariant vector: an invariant function is constant on orbits, the action is transitive, and a constant summing to zero is zero.

Fix $U \subseteq Y$ with $0 \lt |U| \le |Y|/2$, write $U^c = Y \setminus U$, and consider the centered indicator $$\xi \;=\; \mathbf 1_U - \tfrac{|U|}{|Y|}\,\mathbf 1_Y \;\in\; \ell^2_0(Y), \qquad \|\xi\|^2 = \frac{|U|\,|U^c|}{|Y|} \;\ge\; \frac{|U|}{2}.$$ (The norm: $\xi$ takes value $1 - |U|/|Y|$ on $U$ and $-|U|/|Y|$ on $U^c$; expand and simplify.) Property (T) supplies a generator $s \in S$ with $\|\pi(s)\xi - \xi\| \ge \kappa\|\xi\|$. But $\pi(s)\xi - \xi = \mathbf 1_{sU} - \mathbf 1_U$ (the constants cancel), which is supported on the symmetric difference $sU \,\triangle\, U$ and has modulus $1$ there, so $$\kappa^2 \, \frac{|U|}{2} \;\le\; \kappa^2\|\xi\|^2 \;\le\; \|\pi(s)\xi - \xi\|^2 \;=\; |sU \,\triangle\, U|.$$ Every point of $sU \triangle U$ lies at an end of an $s$-labelled or $s^{-1}$-labelled boundary edge of $U$, and each such edge accounts for at most two points, so $|sU \triangle U| \le 2\,|\partial U|$. Combining, $|\partial U| \ge \frac{\kappa^2}{4}|U|$. ∎

3.4 The two big imports

A sofic approximation is not an exact action, so Proposition H does not apply to it; its defective points can, in principle, disconnect the generator graph or create bottlenecks. Kun's theorem says that for property-(T) groups the damage is confined to a vanishing edit:

Import 1 (Kun's expander decomposition — Theorem 2.1 of the chapter) [Kun19, Thm. 1]. Let $H$ be an infinite finitely generated group with property (T), $S = S^{-1} \ni 1$ a finite generating set, and $p_n : H \to \mathrm{Sym}(Y_n)$ a sofic approximation with $|Y_n| \to \infty$; write $X_n$ for its $S$-generator multigraph. Then there are a constant $\gamma \gt 0$ and unlabelled uniformly-bounded-degree multigraphs $L_n$ on the same vertex sets with $$\bigl|E(X_n) \,\triangle\, E(L_n)\bigr| = o(|Y_n|)$$ such that every connected component of $L_n$ is a $\gamma$-expander. (Conventions: boundaries and symmetric differences count multiplicity; if a convention drops identity loops, adjoin the same loop everywhere on both sides — neither boundaries nor the symmetric difference changes. The reference graphs are unlabelled: their edges need not remember generator labels, and decompositions built for different generating sets need not be compatible.)

Crucially, Import 1 does not say the generator graph becomes one expander. It cannot: given any sofic approximation, the disjoint union of many copies of it is again a sofic approximation, with as many components as you like. The number of components is genuinely uncontrolled — only their internal quality ($\gamma$) is uniform (Fig. 7).

The second import turns a single-expander approximation into the exactness property of §2 — for a group commuting with the property-(T) group:

Import 2 (Kun–Thom's expander-centralizer theorem — Theorem 2.2 of the chapter) [KT19, Thm. 1.1 and §4]. Let $K$ have property (T), let $J$ be finitely generated, and fix a finite symmetric generating set $S_K \ni 1$ of $K$. Suppose $K \times J$ has a sofic approximation $p_n : K \times J \to \mathrm{Sym}(Y_n)$ whose $S_K$-generator multigraph $X_{K,n}$ satisfies, for some $\lambda \gt 0$ and every $n$, $$|\partial_{X_{K,n}} U| \;\ge\; \lambda\, \min\{|U|,\, |Y_n \setminus U|\} \qquad (U \subseteq Y_n)$$ — one $\lambda$-expander on the entire approximation set. Then $J$ is LEF. (Multigraph version; boundaries count multiplicity.)

Why the commuting factor? Intuition: on a single expander, the $K$-action is so well mixed that anything commuting with it is forced to behave almost like an exact symmetry of a finite structure; iterating over finite fragments of $J$ yields exact finite models — LEF. The proof is genuinely hard (it runs through property-(T) cohomology and spectral estimates) and we import it whole.

3.5 The gap between the imports is real

Import 1 outputs many expanders; Import 2 demands one. The gap cannot be waved away, because the conclusion can genuinely fail for a union of expanders. The chapter records a known example. Take $$\Lambda = \mathrm{SL}_3(\mathbb Z), \qquad B = \mathrm{BS}(2,3) = \langle a, b \mid a b^2 a^{-1} = b^3 \rangle.$$ Here $\Lambda$ has property (T) and is residually finite, hence sofic (Proposition A); the Baumslag–Solitar group $B$ is finitely presented and sofic but famously not residually finite [Pes08, Ex. 4.6], hence not LEF by Proposition E. Nevertheless the direct product $\Lambda \times B$ is sofic [ES06, Thm. 1(1)] — soficity is closed under direct products. Run Import 1 on the $\Lambda$-generators of any sofic approximation of $\Lambda \times B$: you get a union of expanding $\Lambda$-components. If some argument could upgrade “union of expanders” to “one expander,” Import 2 would force $B$ to be LEF — false. So no general argument closes the gap: the commuting group can shuffle whole components among themselves, using the freedom between the components that expansion does not police (Fig. 8).

What the example lacks — and what the Leavitt algebra will supply — is nesting. In §4 the ambient group $G$ contains a property-(T) subgroup $\Gamma$ and elements $t_i$ that conjugate $\Gamma$ into itself, $t_i \Gamma t_i^{-1} \le \Gamma$, with the commuting group $J$ also swallowed: $t_1 J t_1^{-1} \le \Gamma$. That self-compression is what the matching argument converts into control over the components.
