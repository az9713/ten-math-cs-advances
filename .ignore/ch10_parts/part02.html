2. The two conjectures, and the two theorems that kill them

2.1 Compactness: is a family only as strong as one member?

Forbidding a finite family $\mathcal F$ is at least as restrictive as forbidding any single member $F \in \mathcal F$, so trivially $\mathrm{ex}(n,\mathcal F) \le \mathrm{ex}(n,F)$ for every member. Erdős and Simonovits conjectured in 1982 [ES82] that this is essentially the only effect: the extremal number of a finite family should be, up to a constant factor, the extremal number of one of its members. As stated, that is false for cheap reasons, and the cheap reason is instructive. Consider the folklore pair $\{K_{1,2},\, 2K_2\}$, where $K_{1,2}$ is the two-edge star (one center, two leaves — a cherry from §1) and $2K_2$ is two disjoint edges (Fig. 5).

Proposition D (the folklore correction-forcer). For $n \ge 4$: $$\mathrm{ex}\bigl(n, \{K_{1,2},\, 2K_2\}\bigr) = 1, \qquad \mathrm{ex}(n, K_{1,2}) = \lfloor n/2 \rfloor, \qquad \mathrm{ex}(n, 2K_2) = n-1.$$

Any two distinct edges either share a vertex (forming $K_{1,2}$) or are disjoint (forming $2K_2$); so a graph avoiding both patterns has at most one edge, and one edge is fine. A $K_{1,2}$-free graph is exactly one with all degrees $\le 1$, i.e. a matching, with at most $\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ edges (achieved). A $2K_2$-free graph $G$ has every two edges sharing a vertex. If $G$ contains a triangle $\{a,b,c\}$: any edge with an endpoint outside $\{a,b,c\}$ misses one of the triangle's three edges entirely, giving $2K_2$; so all edges live on $\{a,b,c\}$, at most $3 \le n-1$ of them. If $G$ is triangle-free with two edges $e_1 \neq e_2$, they share exactly one vertex, say $e_1 = uv$, $e_2 = ux$; any further edge must meet both $e_1$ and $e_2$, hence (barring the triangle edge $vx$) must pass through the shared vertex $u$. So $G$ is a subgraph of a star: at most $n-1$ edges, and the star $K_{1,n-1}$ (one center joined to all others) achieves this. ∎

The two members of the folklore family are forests, and forests have linear extremal numbers — the collapse from $\Theta(n)$ to $\Theta(1)$ is a boundary pathology of acyclic patterns. The corrected conjecture, the form the field believed (see Wigderson's note [Wig] for the history), excludes it:

The compactness conjecture (corrected form). For every finite nonempty family $\mathcal F$ of graphs, all of whose members contain cycles, there exist $F \in \mathcal F$ and $C \gt 0$ such that $$\mathrm{ex}(n, \mathcal F) \;\ge\; \tfrac1C\, \mathrm{ex}(n, F) \qquad \text{for all sufficiently large } n. \tag{1}$$

Context: Conlon, Mulrenin, and Pohoata [CMP26] recently disproved a stronger host-graph analog for even cycles conjectured by Verstraëte, but their examples leave (1) untouched. The chapter kills (1) itself — and does so even with every member connected and bipartite, the hardest regime, where extremal numbers are genuinely polynomial mysteries rather than quadratic ones.

Theorem 1.1 (failure of compactness — proved in §6). There exists a finite nonempty family $\mathcal F$ of connected bipartite graphs, every member containing a cycle, such that, with $\varepsilon = 1/48$: $$\boxed{\;\mathrm{ex}(n, \mathcal F) = O\bigl(n^{4/3-\varepsilon}\bigr) \quad\text{while}\quad \mathrm{ex}(n, F) = \Omega\bigl(n^{4/3}\bigr) \ \text{ for every } F \in \mathcal F.\;}\tag{2}$$ In particular no member of $\mathcal F$ satisfies (1): the family ban is polynomially stronger than every individual ban.

2.2 Degeneracy: are sparse-everywhere patterns cheap to force?

Definition ($r$-degenerate). A graph $H$ is $r$-degenerate if every nonempty subgraph of $H$ contains a vertex of degree at most $r$ (degree measured inside that subgraph). Equivalently — peel vertices of degree $\le r$ one at a time; $r$-degeneracy says the peeling never gets stuck, so reversing it builds $H$ by adding vertices each joined to at most $r$ existing ones. Trees are 1-degenerate; planar graphs are 5-degenerate; $K_{r+1}$ is $r$-degenerate but not $(r-1)$-degenerate.

Degenerate patterns are sparse at every scale: an $r$-degenerate graph on $v$ vertices has at most $rv$ edges, and so does its every subgraph. Erdős conjectured in 1967 [Erd67] that this hereditary sparsity makes them cheap to force:

The degeneracy conjecture. Every fixed bipartite $r$-degenerate graph $H$ satisfies $$\mathrm{ex}(n, H) \;=\; O\bigl(n^{2 - 1/r}\bigr). \tag{3}$$

For $r = 2$ this predicts $O(n^{3/2})$ — every bipartite 2-degenerate pattern would be no harder to force than the four-cycle of §1. The conjecture had real evidence behind it:

Theorem 1.2 (failure of the 2-degenerate bound — proved in §11). There exist a fixed connected bipartite 2-degenerate graph $H$ and constants $c, \varepsilon \gt 0$ such that $$\boxed{\;\mathrm{ex}(n, H) \;\ge\; c\, n^{3/2 + \varepsilon}\;}$$ for all sufficiently large $n$.

Two remarks calibrate the statement. First, fixed means fixed: $H$ does not grow with $n$. It will turn out to be a gigantic but concrete graph (a tower-of-exponentials number of vertices — §11 computes just how gigantic), and the theorem's constants depend on it; but for each $n$ the same $H$ is avoided by some $n$-vertex graph with $n^{3/2+\varepsilon}$ edges. Second, the $\varepsilon$ the proof yields is small — the method caps it near $0.0178$ (§11, computed) — but any $\varepsilon \gt 0$ refutes (3), which demanded $O(n^{3/2})$.

2.3 The two proof strategies at a glance

For Theorem 1.1 the family $\mathcal F$ consists of $C_4$, $C_6$, and all “admissible quotients” of two fixed templates built from subdivided complete bipartite graphs (§3; the design is in the spirit of the rooted-power constructions of Bukh–Conlon [BC18]). The upper bound $\mathrm{ex}(n,\mathcal F) = O(n^{21/16})$ comes from counting short paths in a graph avoiding the family (§4) — note $21/16 = 4/3 - 1/48$, the source of the $\varepsilon$. The lower bound $\mathrm{ex}(n,F) = \Omega(n^{4/3})$ for each individual $F$ comes from incidence graphs of generalized quadrangles over $\mathbb F_q$ (§5) — with the crucial twist that the characteristic of the field is chosen per member: quadrangles over $\mathbb F_{2^j}$ dodge one template's quotients, quadrangles over $\mathbb F_{3^j}$ dodge the other's, and no graph needs to dodge both.

For Theorem 1.2 the pattern $H$ is built in layers, with a vertex for every pair of vertices in the preceding layer — maximally 2-degenerate, in a sense (§7; related to the complete degenerate graphs of [GJN22]). The record-breaking host is random: keep each vertex of a Hamming-distance graph on $\{0,1\}^m$ independently with probability $2^{-\beta m}$ (§9). An embedded copy of $H$ would have to pump a bounded entropy potential up by a fixed amount at every layer (§8–§10) — impossible with enough layers — while a second-moment argument shows the host keeps $\Omega(n^{3/2+\varepsilon})$ edges (§11). The two halves of the chapter share no machinery; what they share is the lesson that both structural beliefs failed.
