1. Extremal numbers from zero: Mantel, Kővári–Sós–Turán, and the first incidence geometry

First the vocabulary, all of it standard. A graph $G$ is a finite set $V(G)$ of vertices together with a set $E(G)$ of edges, each edge an unordered pair of distinct vertices (so: no loops, no repeated edges, no directions — the paper's convention, and ours). Two vertices joined by an edge are adjacent; the neighborhood $N_G(v)$ is the set of vertices adjacent to $v$, and the degree $d(v) = |N_G(v)|$ counts them. We write $\delta(G)$ and $\Delta(G)$ for the minimum and maximum degree, and $e(G) = |E(G)|$ for the edge count. A path is a sequence of distinct vertices, consecutive ones adjacent; a cycle $C_k$ is a closed loop of $k \ge 3$ distinct vertices; a graph is connected if every two vertices are joined by a path. A graph is bipartite if $V(G)$ splits into two classes (also called sides) with every edge running between the classes — equivalently (a classical exercise we will not need) if it contains no odd cycle. The girth of a graph is the length of its shortest cycle.

Containment is the crucial convention: $H \subseteq G$ means $G$ has a subgraph isomorphic to $H$ — some injection $V(H) \hookrightarrow V(G)$ carrying edges to edges. Extra edges of $G$ among the image vertices are irrelevant (this is not “induced subgraph”). Given a family $\mathcal F$ of graphs, $G$ is $\mathcal F$-free if it contains no member of $\mathcal F$ as a subgraph.

Definition (extremal number). For a family $\mathcal F$ and $n \ge 1$, $$\mathrm{ex}(n, \mathcal F) := \max\bigl\{\, |E(G)| \;:\; |V(G)| = n,\ G \text{ is } \mathcal F\text{-free} \,\bigr\}.$$ For a single graph $H$ we abbreviate $\mathrm{ex}(n,\{H\})$ to $\mathrm{ex}(n,H)$. Throughout, asymptotic notation is in $n$ with the forbidden graphs held fixed: $f = O(g)$ and $f \ll g$ both mean $f \le Cg$ for a constant $C$ possibly depending on $\mathcal F$; $f = \Omega(g)$ and $f \gg g$ mean $g = O(f)$; $f = \Theta(g)$ means both.

Two cultures live inside this one definition. If $H$ is not bipartite (a triangle, say), then $\mathrm{ex}(n,H) = \Theta(n^2)$ — a constant fraction of all possible edges survives, and the whole game is the constant. The founding result is worth proving because its two-line counting style returns, industrialized, in §4:

Proposition A (Mantel 1907). $\mathrm{ex}(n, C_3) = \lfloor n^2/4 \rfloor$.

Let $G$ be triangle-free with $m$ edges. For every edge $uv$, the neighborhoods $N(u)$ and $N(v)$ are disjoint (a common neighbor would close a triangle), so $d(u) + d(v) \le n$. Summing over all edges, each vertex $v$ appears once per incident edge, i.e. $d(v)$ times: $$\sum_{uv \in E} \bigl(d(u)+d(v)\bigr) \;=\; \sum_{v} d(v)^2 \;\ge\; \frac{\bigl(\sum_v d(v)\bigr)^2}{n} \;=\; \frac{4m^2}{n},$$ the middle inequality by Cauchy–Schwarz. The left side is at most $nm$, so $4m^2/n \le nm$, i.e. $m \le n^2/4$. For the matching construction, split the vertices into halves of sizes $\lfloor n/2\rfloor, \lceil n/2\rceil$ and take all edges between the halves (the complete bipartite graph $K_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor, \lceil n/2\rceil}$): it is bipartite, hence triangle-free, with $\lfloor n^2/4\rfloor$ edges. ∎

But if $H$ is bipartite, the complete bipartite construction is useless — it contains every bipartite pattern — and the extremal number crashes below $n^2$ to some power $n^{1+\alpha}$, $\alpha \lt 1$. Determining these exponents is the degenerate Turán problem, open for most patterns and the arena of this whole chapter. The one case every treatment starts with, and the one we will lean on, is the four-cycle.

1.1 The four-cycle: upper bound by counting cherries

Proposition B (Kővári–Sós–Turán for $C_4$, 1954). Every $n$-vertex $C_4$-free graph has at most $\tfrac n4\bigl(1 + \sqrt{4n-3}\bigr) = \tfrac12 n^{3/2} + O(n)$ edges.

Call a path $x - v - y$ with endpoints $x \neq y$ a cherry centered at $v$ (Fig. 2). Count cherries two ways. Centered at $v$ there are exactly $\binom{d(v)}{2}$ of them — choose the two endpoints among $v$'s neighbors. And each unordered endpoint pair $\{x,y\}$ supports at most one cherry in a $C_4$-free graph: two cherries $x-v-y$ and $x-v'-y$ with $v \neq v'$ are precisely a four-cycle. Hence $$\sum_{v \in V} \binom{d(v)}{2} \;\le\; \binom n2.$$ The function $t \mapsto \binom t2 = \tfrac{t(t-1)}2$ is convex, so by Jensen's inequality, with $\bar d = \tfrac1n\sum_v d(v) = 2m/n$, $$n \binom{\bar d}{2} \;\le\; \sum_v \binom{d(v)}2 \;\le\; \binom n2, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \bar d^2 - \bar d - (n-1) \le 0.$$ Solving the quadratic for $\bar d$: $\;\bar d \le \tfrac12\bigl(1+\sqrt{4n-3}\bigr)$, and $m = n\bar d/2$ gives the claim. ∎

1.2 The four-cycle: lower bound from projective planes

Is $n^{3/2}$ the truth, or just what this counting sees? Random graphs say nothing useful here: a random graph with $n^{1.01}$ edges already contains four-cycles in abundance. The genuinely extremal networks come from finite geometry, and this is the single most important idea to take into §5. Let $q$ be a prime power (a prime, or a power of one — the sizes for which a finite field $\mathbb F_q$ with $q$ elements exists; we prove everything only for prime $q$, where $\mathbb F_q$ is just arithmetic mod $q$, and that suffices). The projective plane $\mathrm{PG}(2,q)$ has as points the one-dimensional linear subspaces of the vector space $\mathbb F_q^3$, and as lines its two-dimensional linear subspaces, with incidence given by containment. The classical Fano plane is the case $q=2$ (Fig. 3).

Proposition C (incidence graphs beat $\tfrac12\cdot$, up to $\sqrt2$). Let $I_q$ be the bipartite incidence graph of $\mathrm{PG}(2,q)$: one vertex per point, one per line, an edge when the point lies on the line. Then $I_q$ has $n_q = 2(q^2+q+1)$ vertices and $(q+1)(q^2+q+1) = \bigl(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}+o(1)\bigr)\tfrac12 n_q^{3/2}$ edges, and contains no $C_4$. Consequently $\mathrm{ex}(n, C_4) = \Theta(n^{3/2})$.

Counts. $\mathbb F_q^3$ has $q^3 - 1$ nonzero vectors; each 1-dimensional subspace contains $q-1$ of them, so there are $(q^3-1)/(q-1) = q^2+q+1$ points, and by the same count inside a 2-dimensional subspace, each line carries $(q^2-1)/(q-1) = q+1$ points. Dually, the lines through a fixed point $P$ correspond to the 1-dimensional subspaces of the quotient $\mathbb F_q^3/P \cong \mathbb F_q^2$, so there are $(q^2-1)/(q-1) = q+1$ of them, and — multiplying and dividing — there are $q^2+q+1$ lines. The edge count is (points)$\times$(lines per point) $= (q^2+q+1)(q+1)$.

No $C_4$. A four-cycle in a bipartite incidence graph is two distinct points both lying on two distinct lines. But two distinct points $P \neq P'$ span a unique 2-dimensional subspace, so they lie on exactly one common line. No $C_4$ exists.

All $n$. The graphs $I_q$ exist only for special sizes $n_q$, but that is enough: given any $n$, pick the largest prime $q$ with $n_q \le n$. By Bertrand's postulate (there is a prime between $x$ and $2x$; this is the one classical fact we quote in this section) the next prime is at most $2q$, so $n \lt n_{2q} \le 9 n_q$, i.e. $n_q \ge n/9$. Add $n - n_q$ isolated vertices to $I_q$: the graph stays $C_4$-free and keeps $\Theta(n_q^{3/2}) = \Theta(n^{3/2})$ edges. With Proposition B, $\mathrm{ex}(n,C_4) = \Theta(n^{3/2})$. ∎

How close do these geometric networks get to the cherry-counting ceiling? Fig. 4 shows both on a log–log plot: the constructions run parallel to the bound at ratio $\to 1/\sqrt2 \approx 0.707$ (computed ratios $0.725, 0.724, \dots, 0.711$ for $q = 2, \dots, 23$), and a finer classical construction (a polarity quotient of $I_q$, which we will not need) closes even the $\sqrt2$. The exponent $3/2$ is exact.

The moral to carry forward. Upper bounds come from counting configurations and charging them to vertex pairs; matching lower bounds come from incidence geometries over finite fields, whose axioms (“two points, one line”) are short-cycle bans in disguise. Chapter 10's compactness half is precisely this template, one level up: the counting gets three more layers (§4), and the projective plane is replaced by its girth-eight cousin, the generalized quadrangle (§5).
