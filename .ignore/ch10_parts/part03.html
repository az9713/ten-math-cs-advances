3. The forbidden family: subdivisions, two templates, admissible quotients

The compactness counterexample needs a family $\mathcal F$ with two contradictory-sounding properties. Jointly strong: banning all of $\mathcal F$ must force every $n$-vertex graph below $n^{4/3 - 1/48}$ edges. Individually weak: for each single member there must exist graphs with $n^{4/3}$ edges avoiding it. The design that achieves this has three ingredients: a subdivided complete bipartite graph as the basic unit (its copies are what the counting argument of §4 finds in abundance), two templates assembling those units (each engineered to be dodged by a different finite geometry in §5), and closure under quotients — because when two overlapping unit copies are found inside a host graph, their union is not the template itself but the template with some vertices identified, and a usable ban must cover every such collapsed form.

Definition 2.1 (subdivisions). $K_{3,k}$ is the complete bipartite graph with classes of sizes $3$ and $k$. For $k \in \{2,3\}$, form $S_k$ from $K_{3,k}$ by replacing each edge with a two-edge path (one new vertex per edge). Call the three original size-3-class vertices bases, the $k$ original vertices of the other class centers, and the inserted vertices subdivision vertices. So $|V(S_2)| = 3 + 2 + 6 = 11$ and $|V(S_3)| = 3 + 3 + 9 = 15$, and each $S_k$ carries the proper two-coloring (a coloring of vertices by two colors with adjacent vertices differing) in which bases and centers get color two and subdivision vertices color one.

Next, the identification mechanism. We work with properly two-colored graphs, and we distinguish some subgraphs whose integrity an identification must respect.

Definition 2.2 (admissible identifications). Let $T$ be a properly two-colored graph with distinguished subgraphs $T_1, T_2$. An equivalence relation $\approx$ on $V(T)$ is admissible if
  (1) $v \approx w$ implies $v$ and $w$ have the same color; and
  (2) for $i \in \{1,2\}$ and $v, w \in V(T_i)$: $v \approx w$ implies $v = w$.
Write $T/{\approx}$ for the simple graph whose vertices are the equivalence classes, with $[v][w]$ an edge whenever some representatives are adjacent in $T$ (repeated edges suppressed). Condition (1) keeps $T/{\approx}$ properly two-colored — in particular bipartite and loop-free; condition (2) makes each map $T_i \to T/{\approx}$ injective, so each distinguished subgraph survives intact as a subgraph of the quotient.

Now the two templates. Both are drawn in Figs. 7 and 8, matching the chapter's Figure 1.

Definition 2.3 (the first template $J_0$, and the family $\mathcal J$). Take two copies of $S_2$ with base triples $\{x, y, z\}$ and $\{x', y, z\}$: they share the two bases $y, z$ and keep all other vertices distinct (in particular their subdivision vertices and centers are disjoint). Adjoin one new color-one vertex $\lambda$ adjacent to $x$ and $x'$. The result is $J_0$, with $|V(J_0)| = 2\cdot 11 - 2 + 1 = 21$ vertices; its distinguished subgraphs $T_1, T_2$ are the two $S_2$ copies. Let $$\mathcal J := \bigl\{\, \text{uncolored } J_0/{\approx} \;:\; \approx \text{ admissible},\ x \not\approx x' \,\bigr\}.$$ The condition $x \not\approx x'$ keeps all four distinguished bases distinct; $\lambda$ may be identified with anything admissibility permits.
Definition 2.4 (the second template $K_0$, and the family $\mathcal K$). Take disjoint copies $S_3^{(1)}, S_3^{(2)}$ of $S_3$, reversing the two-coloring on the second copy (its bases and centers get color one, its subdivision vertices color two). Add a single edge $d_1 d_2$ between a specified center $d_1$ of the first copy and a specified center $d_2$ of the second — a legal edge precisely because the reversal puts $d_1$ and $d_2$ in opposite colors. The result is $K_0$, with $|V(K_0)| = 15 + 15 = 30$; its distinguished subgraphs are $S_3^{(1)}, S_3^{(2)}$. Let $\mathcal K$ consist of the uncolored graphs $K_0/{\approx}$ for all admissible $\approx$.
Definition 2.5 (the forbidden family). $$\mathcal F \;:=\; \{C_4,\ C_6\} \,\cup\, \mathcal J \,\cup\, \mathcal K. \tag{4}$$

Before using $\mathcal F$ we record its basic hygiene — the properties Theorem 1.1 claims for its members. (This is a “Fact”-grade statement in the paper's economy; we prove it since it costs three lines each.)

Lemma E (housekeeping). $\mathcal F$ is a finite nonempty family; every member is connected, bipartite, and contains a cycle.

Finite: there are finitely many equivalence relations on the vertex sets of $J_0$ (21 vertices) and $K_0$ (30 vertices). Bipartite: $C_4, C_6$ are even cycles; quotient members inherit the proper two-coloring by Definition 2.2(1). Connected: each $S_k$ is connected (it is a subdivided connected graph); $J_0$ is connected since its two copies share $y, z$ and $\lambda$ hangs off $x$; $K_0$ is connected through the bridge $d_1d_2$; and any quotient of a connected graph is connected. Cycles: by Definition 2.2(2), each member of $\mathcal J$ contains an injective image of the $S_2$ copy $T_1$, hence a copy of $S_2$ as a subgraph; likewise each member of $\mathcal K$ contains $S_3 \supseteq S_2$. And $S_2$ contains the eight-cycle $b_1\, s_{11}\, c_1\, s_{21}\, b_2\, s_{22}\, c_2\, s_{12}$ through two bases and both centers. ∎

Why close under quotients at all? Fig. 9 animates the point. Suppose the counting argument of §4 locates, inside a host graph $B$, two copies of $S_2$ sharing bases $y, z$, with distinct third bases $x, x'$ joined through a common neighbor $w$. The union of all that is a graph homomorphic to $J_0$ (send $\lambda \mapsto w$), but the two copies may also have collided in some subdivision vertices or centers — the union is $J_0$ with vertices identified. If only $J_0$ itself were forbidden, such collisions would let the configuration slip through the ban. Closing $\mathcal F$ under all admissible identifications seals every escape route, while conditions (1)–(2) of Definition 2.2 keep the quotients bipartite and keep the two unit copies recognizable — which §5 needs when it argues that the geometries avoid every quotient.
