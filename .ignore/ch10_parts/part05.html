5. The witnesses: generalized quadrangles

Now the lower-bound side of Theorem 1.1: for each individual member $F \in \mathcal F$ we must produce $n$-vertex $F$-free graphs with $\Omega(n^{4/3})$ edges. In §1 the $C_4$-free record holder was the incidence graph of a projective plane — girth $6$, exponent $3/2$. One rung up the same ladder sits the generalized quadrangle: an incidence geometry whose bipartite incidence graph has girth $8$ — automatically $C_4$- and $C_6$-free — with exponent $4/3$. The templates of §3 were engineered so that quadrangles over fields of characteristic $2$ dodge all of $\mathcal J$, while quadrangles over characteristic $3$ dodge all of $\mathcal K$. Since the required constructions are classical (the chapter cites the monograph [PT09] and the survey use in [BHKT18]), the paper keeps this section brief; we expand it, proving everything linear-algebraic and importing exactly two named facts of finite geometry.

5.1 The symplectic quadrangle $W(q)$

Let $q$ be a prime power and $V = \mathbb F_q^4$. Fix the alternating bilinear form $$\langle x, y\rangle \;=\; x_1 y_2 - x_2 y_1 + x_3 y_4 - x_4 y_3 .$$ “Bilinear”: linear in each argument. “Alternating”: $\langle x, x\rangle = 0$ for all $x$ (immediate from the formula), whence $\langle x,y\rangle = -\langle y,x\rangle$ (expand $\langle x+y, x+y\rangle = 0$). “Nondegenerate”: if $\langle x, y\rangle = 0$ for all $y$ then $x = 0$ (test $y$ on the four standard basis vectors: each coordinate of $x$ vanishes). For a subspace $W \le V$ write $W^\perp = \{x : \langle x, w\rangle = 0 \ \forall w \in W\}$; nondegeneracy gives the standard dimension law $\dim W^\perp = 4 - \dim W$ and $(W^\perp)^\perp = W$ (the map $x \mapsto \langle x, \cdot\rangle$ identifies $V$ with its dual, carrying $W^\perp$ to the annihilator of $W$). A subspace $W$ is totally isotropic if $\langle W, W\rangle = 0$, i.e. $W \subseteq W^\perp$; total isotropy forces $\dim W \le 2$ here.

Definition (the symplectic generalized quadrangle and its incidence graph). The geometry $W(q)$ has point class and line class $$\mathcal P = \{P \le V : \dim P = 1\}, \qquad \mathcal M = \{M \le V : \dim M = 2,\ \langle M, M\rangle = 0\},$$ with incidence given by containment. Its incidence graph $I_q := I(W(q))$ is the bipartite graph on $\mathcal P \sqcup \mathcal M$ with $P$ adjacent to $M$ exactly when $P \subseteq M$. Two points are collinear if some line contains both.
Proposition F (the quadrangle axioms, proved). In $W(q)$:
  (a) $|\mathcal P| = |\mathcal M| = (q+1)(q^2+1)$; every line contains $q+1$ points and every point lies on $q+1$ lines.
  (b) Two distinct points lie on at most one common line, and $P, P'$ are collinear $\iff$ $\langle P, P'\rangle = 0$.
  (c) (GQ axiom) If $P \in \mathcal P$ does not lie on $M \in \mathcal M$, then exactly one point of $M$ is collinear with $P$.

(a). $V$ has $q^4 - 1$ nonzero vectors, each 1-space $q - 1$ of them: $|\mathcal P| = (q^4-1)/(q-1) = q^3+q^2+q+1 = (q+1)(q^2+1)$. A line, being a 2-space, contains $(q^2-1)/(q-1) = q+1$ points. Lines through a fixed point $P = \langle p \rangle$: a 2-space $M \ni P$ is totally isotropic iff every generator pair is orthogonal; writing $M = \mathrm{span}(p, v)$, alternation reduces this to $\langle p, v\rangle = 0$, i.e. $v \in P^\perp$. So lines through $P$ correspond to 1-spaces of the quotient $P^\perp/P$, a space of dimension $3 - 1 = 2$ (note $P \subseteq P^\perp$ by alternation): there are $(q^2-1)/(q-1) = q+1$ of them. Finally, counting incident (point, line) pairs two ways: $|\mathcal M| (q+1) = |\mathcal P|(q+1)$, so $|\mathcal M| = |\mathcal P|$.

(b). Two distinct points span a unique 2-space, so at most one line contains both; that 2-space is a line iff it is totally isotropic iff (as just computed) $\langle P, P'\rangle = 0$.

(c). First, the quotient form on $P^\perp / P$, defined by $\langle \bar x, \bar y\rangle := \langle x, y \rangle$, is well defined (shifting $x$ by $P$ changes nothing since $P \perp P^\perp$) and nondegenerate: if $x \in P^\perp$ is orthogonal to all of $P^\perp$, then $x \in (P^\perp)^\perp = P$, so $\bar x = 0$.

Now take $P \not\subseteq M$. Dimensions give $\dim(P^\perp \cap M) \ge 3 + 2 - 4 = 1$. If $\dim(P^\perp \cap M) = 2$, i.e. $M \subseteq P^\perp$: since $P \cap M = 0$, the projection $M \to P^\perp/P$ is injective, so its image is all of the 2-dimensional quotient; but $M$ is totally isotropic, making the (nondegenerate!) quotient form identically zero — contradiction. So $P^\perp \cap M$ is exactly one point, call it $R$. Then $\mathrm{span}(P, R)$ is totally isotropic ($\langle P, R \rangle = 0$ plus alternation), i.e. a line through $P$ and $R$: the point $R$ is collinear with $P$. Conversely any point $R' \subseteq M$ collinear with $P$ satisfies $\langle P, R'\rangle = 0$ by (b), so $R' \subseteq P^\perp \cap M = R$. Exactly one. ∎

Proposition G (girth eight). The incidence graph of any geometry satisfying (a)–(c) with $q \ge 2$ — in particular $I_q$ — is bipartite with girth at least $8$; hence it contains no $C_4$ and no $C_6$.

Cycles in a bipartite incidence graph alternate point, line, point, line, … and have even length. A $C_4$ is two distinct points both on two distinct lines — banned by (b). A $C_6$ is a triangle: distinct points $P_1, P_2, P_3$ and distinct lines $M_3 \ni P_1, P_2$; $M_1 \ni P_2, P_3$; $M_2 \ni P_3, P_1$. Then $P_3$ does not lie on $M_3$ (if it did, $P_2, P_3$ would lie on both $M_3$ and $M_1$, two common lines — banned by (b), as $M_1 \neq M_3$ since $M_1 \ni P_3$). But $P_3$ is collinear with two distinct points of $M_3$, namely $P_1$ and $P_2$ — contradicting the “exactly one” of (c). ∎

Fig. 13 shows the whole of $W(2)$ — the celebrated doily: 15 points, 15 lines, three points per line, three lines per point, girth eight. Our validation script (§12) constructs $I_2$ and $I_3$ from the symplectic definition and machine-checks every claim of Propositions F–G.

5.2 Local patterns, and who dodges what

Because $I_q$ is bipartite with no $C_4$ or $C_6$ (Proposition G), the whole apparatus of §4 applies to it: for either class $S \in \{\mathcal P, \mathcal M\}$ we have the derived graph $R_S$, the sets $L(T)$, and $r(T)$. The relations have clean algebraic meanings. Two distinct points satisfy $P \sim P'$ (a common neighbor in $I_q$ = a common line) iff $\langle P, P'\rangle = 0$, by Proposition F(b). Two distinct lines satisfy $M \sim M'$ (a common point) iff $M \cap M' \neq \{0\}$.

Definition 4.1 (local patterns). A $J$-pattern in a class $S$ consists of $R_S$-independent triples $\{x, y, z\}$ and $\{x', y, z\}$, each with at least two common centers, such that $x \sim x'$. An $S_3$-pattern in $S$ is an $R_S$-independent triple with at least three common centers.

These are precisely the footprints of the two template families:

Lemma H (footprints). If $I_q$ contains a member of $\mathcal J$, then some class of $I_q$ contains a $J$-pattern. If $I_q$ contains a member of $\mathcal K$, then both classes contain $S_3$-patterns.

Let a member of $\mathcal J$ sit inside $I_q$. By Definition 2.2(2) its two distinguished $S_2$-copies appear injectively, giving two $S_2$-copies in $I_q$ with base triples $\{x,y,z\}$, $\{x',y,z\}$, $x \neq x'$ (the quotient condition $x \not\approx x'$), all six original vertices of each copy in one class $S$ (bases and centers are at even distance from each other). By Lemma 3.1(2) (valid in $I_q$!) each base triple is $R_S$-independent with at least two common centers. And the image of $\lambda$ is a common neighbor of $x$ and $x'$ in $I_q$, so $x \sim x'$: a $J$-pattern in $S$. For $\mathcal K$: the two $S_3$-copies appear injectively; the bridge $d_1 d_2$ puts the originals of the first copy in one class and (reversed coloring) those of the second in the other class; Lemma 3.1(2) turns each into an $S_3$-pattern — one per class. ∎

So to certify $\mathcal J$-freeness it suffices to rule $J$-patterns out of both classes, and for $\mathcal K$-freeness to rule $S_3$-patterns out of one. Here are the two classical facts we import; both are verified by machine for $q \in \{2,3\}$ in §12.

Import 1 (self-duality in characteristic 2) [PT09, §3.2.1]. For $q$ even, $W(q)$ is self-dual: there is an incidence-preserving bijection swapping $\mathcal P$ and $\mathcal M$. (Hence any statement about the point class holds verbatim for the line class.)
Import 2 (triads in odd characteristic) [PT09, §1.3.6(iii) and §3.3.1; BHKT18, Prop. 4.5]. For $q$ odd, identify $\mathcal M$ with the point class of the dual quadrangle, which is isomorphic to the parabolic quadrangle $Q(4,q)$. Every triad of $Q(4,q)$ — in our language, every $R_{\mathcal M}$-independent triple — has exactly $0$ or $2$ common centers. In particular $r(T) \le 2$ for every independent triple in $\mathcal M$: no $S_3$-pattern.
Proposition 4.2 (quadrangle witnesses). For even $q$, $I_q$ is $\mathcal J$-free; for odd $q$, $I_q$ is $\mathcal K$-free. In either case it contains no $C_4$ or $C_6$.

The heart is that the point class of $W(q)$, for every $q$, contains no $J$-pattern; this we prove outright (Fig. 14). Let $y, z \in \mathcal P$ be unrelated — noncollinear, i.e. $\langle y, z\rangle \neq 0$ — and put $U = y + z$ (the 2-space they span). Since $y \not\perp z$, the form restricted to $U$ is nondegenerate (a vector $ay + bz$ orthogonal to both $y$ and $z$ forces $b\langle z,y\rangle = 0 = a\langle y,z\rangle$, so $a = b = 0$), and consequently $V = U \oplus U^\perp$ with $U^\perp$ likewise 2-dimensional and nondegenerate (any radical vector of $U^\perp$ would be orthogonal to $U^\perp$ and to $U$, hence to $V$).

The common centers of $y, z$ are exactly the points of $U^\perp$: $c \sim y$ and $c \sim z$ means $c \perp y$, $c \perp z$ (Proposition F(b)), i.e. $c \subseteq U^\perp$; conversely each point of $U^\perp$ is orthogonal to both and distinct from both (as $U \cap U^\perp = 0$).

Any candidate third base lies inside $U$: if $\{x, y, z\}$ is independent with $r(\{x,y,z\}) \ge 2$, then $x$ is orthogonal to two distinct common centers $c_1, c_2 \subseteq U^\perp$; two distinct 1-spaces span the 2-space $U^\perp$, so $x \perp U^\perp$, i.e. $x \subseteq (U^\perp)^\perp = U$.

Two candidates are never related: distinct points $x, x' \subseteq U$ with $\langle x, x'\rangle = 0$ would span $U$ and (with alternation) make the form vanish identically on $U$ — contradicting nondegeneracy of $U$. So $x \not\sim x'$, and $\mathcal P$ has no $J$-pattern.

If $q$ is even, Import 1 transfers the conclusion to $\mathcal M$: no $J$-pattern in either class, so by Lemma H, $I_q$ is $\mathcal J$-free. If $q$ is odd, Import 2 says $\mathcal M$ has no $S_3$-pattern, so by Lemma H, $I_q$ is $\mathcal K$-free. The $C_4$/$C_6$ statement is Proposition G. ∎

5.3 The individual lower bounds

Write, as in the paper, $$n_q = 2(q+1)(q^2+1), \qquad e_q = (q+1)^2(q^2+1) \;\ge\; 2^{-4/3}\, n_q^{4/3}, \tag{8}$$ where $e_q = e(I_q)$ counts incidences ($|\mathcal P|$ points times $q+1$ lines each) and the inequality reduces, after raising to the third power and clearing $2^4$, to $(q+1)^2 \ge q^2 + 1$ — true. Note the calibration: girth 8 with $\Theta(q^3)$ vertices and $\Theta(q^4)$ edges is exactly “exponent $4/3$”.

Proposition 4.3 (individual lower bounds). For every $F \in \mathcal F$: $\;\mathrm{ex}(n, F) = \Omega(n^{4/3})$.

Given $F$, choose the field family: if $F \in \{C_4, C_6\} \cup \mathcal J$, use $q = 2^j$; if $F \in \mathcal K$, use $q = 3^j$. Proposition 4.2 makes every such $I_q$ an $F$-free graph. The admissible sizes are dense enough on the doubling scale: for $t \in \{2,3\}$, $$n_{tq} = 2(tq+1)(t^2q^2+1) \le 2 \cdot t(q+1)\cdot t^2(q^2+1) = t^3 n_q \le 27\, n_q.$$ Given large $n$, take the largest admissible $q$ with $n_q \le n$; then $n \lt n_{tq} \le 27 n_q$, so $n_q \gt n/27$. Pad $I_q$ with $n - n_q$ isolated vertices: since $F$ is connected and has an edge, no copy of $F$ can use an isolated vertex, so the padded graph is still $F$-free, and by (8) $$\mathrm{ex}(n, F) \;\ge\; e_q \;\ge\; 2^{-4/3} n_q^{4/3} \;\ge\; 2^{-4/3}\, (n/27)^{4/3} \;\gg\; n^{4/3}.$$ ∎

6. Theorem 1.1 assembled — compactness fails

Proof of Theorem 1.1. The family $\mathcal F$ of (4) is finite and nonempty, with every member connected, bipartite, and containing a cycle (Lemma E). Proposition 3.4 gives $\mathrm{ex}(n, \mathcal F) = O(n^{21/16}) = O(n^{4/3 - 1/48})$; Proposition 4.3 gives $\mathrm{ex}(n, F) = \Omega(n^{4/3})$ for every member. That is exactly (2), and it is incompatible with (1) for any $F \in \mathcal F$ and any constant $C$: the ratio $\mathrm{ex}(n,F)/\mathrm{ex}(n,\mathcal F) \gg n^{1/48} \to \infty$. ∎

Step back and look at the mechanism. Each member of $\mathcal F$ is dodged by an infinite family of quadrangle graphs — but which family depends on the arithmetic of the member: the $\mathcal J$-templates are killed by the self-dual symmetry available only in characteristic $2$, the $\mathcal K$-templates by the triad parity available only in odd characteristic. The family as a whole straddles the two characteristics, and no single host graph gets to choose both. The separation is genuine but glacial — $n^{1/48}$ — as Fig. 15 shows.
