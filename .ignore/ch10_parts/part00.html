0. Motivation — banning patterns in a network

Take a network: $n$ people, some pairs of them friends. Now impose a local rule — say, no two people may have two friends in common. In graph language, the friendship graph must not contain a four-cycle $C_4$ (two vertices joined by two disjoint two-step paths, as in Fig. 1). How many friendships can the network still support? This is the prototype question of extremal graph theory: given a forbidden pattern $H$, how many edges can an $n$-vertex graph have before a copy of $H$ is unavoidable? The answer for $C_4$ is a famous one — about $\tfrac12 n^{3/2}$ edges, far fewer than the $\binom n2 \approx n^2/2$ possible — and we will prove it from scratch in §1, because the machinery of that proof (counting paths of length two, and building record-breaking networks out of finite geometries) is exactly the machinery this chapter pushes much further.

This chapter is about two structural beliefs that guided the field for decades — both plausible, both wrong. Each is a statement about how the answer $\mathrm{ex}(n,\cdot)$ behaves as the forbidden pattern varies, and each is killed in this chapter by an explicit counterexample.

  • Belief one (compactness, Erdős–Simonovits 1982). Banning a finite list $\mathcal F$ of patterns should be essentially no stronger than banning the single most restrictive pattern in the list: some member $F \in \mathcal F$ should satisfy $\mathrm{ex}(n,\mathcal F) \ge c\,\mathrm{ex}(n,F)$. Intuitively, a graph dodging the whole list should be comparable to the best graph dodging its hardest member. The chapter constructs a finite family of connected bipartite graphs, each containing a cycle, whose joint ban costs a polynomial factor $n^{1/48}$ more than any individual ban: $\mathrm{ex}(n,\mathcal F) = O(n^{4/3 - 1/48})$ while every single member has $\mathrm{ex}(n,F) = \Omega(n^{4/3})$.
  • Belief two (degeneracy, Erdős 1967). A pattern that is sparse everywhere should be easy to force. A graph $H$ is $r$-degenerate if every nonempty subgraph of it has a vertex of degree at most $r$ — it can be assembled by adding vertices one at a time, each attached by at most $r$ edges. Erdős conjectured that every bipartite $r$-degenerate $H$ has $\mathrm{ex}(n,H) = O(n^{2-1/r})$; for $r=2$ that means $O(n^{3/2})$, the same order as the humble $C_4$. The chapter constructs a connected bipartite 2-degenerate graph $H$ with $\mathrm{ex}(n,H) \ge c\,n^{3/2+\varepsilon}$ — a sparse-everywhere pattern that is polynomially harder to force than its density suggests.

What makes the chapter a treat for an outsider is that the two counterexamples showcase the field's two great engines, one apiece. The compactness counterexample runs on finite incidence geometry: the record-holding networks are incidence graphs of generalized quadrangles, point–line geometries over finite fields whose defining axiom is precisely the absence of short cycles, and the two forbidden templates are rigged so that geometries over fields of different characteristic dodge different halves of the list — no single graph dodges it all. The degeneracy counterexample runs on the probabilistic and entropy method: the record network is a random induced subgraph of a Hamming-distance graph on binary strings, and the proof that the sparse pattern $H$ cannot appear is an accounting argument in which every layer of an embedded copy would pump a bounded “entropy potential” up by a fixed amount — impossible after enough layers. Remarkably, the two engines meet nowhere: the chapter is two independent short stories, and we tell them in order.

The plan:

  • §1 builds $\mathrm{ex}(n,H)$ from zero and proves the classical $C_4$ result in both directions — the warm-up whose DNA is everywhere below.
  • §2 states both conjectures precisely (including the folklore example that forced a correction to the first), and states the chapter's two theorems.
  • §3–§6 prove Theorem 1.1: the forbidden family (§3), the counting upper bound (§4), the generalized-quadrangle lower bounds (§5), and the assembly (§6).
  • §7–§11 prove Theorem 1.2: the layered graph (§7), the entropy inequality (§8), the random host (§9), the exclusion argument (§10), and the edge count with the parameter window (§11).
  • §12 validates: we rebuild the witnesses by computer for small field sizes and check every checkable claim; §13 is the honest scorecard.
