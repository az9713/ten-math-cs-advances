8. Local surrogates and the central lemma (Lemma 3.3)

The ideal experiment fails to be a strategy on three counts: the questions under $\mathcal{Q}$ may be biased by the conditioning, the history is not supplied to anyone, and the state depends on both players' questions. This section builds the best locally available substitutes for the history and the state, and states the lemma asserting they are good.

8.1 Locally generated histories

Consider the actual experiment the players can run. The referee draws fresh questions $(x,y)\sim\mu$; shared randomness selects a uniform live coordinate $i\in[n]\setminus D$. Alice knows $x$ but not $y$, so the best-informed distribution over histories she can target is the posterior conditioned on her data: $$r_A \sim \mathcal{Q}(R \mid i,\ X_i = x),$$ and symmetrically Bob targets $r_B\sim\mathcal{Q}(R\mid i, Y_i = y)$. These are exactly the sampling tasks Lemma 3.4 couples. Record the two resulting tuple distributions: for Alice's version, $$\mathcal{J}_A(i, r, x, y) \;=\; \frac1m\,\mu(x,y)\;\mathcal{Q}(r\mid i, X_i = x), \qquad \mathcal{J}_B(i, r, x, y) \;=\; \frac1m\,\mu(x,y)\;\mathcal{Q}(r\mid i, Y_i = y). \tag{6}$$ Both retain the genuine question distribution $\mu$ — the bias sits entirely in the history coordinate. If $\mathcal{J}_A$ and $\mathcal{J}_B$ were both equal to $\mathcal{Q}$, histories would be perfectly sampleable; the lemma will bound the actual distances (Fig. 20).

8.2 Locally describable states

Even on the event $r_A = r_B = r$, the target state $\Psi_{r,x,y}$ still depends on both questions. The remedy: each player replaces the other player's effect by its average over the other question, weighted by what their own question says about it: $$\bar H_{r,y} \;=\; \sum_{x'}\mu(x'\mid y)\,H_{r,x'}, \qquad \bar K_{r,x} \;=\; \sum_{y'}\mu(y'\mid x)\,K_{r,y'} .$$ Read the subscripts carefully: $\bar H_{r,y}$ is Bob's estimate of Alice's effect (he knows $y$), and $\bar K_{r,x}$ is Alice's estimate of Bob's effect. Collect the exact branch and its two one-sided surrogates: $$\phi_{r,x,y} = \big(\Gamma(H_{r,x})\otimes\Gamma(K_{r,y})\big)|\psi\rangle, \qquad \phi^A_{r,x} = \big(\Gamma(H_{r,x})\otimes\Gamma(\bar K_{r,x})\big)|\psi\rangle, \qquad \phi^B_{r,y} = \big(\Gamma(\bar H_{r,y})\otimes\Gamma(K_{r,y})\big)|\psi\rangle, \tag{7}$$ and the normalized states $$\Psi^A_{r,x} = \frac{\phi^A_{r,x}}{\|\phi^A_{r,x}\|}, \qquad \Psi^B_{r,y} = \frac{\phi^B_{r,y}}{\|\phi^B_{r,y}\|}.$$ Alice can write down a complete classical description of the bipartite vector $\Psi^A_{r,x}$ from $(r,x)$ alone — it involves only $H_{r,x}$, $\bar K_{r,x}$, $\Gamma$, $\psi$, all computable from the publicly known strategy plus her data; likewise Bob with $\Psi^B_{r,y}$. (Describing a state classically is not the same as holding it; the imported theorem in §9 is what converts matching descriptions into a physical state.)

These normalized surrogates are well defined wherever the posterior lives: if $\mathcal{Q}(i,r,x,y) \gt 0$ then $\mu(x,y) \gt 0$ and $p_r(x,y) \gt 0$, and the operator inequalities $\bar H_{r,y} \succeq \mu(x\mid y)\,H_{r,x}$ and $\bar K_{r,x} \succeq \mu(y\mid x)\,K_{r,y}$ (drop all other terms from the defining sums) give, via (5) and conjugation-monotonicity of the order (§1.1), $$\|\phi^A_{r,x}\|^2 = \langle\psi| H_{r,x}\otimes \bar K_{r,x}|\psi\rangle \;\ge\; \mu(y\mid x)\,p_r(x,y) \gt 0, \qquad \|\phi^B_{r,y}\|^2 \;\ge\; \mu(x\mid y)\,p_r(x,y) \gt 0 .$$

8.3 The central lemma

The attempt to mimic the ideal experiment thus needs two guarantees: the locally generated histories must (approximately) agree with each other and with $\mathcal{Q}$, and the locally described states must (approximately) match the ideal one. The following lemma — the chapter's new technical contribution — provides exactly these, with bounds depending only on $\eta$. Recall $\eta = \big(\log(1/p) + |D|\log(|A||B|)\big)/m$ from (1), and that $(i,r,x,y)\sim\mathcal{Q}$ denotes a tuple from the posterior.

Lemma 3.3 (postselected state alignment and history sampleability). For every finite repeated-game strategy and every $D\subsetneq[n]$ with $p = \mathcal{P}(W_D) \gt 0$, with the histories, branches, surrogates, and distributions defined above: $$\mathbb{E}_{(i,r,x,y)\sim\mathcal{Q}}\,\big\|\Psi_{r,x,y} - \Psi^A_{r,x}\big\|^2 \;\le\; 8\eta, \qquad \mathbb{E}_{(i,r,x,y)\sim\mathcal{Q}}\,\big\|\Psi_{r,x,y} - \Psi^B_{r,y}\big\|^2 \;\le\; 8\eta. \tag{8}$$ Moreover, with $$\kappa = \sqrt{\tfrac32\,\eta},$$ one has $d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{J}_A) \le \kappa$ and $d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{J}_B) \le \kappa$. There exists finite shared randomness such that, on $(x,y)\sim\mu$, Alice and Bob sample a common uniform $i\in[n]\setminus D$ and histories $r_A, r_B$ with respective tuple distributions $\mathcal{J}_A, \mathcal{J}_B$, satisfying $$\mathbb{P}(r_A \ne r_B) \;\le\; 4\kappa. \tag{9}$$

Read the two halves correctly. The state inequalities (8) are averages under the posterior: they say that when the repeated experiment is conditioned on $W_D$, the ideal state is typically close to each player's description — not that every branch is good. (Rare terrible branches are permitted and later absorbed into the average.) The total-variation half compares the ideal posterior with what each player can actually generate from their own question; feeding the family $\{\mathcal{Q}(R\mid i, X_i=x),\ \mathcal{Q}(R\mid i, Y_i=y)\}_{i,x,y}$ into the exact correlated-sampling Lemma 3.4 and averaging (10) over $(x,y)\sim\mu$ gives $$\mathbb{P}(r_A\ne r_B) \;\le\; 2\,d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathcal{J}_A,\mathcal{J}_B) \;\le\; 2\big(d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathcal{J}_A,\mathcal{Q}) + d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{J}_B)\big) \;\le\; 4\kappa,$$ which is precisely how (9) follows from the total-variation bounds — so the genuinely new content is (8) and the two $d_{\mathrm{TV}}\le\kappa$ estimates. Their proof occupies §11–§14.

Where the magic sits. Both statements of Lemma 3.3 are "obvious" analogues of classical facts from Holenstein's proof — with one exception: the bounds contain no factor $1/p$ or $1/\mu_{\min}$. Every earlier quantum analogue degraded as the conditioning event got rarer or as the question distribution got more skewed. Here $p$ enters only through $\log(1/p)$ inside $\eta$ — the information cost — never as an inverse power. That single feature is what turns polynomial into exponential decay, and it is bought by the unnormalized-budget bookkeeping of §13.
