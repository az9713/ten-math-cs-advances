7. The ideal conditioned experiment (Lemma 3.2)

Lemma 3.1 hands us a conditioned experiment in which the average live coordinate is won with probability $q \ge 1-\delta$. This section extracts from it a single-game scenario: one live coordinate $i$, playing the role of the fresh game, embedded in a partially revealed transcript of the other $n-1$ coordinates. The extraction is "ideal" — it describes states and measurements that exist mathematically but that the players cannot yet obtain; §8–§9 will close that gap.

7.1 The reveal pattern and the history

To help construct their eventual single-game strategy, the players use finite-valued public randomness $\lambda \in \Lambda$, independent of all game questions, which samples: a uniformly random live coordinate $i \in [n]\setminus D$, and revealed-coordinate sets $C_X, C_Y \subseteq [n]$ (for Alice's and Bob's questions respectively) satisfying $$D \;\subseteq\; C_X \cap C_Y, \qquad C_X \cup C_Y \;=\; [n]\setminus\{i\}. \tag{2}$$ The exact distribution of $\lambda$ — a symmetric random-reveal scheme with random orders and cuts — is constructed in §11, where its finer properties are needed; this section uses only (2). The first condition puts both questions of every core coordinate on the record, so core success can be checked from the record; the second withholds both live questions $X_i, Y_i$ while revealing at least one question at every other coordinate (Fig. 16).

Now define the revealed-question history and the full history: $$T = (\lambda,\, X_{C_X},\, Y_{C_Y}), \qquad Z \in A^D\times B^D, \qquad R = (T, Z),$$ where $Z = (A_D, B_D)$ is the pair of answer words the strategy produced on the core. Because $T$ contains $\lambda$, conditioning on $T = t$ automatically fixes $i$, $C_X$, $C_Y$. Continue to write $\mathcal{P}$ for the distribution on $\Lambda\times X^n\times Y^n\times A^n\times B^n$ (public randomness adjoined independently), and let $$\mathcal{Q} = \mathcal{P}(\,\cdot\mid W_D)$$ be the posterior. Since $t$ reveals the core questions and $z$ the core answers, the pair $(t,z)$ determines whether $W_D$ occurred — we write $\mathbf{1}_{W_D}(t,z)$ for this indicator. The posterior tuple distribution is $$\mathcal{Q}(i, r, x, y) \;=\; \mathcal{P}\big(R = r,\; X_i = x,\; Y_i = y \,\big\vert\, W_D\big),$$ where $(i,r,x,y)$ are realized values (the realized $r$ determines its live coordinate $i$; we display $i$ anyway to name the extracted coordinate). Two warnings, verbatim from the chapter because they prevent real misreadings: the history $R$ is an analytical variable — nobody hands it to the players; and under $\mathcal{Q}$ the four tuple entries are not independent.

7.2 Why the unrevealed questions factorize

Here is the payoff of the coverage condition. Fix $T = t$ and the live questions $X_i = x$, $Y_i = y$. Which questions are still random? Alice's on $[n]\setminus(C_X\cup\{i\})$ and Bob's on $[n]\setminus(C_Y\cup\{i\})$ — and by (2) these two index sets are disjoint (Fig. 17). So no jointly sampled pair $(X_j, Y_j)\sim\mu$ remains: at every coordinate carrying an unrevealed Alice question, Bob's question is on the record, and vice versa.

Prior factorization. For all $t, x, y$ and all question words $x^n, y^n$ consistent with them, $$\begin{aligned} \mathcal{P}\big(X^n = x^n,\, Y^n = y^n \mid{}& T = t,\, X_i = x,\, Y_i = y\big)\\[2pt] &= \mathcal{P}\big(X^n = x^n \mid T = t,\, X_i = x\big)\; \mathcal{P}\big(Y^n = y^n \mid T = t,\, Y_i = y\big). \end{aligned} \tag{3}$$
Under $\mathcal{P}$, the coordinates' question pairs are independent, and $\lambda$ is independent of all of them. Condition on $(t, x, y)$: coordinates in $C_X\cap C_Y$ and $i$ are fully determined; a coordinate $j \in C_X\setminus C_Y$ has $X_j$ recorded and $Y_j$ still random with law $\mu(\cdot\mid X_j)$; a coordinate $j\in C_Y\setminus C_X$ has $X_j$ random with law $\mu(\cdot\mid Y_j)$. Distinct coordinates stay independent. The conditional law of $(X^n, Y^n)$ is therefore the product over $j \in C_Y\setminus C_X$ of $\mu(x_j\mid y_j)$ times the product over $j\in C_X\setminus C_Y$ of $\mu(y_j\mid x_j)$ — the first group involves only still-random Alice questions, the second only Bob questions, and each factor on the right of (3) reproduces exactly its own group. (The same computation shows each side conditions correctly: e.g. $\mathcal{P}(X^n = x^n\mid T=t, X_i=x)$ integrates the $\mu(y_j|x_j)$ factors out.) ∎

7.3 Local effects and the ideal conditioned state

Fix a realized history $r = (t, z)$ with $z = (a_D, b_D)$. Two families of operators summarize what producing those core answers did to the state. First, the marginal repeated-game effects: summing Alice's POVM over all answer words agreeing with $a_D$ on $D$, $$E^{a_D}_{x^n} \;=\; \sum_{\tilde a^n \in A^n:\ \tilde a_D = a_D} E^{\tilde a^n}_{x^n}, \qquad F^{b_D}_{y^n} \;=\; \sum_{\tilde b^n \in B^n:\ \tilde b_D = b_D} F^{\tilde b^n}_{y^n},$$ the effects of "Alice's core answers came out $a_D$, whatever she answered elsewhere", and Bob's analogue. Each is a positive contraction (a subfamily of a POVM summed). Second, average over each player's own unrevealed questions to get the effective local effects $$H_{r,x} \;=\; \mathbb{E}\big[E^{a_D}_{X^n}\;\big\vert\; T = t,\, X_i = x\big], \qquad K_{r,y} \;=\; \mathbb{E}\big[F^{b_D}_{Y^n}\;\big\vert\; T = t,\, Y_i = y\big],$$ positive contractions on $\mathcal{H}_A$, $\mathcal{H}_B$ respectively (averages of positive contractions, by §4.4). These two operators are the protagonists of the entire second half of the proof.

Exact branch probability. For every $(t,z,x,y)$ with positive conditioning probability, $$p_r(x,y) \;:=\; \mathcal{P}\big(Z = z \mid T = t,\, X_i = x,\, Y_i = y\big) \;=\; \langle\psi|\, H_{r,x}\otimes K_{r,y}\,|\psi\rangle. \tag{4}$$
Given the questions, the Born rule gives $\mathcal{P}(Z = z \mid X^n, Y^n) = \langle\psi| E^{a_D}_{X^n}\otimes F^{b_D}_{Y^n}|\psi\rangle$ (sum the joint POVM over all answers extending $z$). Average over the conditional law of $(X^n, Y^n)$ given $(t,x,y)$: by the factorization (3) this law is a product of an Alice part and a Bob part, so the expectation of the tensor product splits into the tensor product of expectations: $$\mathbb{E}\big[\langle\psi| E^{a_D}_{X^n}\otimes F^{b_D}_{Y^n}|\psi\rangle\big] = \langle\psi|\;\mathbb{E}[E^{a_D}_{X^n}]\otimes \mathbb{E}[F^{b_D}_{Y^n}]\;|\psi\rangle = \langle\psi|\, H_{r,x}\otimes K_{r,y}\,|\psi\rangle.$$ ∎

Now the state. A POVM effect fixes an outcome's probability but not the post-measurement state: many "branch operators" are compatible with the same effect. To fix the branch, choose a common purification: an assignment $F \mapsto \Gamma(F)$, mapping each positive contraction to an operator $$\Gamma(F): \mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{A} \qquad\text{satisfying}\qquad \Gamma(F)^\dagger\,\Gamma(F) = F, \tag{5}$$ where $\mathcal{A}$ is a fixed finite auxiliary space (Fig. 18). Property (5) is exactly "the branch reproduces the Born probability": $\|\Gamma(F)\,\psi\|^2 = \langle\psi|F|\psi\rangle$ for every state. The obvious choice $\Gamma(F) = F^{1/2}$ (with $\mathcal{A} = \mathbb{C}$) satisfies (5); the proof of the central lemma will instead use the resolvent purification of §12, which satisfies (5) and an entropy-control estimate that $F^{1/2}$ lacks. Everything in this section and the next two works for any fixed $\Gamma$ satisfying (5).

The ideal conditioned branch and its normalized state are $$\phi_{r,x,y} \;=\; \big(\Gamma(H_{r,x})\otimes\Gamma(K_{r,y})\big)\,|\psi\rangle, \qquad \Psi_{r,x,y} \;=\; \frac{\phi_{r,x,y}}{\|\phi_{r,x,y}\|}.$$ By (5) and (4), $\|\phi_{r,x,y}\|^2 = p_r(x,y)$, so $\Psi_{r,x,y}$ is well defined on every branch of positive posterior probability. Read it as: the joint state of the players, given that the history came out $r$ and the live questions are $(x,y)$. It is ideal in a precise sense — its very definition uses data ($r$, and both $x$ and $y$) that no single player possesses.

7.4 The ideal state exactly simulates the live coordinate

Two separate questions arise. (Q1) If someone handed Alice and Bob the state $\Psi_{r,x,y}$, could they reproduce the original strategy's conditional behavior at coordinate $i$? (Q2) Can they approximately obtain it from their separate data? This subsection answers (Q1) exactly; (Q2) is the central lemma. For (Q1) we need to split each player's effect by their live answer: define the answer-refinement operators $$H^a_{r,x} = \mathbb{E}\big[E^{\,a_D,\,a_i = a}_{X^n}\;\big\vert\; T=t, X_i=x\big], \qquad K^b_{r,y} = \mathbb{E}\big[F^{\,b_D,\,b_i = b}_{Y^n}\;\big\vert\; T=t, Y_i=y\big],$$ where $E^{a_D, a_i=a}_{x^n}$ sums the POVM over answer words agreeing with $a_D$ on $D$ and equal to $a$ at $i$. These are positive contractions with $$\sum_{a\in A} H^a_{r,x} = H_{r,x}, \qquad \sum_{b\in B} K^b_{r,y} = K_{r,y}.$$ The next lemma turns any such decomposition of an effect into a genuine measurement on the purified space — taking care of the possibility that $H_{r,x}$ is singular.

Lemma A.1 (singular-safe local POVM refinement). Let $0 \preceq F \preceq I$ and let $\{F^a : a\in A\}$ be positive operators with $\sum_a F^a = F$. Then there is a POVM $\{M^a_F : a\in A\}$ on $\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{A}$ such that $$\Gamma(F)^\dagger\, M^a_F\,\Gamma(F) \;=\; F^a \qquad (a\in A). \tag{36}$$

Let $F^{[-1/2]}$ be the pseudo-inverse square root (§1.1) and set $U_F = \Gamma(F)\,F^{[-1/2]}$. By (5), $$U_F^\dagger U_F = F^{[-1/2]}\,F\,F^{[-1/2]} = \Pi,$$ the orthogonal projection onto $\operatorname{supp} F$ — so $U_F$ is an isometry on $\operatorname{supp}F$, and $U_F U_F^\dagger$ is the projection onto its image. Choose a default answer $a_0\in A$ and set $$M^a_F \;=\; U_F\, F^{[-1/2]}F^a F^{[-1/2]}\, U_F^\dagger \;+\; \mathbf{1}_{\{a = a_0\}}\,\big(I - U_F U_F^\dagger\big).$$ Positivity: each summand is PSD ($F^a \succeq 0$ is conjugated; the second term is a projection). Sum to identity: since $0 \preceq F^a \preceq F$, each $F^a$ is supported inside $\operatorname{supp}F$, so $\sum_a F^{[-1/2]}F^aF^{[-1/2]} = F^{[-1/2]}FF^{[-1/2]} = \Pi$, whence $\sum_a M^a_F = U_F\,\Pi\,U_F^\dagger + I - U_FU_F^\dagger = U_FU_F^\dagger + I - U_FU_F^\dagger = I$.

Identity (36): first, $U_F^\dagger\,\Gamma(F) = F^{[-1/2]}\,\Gamma(F)^\dagger\Gamma(F) = F^{[-1/2]}F = F^{1/2}$; and $\Gamma(F)$ has image inside $\operatorname{im}U_F$ (as $\Gamma(F) = U_F F^{1/2}$ on $\operatorname{supp}F$, and $\Gamma(F)$ kills $\ker F$ by (5): $\|\Gamma(F)v\|^2 = \langle v, Fv\rangle = 0$ there), so the $(I - U_FU_F^\dagger)$ term contributes $0$. Hence $$\Gamma(F)^\dagger M^a_F\,\Gamma(F) = F^{1/2}\big(F^{[-1/2]}F^aF^{[-1/2]}\big)F^{1/2} = \Pi\,F^a\,\Pi = F^a,$$ the last step because $F^a$ is supported in $\operatorname{supp}F$. All statements hold verbatim when $F$ has nontrivial kernel. (Machine check c21 builds these operators for a singular $3\times3$ example and verifies PSD, sum $= I$, and (36).) ∎

Lemma 3.2 (the ideal state exactly simulates the remaining coordinate). Fix $(i,r,x,y)$ with $\mathcal{Q}(i,r,x,y) \gt 0$. If Alice and Bob share $\Psi_{r,x,y}$, there are local POVMs $\{\widetilde M^a_{r,x}\}_{a\in A}$ and $\{\widetilde N^b_{r,y}\}_{b\in B}$ — computable from $(r,x)$ and $(r,y)$ respectively — with $$\langle\Psi_{r,x,y}|\; \widetilde M^a_{r,x}\otimes \widetilde N^b_{r,y}\;|\Psi_{r,x,y}\rangle \;=\; \mathcal{Q}\big(A_i = a,\, B_i = b \,\big\vert\, R = r,\, X_i = x,\, Y_i = y\big).$$
Apply Lemma A.1 to the decomposition $\{H^a_{r,x}\}$ of $H_{r,x}$ and to $\{K^b_{r,y}\}$ of $K_{r,y}$, obtaining POVMs $\widetilde M^a_{r,x}$ on $\mathcal{H}_A\otimes\mathcal{A}$ and $\widetilde N^b_{r,y}$ on $\mathcal{H}_B\otimes\mathcal{A}$. Using $\Psi = \phi/\|\phi\|$, $\phi = (\Gamma(H_{r,x})\otimes\Gamma(K_{r,y}))\psi$, $\|\phi\|^2 = p_r(x,y)$, and (36) on each side, $$\langle\Psi|\widetilde M^a\otimes\widetilde N^b|\Psi\rangle = \frac{\langle\psi|\,\big(\Gamma(H)^\dagger \widetilde M^a \Gamma(H)\big)\otimes\big(\Gamma(K)^\dagger \widetilde N^b \Gamma(K)\big)\,|\psi\rangle}{p_r(x,y)} = \frac{\langle\psi|\, H^a_{r,x}\otimes K^b_{r,y}\,|\psi\rangle}{p_r(x,y)}.$$ It remains to recognize numerator and denominator. Exactly as in the proof of (4) — the factorization (3) splitting the expectation — the numerator equals $\mathcal{P}(Z = z,\, A_i = a,\, B_i = b \mid T=t, X_i=x, Y_i=y)$, and the denominator is $\mathcal{P}(Z = z\mid T=t,X_i=x,Y_i=y)$. Their ratio is $\mathcal{P}(A_i=a, B_i=b\mid R=r, X_i=x, Y_i=y)$; and conditioning further on $W_D$ changes nothing, because $W_D$ is determined by $(t,z)$, which is already fixed. That conditional is the $\mathcal{Q}$-conditional claimed. (Machine checks c31, c32 verify both (4) and this identity on all $64$ branches of a genuinely correlated entangled strategy for $\mathrm{CHSH}^{\otimes 2}$.) ∎

7.5 Why the ideal experiment wins with probability $q$

Suppose — still ideally — that for each $(i,r,x,y)$ the players hold $\Psi_{r,x,y}$ and measure as in Lemma 3.2. Their answers then reproduce the original strategy's conditional answer distribution at coordinate $i$, so they win the live coordinate with probability $\mathcal{Q}(W_i \mid R=r, X_i=x, Y_i=y)$. Average over the posterior: since $i$ is uniform on $[n]\setminus D$ and independent of the game variables, the law of total expectation gives $$\mathbb{E}_{(i,r,x,y)\sim\mathcal{Q}}\;\mathcal{Q}\big(W_i\mid R=r, X_i=x, Y_i=y\big) \;=\; \frac1m \sum_{j\in[n]\setminus D} \mathcal{P}(W_j\mid W_D) \;=\; q. $$ (Machine check c33 confirms the identity on the $\mathrm{CHSH}^{\otimes2}$ toy.) This is an analytical success probability: it assumes the players can sample $\mathcal{Q}$, obtain the history, and hold the two-question state $\Psi_{r,x,y}$ — three assumptions that are all false as stated. Quantifying how nearly they can be made true is the content of the central lemma, next.
