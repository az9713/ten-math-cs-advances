Repeating a quantum game finally works: exponential parallel repetition for all entangled games

A rigorous, self-contained walkthrough of Chapter 6 of Ten Advances in Mathematics and Theoretical Computer Science (PDF pages 154–182): for every finite two-player one-round game whose entangled value is $1-\varepsilon \lt 1$, the value of $n$ parallel copies decays exponentially in $n$ — resolving a question open since 2004. Written for a reader with a PhD in some mathematical field, but not necessarily quantum information or complexity theory. Every symbol is defined before use; everything provable at the graduate-core level is proved; exactly one external theorem is imported, with a precise statement.

0. Motivation — interrogation in two rooms

Two suspects are questioned in separate rooms. The interrogator draws a pair of related questions from a playbook, asks one question in each room, and compares the answers against a rule that only she knows. The suspects agreed on a story beforehand, but once the questioning starts they cannot communicate. Suppose the best possible story lets them pass the comparison with probability $0.9$ — there is an unavoidable $10\%$ chance of being caught. Natural plan: repeat the test. Run twenty independent rounds and accept only if the pair passes every round. Intuition says the survivors' probability should crash to something like $0.9^{20} \approx 0.12$, and with more rounds, exponentially toward zero.

This intuition — repetition amplifies certainty exponentially — is the engine behind error reduction everywhere in computer science, and in the theory of interactive proofs it has a precise name: parallel repetition. All $n$ question pairs are asked at once (that is the "parallel"), each suspect sees their own $n$ questions together, and answers all of them in one go. And here the innocent-looking intuition breaks. Because each player sees all $n$ questions before answering any of them, answers can be correlated across rounds in ways that help. In §3 we will meet a concrete game (Fig. 8) where the probability of surviving two rounds is exactly the same as the probability of surviving one: $\tfrac12$, not $\tfrac14$. Repetition still helps in the classical world — but proving it took until 1995, when Raz's celebrated parallel repetition theorem established exponential decay for every classical game, by a genuinely difficult argument.

Now make the suspects quantum. Before the interrogation they prepare a joint quantum state — a physical resource, fully legal under the rules, since carrying a box into your room is not communication. When the questions arrive, each measures their half of the state in a way that depends on their own question, and answers with the outcome. This is not science fiction generosity: such "entangled games" (Fig. 1) are precisely the mathematical model of Bell-inequality experiments, and the extra power is real. In §2 we will fully work the famous CHSH game, where classical players win with probability at most $3/4$ while entangled players reach $\cos^2(\pi/8) \approx 0.854$ — a gap confirmed in laboratories for four decades.

So the fundamental question, explicitly posed as open in 2004: does parallel repetition still amplify against entangled players? For a game $G$ with entangled value $\omega^*(G) = 1 - \varepsilon \lt 1$, write $G^{\otimes n}$ for the $n$-fold parallel repetition. Playing each copy independently shows $\omega^*(G^{\otimes n}) \ge \omega^*(G)^n$; the question is the matching upper bound: is there $c_G \gt 0$ with $\omega^*(G^{\otimes n}) \le e^{-c_G n}$? For two decades the answer was known only for special classes of games — XOR games, unique games, projection games, free games — or for modified games ("anchored" repetition). The best general result, due to Yuen (2016), gave decay like $n^{-1/4}$: genuine, but polynomial, not exponential (Fig. 2). The chapter we walk through closes the question for every finite game at once.

Theorem 1.1 (quantum parallel repetition; stated precisely in §3). There is a universal constant $c_{\mathrm{qs}} \gt 0$ such that every finite two-player one-round entangled game $G$ with answer alphabets $A, B$ and $\varepsilon = 1 - \omega^*(G) \gt 0$ satisfies $$\omega^*(G^{\otimes n}) \;\le\; \exp\!\left(- c_{\mathrm{qs}}\, \frac{\varepsilon^{13}}{\varepsilon + \log(|A||B|)}\; n\right) \qquad (n \ge 1).$$

Three features deserve immediate emphasis. First, universality: the constant $c_{\mathrm{qs}}$ does not depend on the game — not on the question distribution $\mu$, its support, the alphabet sizes, or the dimension of the entangled state; the game enters only through $\varepsilon$ and the answer-alphabet size. Second, the game is unchanged: unlike anchored repetition, which proves exponential decay for a modified game (useful for hardness amplification but silent about $G$ itself), this theorem speaks about the original $G^{\otimes n}$. Third, the exponent $13$ is inherited from the tools (one of them a quantum state-preparation lemma with a $1/12$-power loss) and is not claimed optimal; the point is that the decay is exponential at all.

Why was this hard for twenty years? The classical proofs (Raz; simplified by Holenstein) run a beautiful conditioning argument: assume the players win all $n$ rounds too often, condition on winning a small core set of rounds, and show that inside the conditioned experiment some single round is played too well — contradicting the value of the single game. The step that breaks quantumly is the conditioning. A classical strategy is a function; you can always resample its randomness consistently with any event you condition on. A quantum strategy's randomness lives in a state, and extracting answers measures — hence destroys — that state. Conditioning on the exponentially rare event "won every core round" is a postselection, and every previously known way to make the players re-create the postselected state paid a factor $1/\mathbb{P}(\text{event})$ — exponentially large, devouring the very bound one wants to prove. Yuen's polynomial theorem managed the loss by conditioning very gently; the price was polynomial decay.

The chapter's new idea is a postselection-stable sampleability estimate (Lemma 3.3, proved in §11–§14): a way to measure how well each player can locally re-create the conditioned state in which the budget itself carries a factor $p = \mathbb{P}(\text{event})$ that cancels — exactly, not approximately — against the $1/p$ of the postselection. No inverse dependence on the conditioning probability survives. The engine of the cancellation is a purification of measurement effects by resolvents, $\Gamma(F) = F(F+uI)^{-1}$, whose squared movements are controlled by an operator entropy that telescopes along a martingale of gradually revealed questions. All of this will be built from scratch and proved.

Here is the route. It is a single spine: each section uses only its predecessors.

  • §1 builds the finite-dimensional quantum formalism from linear algebra: states, tensor products, measurements, the spectral theorem (proved), operator order, partial trace. If you know this material, skim it; everything later cites it by name.
  • §2 defines nonlocal games and the entangled value $\omega^*$, then works the CHSH game end to end: classical value $3/4$, entangled value exactly $\cos^2(\pi/8)$, including a full proof of Tsirelson's bound.
  • §3 defines parallel repetition, proves the easy direction $\omega^*(G^{\otimes n}) \ge \omega^*(G)^n$, proves that Feige's game makes two-fold repetition completely free for the players, surveys what was known, and states Theorem 1.1 precisely.
  • §4 installs the classical information tools: relative entropy, chain rule, Pinsker's inequality, the entropy function $H_1$, Doob martingales, and exact finite correlated sampling (Lemma 3.4) — all proved.
  • §5 presents the proof plan: the contradiction scheme, the four parameters $m, p, q, \eta$, and a map of every lemma.
  • §6–§9 execute the plan modulo the central lemma: greedy conditioning, the ideal conditioned experiment, the central state-alignment lemma (statement), and the rounding of the ideal experiment into a genuine single-game strategy, using the one external import (quantum correlated sampling / embezzlement).
  • §10 chooses the parameters and completes the contradiction — the full proof of Theorem 1.1, including the explicit universal constant.
  • §11–§14 prove the central lemma: reveal martingales, resolvent purification with its one-step entropy estimate, the telescoping budget with the exact $p$-cancellation, and the classical history-sampling half.
  • §15 re-derives the key numbers by machine — including brute-force game values and a numerical end-to-end test of the conditioned-experiment identities on a genuinely entangled two-copy strategy. §16 states honestly what is and is not established.
