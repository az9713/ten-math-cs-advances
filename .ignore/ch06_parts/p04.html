4. The classical information toolkit

The proof of Theorem 1.1 is an information-theoretic argument wearing quantum clothes. This section builds the classical wardrobe: relative entropy and its chain rule, Pinsker's inequality, the entropy function $H_1$ with Jensen's inequality, conditional expectation, and the exact correlated-sampling lemma (Lemma 3.4 of the chapter). Everything is proved; everything is finite.

4.1 Relative entropy and total variation

Definition (relative entropy, total variation). For probability distributions $\mathcal{D}, \mathcal{E}$ on a finite set $U$, $$D(\mathcal{D}\,\|\,\mathcal{E}) = \sum_{u\in U} \mathcal{D}(u)\,\log\frac{\mathcal{D}(u)}{\mathcal{E}(u)}, \qquad d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{E}) = \frac12\sum_{u\in U}\big|\mathcal{D}(u) - \mathcal{E}(u)\big|,$$ with the conventions $0\log 0 = 0$ and $D(\mathcal{D}\|\mathcal{E}) = +\infty$ when $\operatorname{supp}\mathcal{D} \not\subseteq \operatorname{supp}\mathcal{E}$. Logarithms are natural throughout. Named distributions are written in calligraphic font ($\mathcal{P}$, $\mathcal{Q}$, $\mathcal{J}_A$, $\mathcal{J}_B$); the referee's question distribution keeps its conventional symbol $\mu$.

Total variation is the operational distance: $d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{E}) = \max_{S\subseteq U}\,[\mathcal{D}(S) - \mathcal{E}(S)]$ (take $S = \{u : \mathcal{D}(u) \gt \mathcal{E}(u)\}$; any other $S$ only loses mass), so two distributions at total variation $\kappa$ assign every event probabilities within $\kappa$. More generally, for any function $w: U \to [0,1]$: subtracting the constant $\tfrac12$ from $w$ changes neither side of the difference below, and leaves $|w - \tfrac12| \le \tfrac12$, so $$\big|\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}} w - \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{E}} w\big| \;=\; \Big|\sum_u \big(\mathcal{D}(u)-\mathcal{E}(u)\big)\big(w(u)-\tfrac12\big)\Big| \;\le\; \tfrac12\sum_u \big|\mathcal{D}(u)-\mathcal{E}(u)\big| \;=\; d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{E}).$$ We use this expectation transfer bound repeatedly in §9.

Relative entropy is the bookkeeping currency. Its two engines are next.

Proposition J (log-sum inequality). For nonnegative numbers $a_1,\dots,a_k$ and $b_1,\dots,b_k$ with $a = \sum_i a_i$, $b = \sum_i b_i \gt 0$, $$\sum_{i=1}^k a_i \log\frac{a_i}{b_i} \;\ge\; a\,\log\frac{a}{b},$$ with the conventions above. In particular (taking $k = |U|$, $a_i = \mathcal{D}(u)$, $b_i = \mathcal{E}(u)$): $D(\mathcal{D}\|\mathcal{E}) \ge 0$, with equality iff $\mathcal{D} = \mathcal{E}$.
The function $f(t) = t\log t$ is strictly convex on $[0,\infty)$ ($f''(t) = 1/t \gt 0$). Write $\lambda_i = b_i / b$ (a probability vector; terms with $b_i = 0$ force $a_i = 0$ or the left side is $+\infty$ and there is nothing to prove) and $t_i = a_i/b_i$. Then by Jensen's inequality (Proposition L below, whose proof is independent), $$\sum_i a_i\log\frac{a_i}{b_i} = b\sum_i \lambda_i f(t_i) \;\ge\; b\, f\!\Big(\sum_i \lambda_i t_i\Big) = b\,f(a/b) = a\log\frac{a}{b}.$$ Equality in strict convexity forces all $t_i$ equal, i.e. $\mathcal{D}$ proportional — hence equal — to $\mathcal{E}$. (Machine check c13.) ∎
Proposition K (chain rule and its consequences). Let $\mathcal{D}, \mathcal{E}$ be distributions of a pair $(U, V)$. Then $$D(\mathcal{D}_{UV}\,\|\,\mathcal{E}_{UV}) \;=\; D(\mathcal{D}_U\,\|\,\mathcal{E}_U) \;+\; \mathbb{E}_{u\sim \mathcal{D}_U}\, D\big(\mathcal{D}_{V|U=u}\,\big\|\,\mathcal{E}_{V|U=u}\big).$$ Consequences (all used later): (a) marginalization can only lose: $D(\mathcal{D}_U\|\mathcal{E}_U) \le D(\mathcal{D}_{UV}\|\mathcal{E}_{UV})$; (b) convexity in the first argument: $D\big(\sum_k \lambda_k \mathcal{P}_k\,\big\|\,\mathcal{R}\big) \le \sum_k \lambda_k D(\mathcal{P}_k\|\mathcal{R})$; (c) coordinate subadditivity against product references: if $\mathcal{E} = \rho_1\otimes\cdots\otimes\rho_m$ is a product distribution, then $\sum_{i=1}^m D(\mathcal{D}_{U_i}\|\rho_i) \le D(\mathcal{D}_{U_1\cdots U_m}\|\mathcal{E})$.

The chain rule is the identity $\log\frac{\mathcal{D}(u,v)}{\mathcal{E}(u,v)} = \log\frac{\mathcal{D}_U(u)}{\mathcal{E}_U(u)} + \log\frac{\mathcal{D}(v|u)}{\mathcal{E}(v|u)}$ averaged over $\mathcal{D}$. (a): the conditional term is an average of relative entropies, nonnegative by Proposition J. (b): apply the log-sum inequality entrywise: for each outcome $u$, with $a_k = \lambda_k \mathcal{P}_k(u)$ and $b_k = \lambda_k \mathcal{R}(u)$, $$\Big(\sum_k\lambda_k\mathcal{P}_k(u)\Big)\log\frac{\sum_k\lambda_k\mathcal{P}_k(u)}{\mathcal{R}(u)} \le \sum_k \lambda_k\,\mathcal{P}_k(u)\log\frac{\mathcal{P}_k(u)}{\mathcal{R}(u)};$$ sum over $u$. (c): induct on $m$. By the chain rule with $U = U_1$, $V = (U_2,\dots,U_m)$: $$D(\mathcal{D}\|\mathcal{E}) = D(\mathcal{D}_{U_1}\|\rho_1) + \mathbb{E}_{u_1} D\big(\mathcal{D}_{U_2\cdots U_m | u_1}\,\big\|\,\rho_2\otimes\cdots\otimes\rho_m\big) \ge D(\mathcal{D}_{U_1}\|\rho_1) + \sum_{i=2}^m \mathbb{E}_{u_1} D\big(\mathcal{D}_{U_i|u_1}\,\big\|\,\rho_i\big)$$ by the induction hypothesis applied conditionally; then $\mathbb{E}_{u_1} D(\mathcal{D}_{U_i|u_1}\|\rho_i) \ge D(\mathcal{D}_{U_i}\|\rho_i)$ by (b), since $\mathcal{D}_{U_i} = \mathbb{E}_{u_1}[\mathcal{D}_{U_i|u_1}]$. ∎

4.2 Pinsker's inequality

Proposition (Pinsker's inequality). For all distributions $\mathcal{D}, \mathcal{E}$ on a finite set, $$d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{E}) \;\le\; \sqrt{\tfrac12\, D(\mathcal{D}\,\|\,\mathcal{E})}.$$

Step 1: reduce to two outcomes. Let $S = \{u : \mathcal{D}(u) \gt \mathcal{E}(u)\}$, and let $\bar{\mathcal{D}} = (\mathcal{D}(S), 1-\mathcal{D}(S))$, $\bar{\mathcal{E}} = (\mathcal{E}(S), 1-\mathcal{E}(S))$ be the induced two-outcome distributions. Then $d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\bar{\mathcal{D}},\bar{\mathcal{E}}) = \mathcal{D}(S) - \mathcal{E}(S) = d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{E})$, while grouping outcomes can only decrease relative entropy: applying the log-sum inequality separately to the terms inside $S$ and inside $S^c$ gives $D(\bar{\mathcal{D}}\|\bar{\mathcal{E}}) \le D(\mathcal{D}\|\mathcal{E})$. So the binary case suffices.

Step 2: binary case. For $p = \mathcal{D}(S)$, $q = \mathcal{E}(S)$ define $$g(q) \;=\; p\log\frac{p}{q} + (1-p)\log\frac{1-p}{1-q} \;-\; 2(p-q)^2 .$$ Then $g(p) = 0$ and $$g'(q) = -\frac{p}{q} + \frac{1-p}{1-q} + 4(p - q) = (q-p)\left[\frac{1}{q(1-q)} - 4\right].$$ Since $q(1-q) \le \tfrac14$, the bracket is $\ge 0$, so $g' \le 0$ for $q \lt p$ and $g' \ge 0$ for $q \gt p$: the minimum of $g$ is $g(p) = 0$. Hence $D_2(p\|q) \ge 2(p-q)^2 = 2\,d_{\mathrm{TV}}^2$, which rearranges to the claim. (Machine checks c07, c07b: $500$ random pairs, and near-equality for close binary distributions — Fig. 10 shows the binary picture.) ∎

4.3 The entropy function $H_1$ and Jensen's inequality

Proposition L (Jensen, finite form). Let $f$ be concave on an interval $I$, let $t_1,\dots,t_k \in I$ and let $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_k \ge 0$ sum to $1$. Then $\sum_i \lambda_i f(t_i) \le f\big(\sum_i \lambda_i t_i\big)$. For convex $f$ the inequality reverses.
Induction on $k$; the case $k=2$ is the definition of concavity. For $k \gt 2$ with $\lambda_k \lt 1$: write $\sum_{i\le k}\lambda_i t_i = (1-\lambda_k)\, s + \lambda_k t_k$ with $s = \sum_{i\lt k}\frac{\lambda_i}{1-\lambda_k} t_i \in I$; apply the $k=2$ case and then the induction hypothesis to $s$. ∎

The scalar entropy $H_1(v) = -v\log v$ (with $H_1(0) = 0$) from §1.1 is concave, so Jensen gives $\mathbb{E}\,H_1(V) \le H_1(\mathbb{E} V)$ for any $[0,1]$-valued random variable $V$ — Fig. 11. Two consequences we will use verbatim:

  • Counting form. If nonnegative numbers $p_1, \dots, p_N$ sum to $P \le 1$, then, by the log-sum inequality with $b_i = 1$, $\sum_i p_i \log p_i \ge P \log(P/N)$, i.e. $$\sum_{i=1}^N H_1(p_i) \;\le\; H_1(P) + P\log N .$$ This is the exact accounting step of Lemma 4.5 in §13.
  • Power form. For a nonnegative random variable $Z$ and $0 \lt \theta \lt 1$, concavity of $t\mapsto t^\theta$ gives $\mathbb{E}[Z^{\theta}] \le (\mathbb{E} Z)^{\theta}$. We use $\theta = \tfrac1{12}$ (on $Z = \|\cdot\|^2$-type quantities) and $\theta = \tfrac12$ in §9.

4.4 Conditional expectation, for numbers and for operators

Let $\Omega$ be a finite probability space and $\mathcal{G}$ a partition of $\Omega$ into "atoms" (the information available: which atom occurred). The conditional expectation of a random object $F : \Omega \to \mathcal{V}$ (values in any real vector space: scalars, vectors, or Hermitian operators) is the $\mathcal{G}$-measurable object $$\mathbb{E}[F \mid \mathcal{G}](\omega) \;=\; \frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(A_\omega)}\sum_{\omega'\in A_\omega} \mathbb{P}(\omega')\,F(\omega'), \qquad A_\omega = \text{the atom containing } \omega,$$ defined on atoms of positive probability. Two facts, immediate from the formula: linearity, and the tower property — if $\mathcal{G}'$ refines $\mathcal{G}$, then $\mathbb{E}\big[\,\mathbb{E}[F\mid\mathcal{G}']\,\big\vert\,\mathcal{G}\big] = \mathbb{E}[F\mid\mathcal{G}]$, since both sides are the $\mathbb{P}$-weighted average of $F$ over each $\mathcal{G}$-atom. For operator-valued $F$: if every value satisfies $0 \preceq F(\omega) \preceq I$, then $0 \preceq \mathbb{E}[F|\mathcal{G}] \preceq I$, because a convex combination of PSD operators is PSD. A sequence $F_0, F_1, \dots, F_N$ adapted to a growing chain of partitions with $\mathbb{E}[F_{j+1}\mid \mathcal{G}_j] = F_j$ is a martingale; the ones we need arise as $F_j = \mathbb{E}[F \mid \mathcal{G}_j]$ for a fixed $F$ (a Doob martingale — "reveal information one step at a time and track the running best prediction"), for which the martingale property is exactly the tower property. They enter in §11.

4.5 Exact finite correlated sampling (Lemma 3.4)

The last tool answers an operational question: two parties each hold a probability distribution — possibly different — over the same finite set, and want to sample from their own distributions while agreeing as often as their distributions allow. No communication; only shared randomness prepared in advance.

Lemma 3.4 (exact finite classical correlated sampling). Let $\mathcal{F}$ be a finite family of probability distributions on a finite set $R$. There exists a single finite-valued shared random variable and, for each $\mathcal{D}\in\mathcal{F}$, an output $R_{\mathcal{D}}$ computable from the shared variable alone, having exactly distribution $\mathcal{D}$, such that $$\mathbb{P}(R_{\mathcal{D}} \ne R_{\mathcal{E}}) \;\le\; 2\, d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{E}) \qquad (\mathcal{D},\mathcal{E}\in\mathcal{F}). \tag{10}$$

(Chapter's Appendix A.7.) Generate a shared i.i.d. stream of proposals $(Z_j, U_j)_{j\ge1}$ with $Z_j$ uniform on $R$ and $U_j$ uniform on $[0,1]$ — darts thrown uniformly under the box of Fig. 12. The procedure indexed by $\mathcal{D}$ outputs the first $Z_j$ whose companion satisfies $U_j \le \mathcal{D}(Z_j)$. Each proposal is accepted with probability $\sum_z \frac1{|R|}\mathcal{D}(z) = \frac1{|R|}$, and conditional on acceptance the accepted $z$ has probability proportional to $\mathcal{D}(z)$ — so the output has exactly distribution $\mathcal{D}$, for every $\mathcal{D}$ simultaneously, from the same stream.

Now compare the runs for $\mathcal{D}$ and $\mathcal{E}$. Inspect the first proposal accepted by either procedure. Conditional on being that proposal, its pair $(z, u)$ is uniform under the pointwise maximum: the probability that it is accepted by only one procedure is $$\frac{\sum_z |\mathcal{D}(z)-\mathcal{E}(z)|}{\sum_z \max\{\mathcal{D}(z),\mathcal{E}(z)\}} = \frac{2\,d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{E})}{1 + d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{E})} \;\le\; 2\,d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{E}),$$ using $\sum_z\max = \sum_z\tfrac{\mathcal{D}+\mathcal{E}}{2} + \sum_z\tfrac{|\mathcal{D}-\mathcal{E}|}{2} = 1 + d_{\mathrm{TV}}$. If the first-accepted proposal is accepted by both, both procedures stop there with the same output; so disagreement requires the complementary event, giving (10).

Finally, finiteness. Since $\mathcal{F}$ is finite, with probability one every procedure terminates, and the joint output vector $(R_{\mathcal{D}})_{\mathcal{D}\in\mathcal{F}}$ takes values in the finite set $R^{\mathcal{F}}$. Use that vector itself as the shared random variable: each party reads off the component indexed by the distribution they locally hold. This preserves the exact marginals and all pairwise disagreement bounds, with no infinite randomness register. (Machine checks c08a, c08b: the exact disagreement formula and a $200{,}000$-run simulation.) ∎

Lemma 3.4 is the entire classical half of the eventual strategy: in §9, Alice and Bob will apply it to the family of conditional history distributions $\{\mathcal{Q}(R\mid i, X_i = x),\ \mathcal{Q}(R\mid i, Y_i = y)\}_{i,x,y}$, so that from separate questions they can settle on a common transcript of the conditioned repeated game. What it cannot do is prepare a quantum state; that requires the one imported theorem (§9), and the bulk of the proof (§11–§14) is about making both halves' inputs small.
