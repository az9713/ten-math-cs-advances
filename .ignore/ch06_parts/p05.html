5. The proof at altitude

We now have everything needed to state the plan of the whole proof precisely. Theorem 1.1 is proved by contradiction: assume some repeated strategy wins all $n$ coordinates with probability exceeding the claimed bound, and manufacture from it a strategy for the single game $G$ whose winning probability exceeds $\omega^*(G)$ — impossible, since $\omega^*(G)$ is a supremum over all strategies.

5.1 The standing experiment and the four parameters

Fix an actual finite-dimensional strategy for $G^{\otimes n}$: shared state $|\psi\rangle$, local measurements $\{E^{a^n}_{x^n}\}_{a^n\in A^n}$ and $\{F^{b^n}_{y^n}\}_{b^n\in B^n}$. Running the referee's experiment with this strategy generates a random question-and-answer word $(X^n, Y^n, A^n, B^n)$ with distribution $$\mathcal{P}(x^n, y^n, a^n, b^n) \;=\; \mu^{\otimes n}(x^n,y^n)\; \langle\psi|\, E^{a^n}_{x^n}\otimes F^{b^n}_{y^n}\,|\psi\rangle .$$ Write $W_i$ for the event that coordinate $i$ is won, i.e. $V(X_i, Y_i, A_i, B_i) = 1$, and for $D \subseteq [n]$, $$W_D = \bigcap_{j\in D} W_j, \qquad \vartheta = \mathcal{P}(W_{[n]}) .$$ The letter $\vartheta$ always denotes the all-coordinates success probability of the fixed strategy; the contradiction hypothesis will be that $\vartheta$ is larger than the bound of Theorem 1.1 allows.

The argument revolves around a small core $D \subsetneq [n]$ of coordinates — chosen in §6 — and four derived parameters:

$$m = n - |D|, \qquad p = \mathcal{P}(W_D), \qquad q = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i\in[n]\setminus D} \mathcal{P}(W_i \mid W_D), \qquad \eta = \frac{\log(1/p) + |D|\,\log(|A||B|)}{m}. \tag{1}$$

In words: $p$ is the probability of winning every core coordinate; $q$ is the average conditional winning probability of the remaining coordinates given core success; and $\eta$ is the information cost per remaining coordinate — the total "surprise" of the conditioning event ($\log(1/p)$ units) plus the total description length of the core answers ($|D|$ answers from an alphabet of size $|A||B|$, so $|D|\log(|A||B|)$ units), divided among the $m$ live coordinates. The conditioning lemma will make $q$ close to $1$ while $\eta$ stays small; crucially, $p$ itself is allowed to be exponentially small — only its logarithm is budgeted.

The entire quantum construction (§7–§9) exists to prove one implication. With $B_{\mathrm{qs}} \ge 1$ the universal rounding constant defined in §9 (equation (11)):

Operational goal. For the core $D$ supplied by Lemma 3.1, $$0 \le \eta \le 1 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \omega^*(G) \;\ge\; q - B_{\mathrm{qs}}\,\eta^{1/12}.$$

Its left side is computed inside the conditioned repeated experiment; its right side asserts the existence of an actual single-game strategy, receiving fresh questions from $\mu$, whose value is nearly $q$. Once this implication stands, §10 finishes by arithmetic: choose parameters so that $q \ge 1 - \varepsilon/4$ and $B_{\mathrm{qs}}\eta^{1/12} \le \varepsilon/4 + \varepsilon/8$, forcing $\omega^*(G) \gt 1 - \varepsilon$, the desired contradiction.

5.2 Why the classical proof does not port, and what replaces it

The classical Raz–Holenstein argument implements the same outline: condition on winning the core, then embed a fresh single-game question pair into a random live coordinate of the conditioned experiment, using correlated sampling to let both players agree on the conditioned transcript. The single step with no quantum analogue is the innocuous-sounding "run the conditioned strategy". A classical strategy conditioned on an event is still a strategy: its randomness is a variable you can resample under the conditional law. A quantum strategy's "randomness" is the shared state, and the event $W_D$ lives in the outcomes of measurements that have already been performed on it. Conditioning is postselection: the players would need to hold the leftover state given that the core answers came out right — and if they try to produce it by actually playing and discarding failures, they wait $1/p$ attempts, which is exponential; every quantitative version of this replay loses a factor polynomial in $1/p$ (Fig. 13). Yuen's polynomial theorem tamed the loss by keeping $p$ polynomially large — conditioning on very little — and paid with polynomial decay.

The chapter's resolution is not to avoid postselection but to make every estimate postselection-stable: each bound about the conditioned experiment is derived as (an unnormalized bound carrying an explicit factor $p$) divided by (the posterior weight, carrying $1/p$), and the two cancel identically. Concretely, the central Lemma 3.3 asserts:

  • State alignment. The "ideal" conditioned state $\Psi_{r,x,y}$ of the live coordinate (defined in §7; it depends on the full history $r$ and both live questions $x, y$) is, on average over the posterior, $8\eta$-close in squared norm to a state $\Psi^A_{r,x}$ that Alice can describe from her data alone, and likewise to a Bob-describable $\Psi^B_{r,y}$.
  • History sampleability. The posterior distribution of the history is within total variation $\kappa = \sqrt{3\eta/2}$ of distributions each player can sample using only their own question, so Lemma 3.4 lets them agree on a common history except with probability $4\kappa$.

Note what is absent: no $1/p$, no minimum question probability, no structure of $\mu$. The price of quantumness is only the exponent — the states are prepared by the imported embezzlement theorem at a $1/12$-power loss, which is where $\eta^{1/12}$ (and ultimately $\varepsilon^{13}$) comes from.

One reading note before we descend. The proof manipulates three different probability measures over the same experiment, and confusing them is the main hazard of the chapter: $\mathcal{P}$ (the prior: the strategy simply played), $\mathcal{Q} = \mathcal{P}(\,\cdot\mid W_D)$ (the posterior: conditioned on core success), and the locally generated $\mathcal{J}_A, \mathcal{J}_B$ of §8 (what the players can actually sample). Factorization identities hold under $\mathcal{P}$; success probabilities are computed under $\mathcal{Q}$; the strategy operates under $\mathcal{J}$; the lemmas bound the distances between them.
