3. Parallel repetition, and why it is genuinely subtle

3.1 The repeated game

Definition 2.2 (standard parallel repetition). For $n \ge 1$, the repeated game $G^{\otimes n}$ samples $n$ independent question pairs $$(x^n, y^n) \sim \mu^{\otimes n}, \qquad x^n = (x_1,\dots,x_n),\; y^n = (y_1,\dots,y_n),$$ sends the whole tuple $x^n$ to Alice and $y^n$ to Bob, permits arbitrary finite-dimensional joint local POVMs $$\{E^{a^n}_{x^n} : a^n \in A^n\}, \qquad \{F^{b^n}_{y^n} : b^n \in B^n\},$$ and accepts according to $$V^{\otimes n}(x^n, y^n, a^n, b^n) \;=\; \prod_{i=1}^n V(x_i, y_i, a_i, b_i).$$ Write $v_n = \omega^*(G^{\otimes n})$ for its entangled value. For $S \subseteq [n] = \{1,\dots,n\}$ and a tuple $x^n$, write $x_S = (x_j)_{j\in S}$, and $$\mu^S(x_S, y_S) = \prod_{j\in S}\mu(x_j,y_j), \qquad \mu^S_X(x_S) = \prod_{j\in S}\mu_X(x_j), \qquad \mu^S_Y(y_S) = \prod_{j\in S}\mu_Y(y_j).$$

The products over $S$ express independence between distinct coordinates of the referee's experiment, not independence between the two questions of one coordinate (those are correlated through $\mu$). The crucial freedom, drawn in Fig. 7: although the referee's coordinates are independent, Alice answers with one measurement on all of $x^n$ — her answer $a_i$ at coordinate $i$ may depend on every question she received, and likewise for Bob. Winning probabilities therefore need not factor across coordinates.

Proposition H (independent play; the easy direction). $\;\omega^*(G^{\otimes n}) \ge \omega^*(G)^n$.
Let $(\psi, E, F)$ be any strategy for $G$ with value $w$. Take $n$ copies: state $\psi^{\otimes n} \in (\mathcal{H}_A\otimes\mathcal{H}_B)^{\otimes n}$ (reordered so all Alice factors are together), Alice's POVM $E^{a^n}_{x^n} = E^{a_1}_{x_1}\otimes\cdots\otimes E^{a_n}_{x_n}$, similarly for Bob. The joint winning probability factors: each coordinate wins independently with probability $w$, so all $n$ win with probability $w^n$. Taking suprema over single-game strategies gives $v_n \ge \omega^*(G)^n$ (the function $w \mapsto w^n$ is continuous and increasing, so the supremum passes through). ∎

3.2 A game where repetition is free: Feige's counterexample

If cross-coordinate answers could not help, $v_n$ would equal $\omega^*(G)^n$ and this chapter would not exist. The following classical game, due to Feige, kills that hope in the strongest way: two copies cost the players nothing at all.

Toy (Feige's game $G_F$). Questions are independent fair bits: $x, y \in \{0,1\}$, $\mu$ uniform on $\{0,1\}^2$. Each answer is a claim naming a player and a bit: $A = B = \{1,2\}\times\{0,1\}$, an answer $(c,d)$ read as "player $c$ received the bit $d$". The players win iff they make the same, true claim: $$V\big(x,y,(c,d),(c',d')\big) = 1 \iff (c,d) = (c',d') \text{ and } \big[(c = 1 \wedge d = x)\text{ or } (c = 2 \wedge d = y)\big].$$
Proposition I (repetition is free for $G_F$). $$\omega(G_F) = \tfrac12 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \omega(G_F^{\otimes 2}) = \tfrac12 \;=\; \omega(G_F) \;\ne\; \omega(G_F)^2.$$

(i) Single game, upper bound. By Proposition D take deterministic $f, g$ and view the four question pairs as a $2\times2$ grid. Suppose some row is fully won: $(x,0)$ and $(x,1)$ both win for a fixed $x$. Then $g(0) = f(x) = g(1) =: (c,d)$; if $c = 2$, correctness at both $y$ forces $d = 0$ and $d = 1$, impossible; so $(c,d) = (1,x)$. Symmetrically, a fully won column (fixed $y$, both $x$) forces $f(0) = f(1) = g(y) = (2,y)$. Now any $3$ winning cells of a $2\times2$ grid contain a full row and a full column, which would force $f(x)$ to equal both $(1,x)$ and $(2,y)$ — contradiction. So at most $2$ of the $4$ cells are won: $\omega(G_F) \le 1/2$. Both players always answering $(1,0)$ win exactly when $x = 0$: value $1/2$. (Machine check c04: the maximum over all $256$ deterministic pairs is exactly $0.5$.)

(ii) Two copies, lower bound: the cross strategy. In $G_F^{\otimes 2}$, Alice sees $(x_1, x_2)$ and Bob $(y_1, y_2)$. Let both route their claims through coordinate-crossed data (Fig. 8): $$a_1 = (1, x_1),\quad a_2 = (2, x_1); \qquad b_1 = (1, y_2),\quad b_2 = (2, y_2).$$ Coordinate 1 is won iff $a_1 = b_1$ and the claim is true; $a_1 = (1,x_1)$ is automatically true at coordinate 1, and $b_1 = a_1$ iff $y_2 = x_1$. Coordinate 2 is won iff $a_2 = b_2$ and the claim "player 2 received $d$" is true at coordinate 2, i.e. $d = y_2$; here $b_2 = (2, y_2)$ is automatically true, and $a_2 = b_2$ iff $x_1 = y_2$. So both coordinates are won precisely on the single event $\{x_1 = y_2\}$, of probability $\tfrac12$. (Machine check c05.)

(iii) Two copies, upper bound. Given any deterministic strategy for $G_F^{\otimes 2}$ with $\mathbb{P}(\text{win both}) = v$, build a single-game strategy: on question $x$, Alice privately samples a fair bit $x_2$ and answers with the first component $a_1$ of her $G_F^{\otimes 2}$ answer to $(x, x_2)$; Bob privately samples $y_2$ and answers $b_1$ of his answer to $(y, y_2)$. Because $\mu$ is a product distribution, the simulated question 4-tuple $(x, y, x_2, y_2)$ has exactly the distribution $\mu^{\otimes 2}$, so the simulated experiment is the true repeated experiment, and $$\omega(G_F) \;\ge\; \mathbb{P}(\text{win coordinate } 1) \;\ge\; \mathbb{P}(\text{win both}) = v .$$ Hence $\omega(G_F^{\otimes 2}) \le \omega(G_F) = 1/2$, met by (ii). (Machine check c06 confirms by exact search: over all $16^4 = 65{,}536$ Alice strategies, with Bob best-responding, the maximum is exactly $0.5$.) ∎

Feige's game is a counterexample to the naive product intuition, not to repetition itself: by Raz's theorem its repeated value does eventually decay exponentially (indeed $\omega(G_F^{\otimes n}) \le \omega(G_F^{\otimes 2})^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}$ gives nothing — the point is that no such easy argument works, while Raz's theorem still does). What the example proves is that any repetition theorem must handle strategies whose per-coordinate failures are engineered to overlap. Keep the picture of Fig. 8 in mind: all of §6–§14 is machinery for prying one coordinate loose from such couplings.

3.3 The theorem, and the landscape it settles

Theorem 1.1 (quantum parallel repetition theorem for two-player games). There exists a universal constant $c_{\mathrm{qs}} \gt 0$ such that the following holds. Let $G = (X,Y,A,B,\mu,V)$ be any finite two-player one-round entangled game with nonempty answer alphabets, and put $$\varepsilon = 1 - \omega^*(G) \gt 0, \qquad \ell = \log(|A||B|).$$ Then, for every integer $n \ge 1$, $$\omega^*(G^{\otimes n}) \;\le\; \exp\!\left(-\,c_{\mathrm{qs}}\,\frac{\varepsilon^{13}}{\varepsilon + \log(|A||B|)}\,n\right).$$

The proof will produce the explicit constant $c_{\mathrm{qs}} = \tfrac{1}{8}(4 B_{\mathrm{qs}})^{-12}$, where $B_{\mathrm{qs}}$ is a universal rounding constant assembled in §9 from the quantum sampling import. The exponent $13$ tracks the $1/12$-power loss of that import plus one factor of $\varepsilon$ from the conditioning budget; §10 displays the accounting and §16 discusses why nobody believes it is sharp.

Fig. 9 places the theorem among its predecessors; each row is a genuine theorem with its own techniques, and the history explains why the bottom-right cell stayed open for twenty years.

Three prior quantitative benchmarks are worth recording exactly, for comparison in §16. Classically, Holenstein's information-theoretic form of Raz's theorem gives $$\omega(G^{\otimes n}) \le \exp\!\left(-\Omega\!\left(\frac{\varepsilon^3\, n}{1 + \log(|A||B|)}\right)\right), \qquad \varepsilon = 1 - \omega(G),$$ so the classical exponent loses $\varepsilon^3$ where Theorem 1.1 loses $\varepsilon^{13}$. (Raz's separate counterexample shows some polynomial loss in $\varepsilon$ is necessary even classically: the strongest conceivable bound, linear in $\varepsilon$, is false.) Quantumly, Yuen's theorem for arbitrary games reads $$\omega^*(G^{\otimes n}) \;\le\; c\,\frac{s_G\, \log n}{\varepsilon^{17}\, n^{1/4}} \qquad (n \ge 2),$$ with $s_G$ the answer bit-length — decay, but no exponential. And anchored repetition (Bavarian–Vidick–Yuen) achieves exponential decay for a game $G_\perp$ obtained by adding "anchor" questions to $G$; excellent for amplifying hardness, but a statement about a different game.

Finally, the model matters when $\omega^*(G) = 0$: unusual, but allowed, and Theorem 1.1 covers it with a direct argument in §10 (the bound is trivially $0$). From here on the interesting case is $0 \lt \varepsilon \lt 1$.
