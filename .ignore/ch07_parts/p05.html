5. Characteristic-two fields and Reed–Solomon codes

The encoding of §6 lives inside a large finite field of characteristic two. This section constructs those fields, records the two polynomial facts the whole chapter leans on (the root bound and Lagrange interpolation), and defines Reed–Solomon codes together with the parity checks that will later become rows of the binary matrix $H$.

5.1 Building $\mathbb{F}_{2^e}$

Let $\mathbb{F}_2[x]$ be the ring of polynomials with coefficients in $\mathbb{F}_2$. A polynomial $\pi$ of degree $e \ge 1$ is irreducible if it is not a product of two polynomials of smaller degree. Since $\mathbb{F}_2[x]$ has division with remainder, the ideal $(\pi)$ of multiples of an irreducible $\pi$ is maximal (any polynomial outside it has $\gcd 1$ with $\pi$, so Bézout gives an inverse mod $\pi$), and therefore the quotient $$K = \mathbb{F}_2[x]/(\pi)$$ is a field with exactly $2^e$ elements: the residues of the $2^e$ polynomials of degree $< e$. Addition is coefficientwise XOR; multiplication is polynomial multiplication followed by reduction mod $\pi$. Every such $K$ has characteristic two: $1 + 1 = 0$, so every element is its own negative — a fact we will exploit constantly ($-$ and $+$ agree, and squaring is additive: $(a+b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2 = a^2 + b^2$). Fig. 14 lays out the sixteen elements of $\mathbb{F}_{16} = \mathbb{F}_2[x]/(x^4 + x + 1)$, the toy field used in all worked examples and in the machine validation.

Does an irreducible $\pi$ of every degree $e$ exist — and can we find one deterministically? Yes on both counts.

Proposition C (existence and deterministic construction of $\mathbb{F}_{2^e}$). For every $e \ge 1$ there is an irreducible $\pi \in \mathbb{F}_2[x]$ of degree $e$; consequently a field with $2^e$ elements exists. Moreover, when $2^e$ is bounded by a polynomial in the relevant input parameter (here $q < 2N^{200}$), such a $\pi$ can be found by exhaustive search in deterministic polynomial time.
We use three steps, each standard graduate algebra, proved here for self-containment.

Step 1: some finite extension contains $2^e$ distinct roots of $f(x) = x^{2^e} - x$. Given any nonconstant polynomial over any field, the quotient construction above (by any irreducible factor) produces an extension field in which it acquires a root; splitting off that root and iterating produces a finite extension $E \supseteq \mathbb{F}_2$ in which $f$ factors into linear factors. The formal derivative of $f$ is $f' = 2^e x^{2^e - 1} - 1 = 1$ in characteristic two (the first term has even coefficient), so $\gcd(f, f') = 1$ and $f$ has no repeated roots: a repeated root $\alpha$ would make $(x-\alpha)^2 \mid f$, forcing $(x - \alpha) \mid f'$. Hence $f$ has exactly $2^e$ distinct roots in $E$.

Step 2: the roots form a field $F$ with $\lvert F \rvert = 2^e$. The map $\phi(a) = a^2$ satisfies $\phi(a+b) = \phi(a) + \phi(b)$ (char 2) and $\phi(ab) = \phi(a)\phi(b)$; so its $e$-fold iterate $a \mapsto a^{2^e}$ is a ring homomorphism, and the set of $a$ with $a^{2^e} = a$ is closed under addition, multiplication, and (for $a \ne 0$, multiplying the equation by $a^{-2^e}$) inversion. It contains $0$ and $1$. So the root set is a subfield of $E$ of size $2^e$.

Step 3: $F^\times$ is cyclic, and a generator's minimal polynomial has degree $e$. Let $F^\times$ be the multiplicative group, of order $s = 2^e - 1$, and let $x_0 \in F^\times$ have maximal multiplicative order $\mathrm{ord}(x_0) = h$. For any $y \in F^\times$, $\mathrm{ord}(y)$ divides $h$: otherwise some prime power $\pi^k \,\|\, \mathrm{ord}(y)$ exceeds the power of $\pi$ in $h$, and the element $x_0^{\pi^{a}} y^{\,\mathrm{ord}(y)/\pi^{k}}$ (with $\pi^a \,\|\, h$) has order $\mathrm{lcm} > h$ — using that for commuting elements of coprime orders, orders multiply. Hence every element of $F^\times$ satisfies $x^h = 1$; the polynomial $x^h - 1$ has at most $h$ roots in a field (Proposition D below), so $s \le h$, i.e. $x_0$ generates. Now let $\theta$ generate $F^\times$ and let $\pi_\theta$ be its minimal polynomial over $\mathbb{F}_2$ (the lowest-degree monic polynomial vanishing at $\theta$; it is irreducible, since a factorization would make a smaller-degree factor vanish at $\theta$). The subfield $\mathbb{F}_2(\theta) \subseteq F$ contains all powers of $\theta$ and $0$, hence equals $F$; and $\mathbb{F}_2(\theta) \cong \mathbb{F}_2[x]/(\pi_\theta)$ has $2^{\deg \pi_\theta}$ elements, forcing $\deg \pi_\theta = e$.

Deterministic search. Enumerate the $2^e$ monic binary polynomials of degree $e$; test each for irreducibility by trial division by all $\approx 2^{e/2+1}$ polynomials of degree $\le e/2$. Total cost $2^{O(e)} = N^{O(1)}$ bit operations when $2^e \le 2N^{200}$ — brute force is genuinely fast enough here, because $e = O(\log N)$. (Standard polynomial-time irreducibility tests do better; they are not needed.) ∎

5.2 The two polynomial workhorses

Proposition D (root bound). Over any field $K$, a nonzero polynomial $f \in K[X]$ of degree $\le D$ has at most $D$ roots in $K$. Consequently two polynomials of degree $\le D$ agreeing at $D+1$ points are equal.
Induction on $D$. If $f(\alpha) = 0$, divide with remainder: $f = (X-\alpha) g + r$ with $r$ constant; evaluating at $\alpha$ gives $r = 0$, so $f = (X - \alpha)g$ with $\deg g = \deg f - 1$ (the factor theorem). Any other root $\beta \neq \alpha$ satisfies $(\beta - \alpha) g(\beta) = 0$, and a field has no zero divisors, so $g(\beta) = 0$; by induction $g$ has at most $D - 1$ roots. The consequence follows by applying the bound to the difference. ∎
Proposition E (Lagrange interpolation). Let $a_1, \dots, a_m \in K$ be distinct and $\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_m \in K$. There is exactly one polynomial $Q \in K[X]$ with $\deg Q \le m-1$ and $Q(a_i) = \sigma_i$ for all $i$, namely $$Q(X) = \sum_{i=1}^m \sigma_i \prod_{\substack{1 \le h \le m \\ h \ne i}} \frac{X - a_h}{a_i - a_h}.$$
Each product is a polynomial of degree $m-1$ (denominators are nonzero scalars since the $a_i$ are distinct) that equals $1$ at $a_i$ and $0$ at every other $a_h$; the displayed sum therefore has degree $\le m-1$ and the required values. Uniqueness is Proposition D applied to the difference of two candidates, which vanishes at the $m > m-1$ points $a_i$. ∎

5.3 Reed–Solomon codes and their parity checks

Definition (Reed–Solomon evaluation code). Let $K = \mathbb{F}_q$, let $P \subseteq K$ be a set of evaluation points, and let $0 \le D < \lvert P \rvert$. The Reed–Solomon code of degree $D$ on $P$ is the set of all evaluation vectors of low-degree polynomials: $$\mathrm{RS}(P, D) \;=\; \Big\{\, \big(f(p)\big)_{p \in P} \;:\; f \in K[X],\ \deg f \le D \,\Big\} \;\le\; K^{P}.$$

By Proposition D, distinct polynomials of degree $\le D$ give distinct codewords (they cannot agree at $\lvert P\rvert > D$ points), so each codeword has a unique interpolating polynomial; the code is a $K$-linear subspace of dimension $D + 1$ (a basis of polynomials $1, X, \dots, X^D$ gives a basis of codewords). Membership is a linear condition: form the $(D{+}1) \times \lvert P \rvert$ evaluation matrix of the monomials, compute a basis of its null space by Gaussian elimination over $K$ — each null vector $(\lambda_p)_{p}$ gives a parity check $\sum_{p} \lambda_p\, y_p = 0$ satisfied exactly by... more precisely, a vector $y \in K^P$ is a codeword iff it is orthogonal to all $\lvert P \rvert - D - 1$ basis null vectors, because the code is precisely the row space of the evaluation matrix and orthogonality to a subspace's complement characterizes membership (rank–nullity over the field $K$). Fig. 15 shows what a Reed–Solomon codeword actually looks like — and what it does not look like.

One last translation step makes everything binary. Fix once and for all an $\mathbb{F}_2$-basis of $K$ (for the quotient construction, the residues $1, x, \dots, x^{e-1}$). Every element of $K$ is then a length-$e$ bit vector, and every $K$-linear equation $\sum_j \lambda_j y_j = \mu$ in unknowns $y_j$ becomes, upon expanding each product $\lambda_j y_j$ bitwise, a system of exactly $e$ binary-linear equations in the bits of the $y_j$. In our use case the unknowns will themselves be single bits $x_{\tau,p,w} \in \{0,1\}$ multiplied by known field constants, so each field-valued parity check contributes $e$ rows to the binary matrix $H$ — this is where the system (3) of §7 gets its rows.
