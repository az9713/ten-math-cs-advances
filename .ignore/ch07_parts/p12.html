12. Soundness II: roots remember clause bits (Lemma 12)

Here is the payoff of the shifted moments (C4). Each clause table $(C,\beta)$ committed to a local assignment $\beta$; the following lemma shows that this commitment is burned into every root of the table's polynomial $G_{(C,\beta)}$: at the anchor $a_i$ of each clause variable, the root is infinitesimally close to the committed bit $\beta_i$. The proof is the chapter's technical summit — a pure valuation-theory argument, using every tool from §10.

Setup: for each variable index $i$, the anchor $a_i$ lies outside $P$, and by Lemma 4 there is a valuation $v_i$ on the common splitting field $E$ extending $\mathrm{ord}_{X - a_i}$. Fix one such $v_i$ per variable, once and for all — the same $v_i$ is used for every clause table involving variable $i$. (This shared choice is what will let different clauses talk to each other in §13.)

Lemma 12 (local valuation extraction). Let $\tau = (C, \beta)$ be a clause subtype, $i \in I_C$, and $E/F$ the common finite separable splitting field of §11. Then for every root $\alpha \in R_\tau$, $$v_i(\alpha - \beta_i) \;\ge\; 1. \tag{16}$$

Write $\ell_i = X - a_i$, $h = h_\tau$, and enumerate the distinct roots as $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_h$; there is nothing to prove if $h = 0$. Abbreviate this table's Hankel determinant $\Delta_h(X) = \det(\mu_{\tau, r+s}(X))_{0\le r,s<h}$.

Step 0: the shifted moments are polynomials tied to the ordinary ones. Recall from (2) that $\eta_{\tau,i,j}(X)$ is the unique polynomial of degree $\le (d-1)j$ interpolating the shifted moment vector. Pointwise, for each $p \in P$ and $w \in S_\tau(p)$, the binomial theorem gives $(w - \beta_i)^j = \sum_{l=0}^j \binom{j}{l} (-\beta_i)^{j-l} w^l$ (an identity with integer coefficients, valid in any characteristic); multiplying the definition of $\eta_{\tau,i,j}(p)$ by $(p - a_i)^j$ and summing over the fiber: $$\ell_i^{\,j}\, \eta_{\tau,i,j}(X) \;=\; \sum_{l=0}^{j} \binom{j}{l} (-\beta_i)^{j-l}\, \mu_{\tau,l}(X) \tag{17}$$ holds at every $p \in P$; both sides are polynomials of degree $\le dj < \lvert P\rvert$, so by the root bound (17) is an identity in $K[X]$ — not merely an agreement on the sampled grid.

Step 1: recentered roots and their power sums. Define the recentered, rescaled roots $$y_s \;=\; \frac{\alpha_s - \beta_i}{\ell_i} \;\in\; E \qquad (1 \le s \le h).$$ Combining (17) with the root-moment identities (15) — expand $(\alpha_s - \beta_i)^j$ binomially, sum over $s$, and compare with (17) — yields $$\sum_{s=1}^{h} y_s^{\,j} \;=\; \eta_{\tau,i,j}(X) \qquad (0 \le j \le T). \tag{18}$$ The right-hand side is a polynomial in $X$, so every displayed power sum has $v_i \ge 0$. The whole game is now: the $y_s$ have nonnegative-valuation power sums, and we must conclude each $y_s$ itself has nonnegative valuation, i.e. $v_i(\alpha_s - \beta_i) = v_i(y_s \ell_i) = v_i(y_s) + 1 \ge 1$.

Step 2: the $y_s$ satisfy a monic equation with controlled coefficients. Write $G_\tau(Y) = Y^h + \sum_{l<h} c_l Y^l$ and $D_0 = dh^2 + h$. Lemma 10(iii) gives $c_l = n_l/\Delta_h$ with polynomial numerator and denominator, so $$v_i(c_l) = \mathrm{ord}_{\ell_i}(n_l) - \mathrm{ord}_{\ell_i}(\Delta_h) \ \ge\ 0 - \deg \Delta_h \ \ge\ -dh(h-1) \ \ge\ -dh^2$$ (a nonzero polynomial's order at a point is between $0$ and its degree). The $y_s$ are precisely the roots of the recentered polynomial $$\ell_i^{-h}\, G_\tau(\ell_i Y + \beta_i) \;=\; Y^h + \sum_{l < h} b_l\, Y^l, \qquad b_l = \ell_i^{\,l-h} \Bigg[ \binom{h}{l} \beta_i^{\,h-l} + \sum_{k'=l}^{h-1} c_{k'} \binom{k'}{l} \beta_i^{\,k'-l} \Bigg] \in F,$$ (substitute $Y \mapsto \ell_i Y + \beta_i$ and expand binomially) and each coefficient obeys, term by term, $$v_i(b_l) \;\ge\; (l - h) + \min(0,\ \min_{k'} v_i(c_{k'})) \;\ge\; -h - dh^2 \;=\; -D_0 . \tag{†}$$ Set $b_h = 1$. If some $y_s = 0$, then $v_i(y_s) = +\infty$ and (16) already holds for that root; so fix $s$ with $y_s \ne 0$ and let $t_s = v_i(y_s) \in \mathbb{Q}$.

Step 3: a floor. If $t_s < -D_0$, then in the vanishing sum $y_s^h + \sum_{l<h} b_l y_s^l = 0$ the leading term would be the unique minimum-valuation summand: for every $l < h$ with $b_l \ne 0$, $$v_i(b_l\, y_s^{\,l}) \ \ge\ -D_0 + l\, t_s \ >\ (h-l)\,t_s + l\,t_s \ =\ v_i(y_s^{\,h}),$$ using $(h-l)t_s \le t_s < -D_0$. By Lemma 2 such a sum cannot vanish — contradiction. Hence $$t_s \ \ge\ -D_0. \tag{19}$$

Step 4: negative means substantially negative. Suppose $t_s < 0$. Since $\sum_{l=0}^{h} b_l y_s^l = 0$ (with $b_h = 1$), Lemma 2 forces the minimum of $v_i(b_l) + l\,t_s$ over the indices with $b_l \neq 0$ to be attained at least twice: say at $l < k'$. The coefficients $b_l$ lie in $F$, where $v_i$ is the integer-valued $\mathrm{ord}_{\ell_i}$, so $$(k' - l)\, t_s \;=\; v_i(b_l) - v_i(b_{k'}) \;\in\; \mathbb{Z}, \qquad 1 \le k' - l \le h,$$ i.e. $t_s$ is a fraction with denominator at most $h$; being negative, it satisfies $$t_s \le -\tfrac1h. \tag{20}$$ This is the pivot of the whole proof (Fig. 32): ramification can shrink valuations, but only down to $1/h$-quanta — a negative $t_s$ cannot sneak arbitrarily close to zero.

Step 5: amplification through the moment window. Let $V = (y_s^{\,j})_{0 \le j < h,\ 1 \le s \le h}$, the Vandermonde matrix of the $y_s$. Since $y_s - y_r = (\alpha_s - \alpha_r)/\ell_i$, Proposition G and equation (8) give $$(\det V)^2 = \prod_{r<s} (y_s - y_r)^2 = \ell_i^{-h(h-1)}\, \Delta_h .$$ As $\Delta_h \in K[X]\setminus\{0\}$, we get $0 \le v_i(\Delta_h) \le \deg \Delta_h \le dh(h-1)$; so $V$ is invertible and $$2\, v_i(\det V) = v_i(\Delta_h) - h(h-1) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_i(\det V) \le \tfrac12 (d-1)h(h-1) \le dh^2 .$$ By (19) every entry of $V$ has $v_i \ge (h-1)(-D_0) \ge -hD_0$, so every cofactor (a sum of products of $h - 1$ entries) has $v_i \ge -h^2 D_0$, and the inverse $V^{-1} = \mathrm{adj}(V)/\det V$ satisfies $$v_i\big( (V^{-1})_{s,j} \big) \ \ge\ -h^2 D_0 - dh^2 \ \ge\ -U_h, \qquad U_h := h^2 D_0 + dh^2 + 1 \ \le\ 4N^{17}. \tag{21}$$ Now choose the amplification exponent $$z = h\,U_h + 1;$$ the required moments exist because $z + h - 1 \le 4N^{21} + N^4 \le 5N^{21} < T = N^{30}$. Equation (18) at exponents $z, z+1, \dots, z+h-1$ reads, in matrix form, $$V \begin{pmatrix} y_1^{\,z} \\ \vdots \\ y_h^{\,z} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \eta_{\tau,i,z} \\ \vdots \\ \eta_{\tau,i,z+h-1} \end{pmatrix},$$ (the $j$-th row is $\sum_s y_s^{\,j}\, y_s^{\,z} = \sum_s y_s^{\,z+j}$). The right-hand entries are polynomials: $v_i \ge 0$. Applying $V^{-1}$ and the ultrametric inequality entrywise with (21): $$v_i\big( y_s^{\,z} \big) \ \ge\ -U_h \qquad (1 \le s \le h).$$ But if $t_s < 0$, then by (20) $$v_i\big( y_s^{\,z} \big) = z\, t_s \ \le\ -\frac{hU_h + 1}{h} \ =\ -U_h - \frac1h \ <\ -U_h,$$ a contradiction. Hence $t_s \ge 0$ for every root, i.e. $v_i(\alpha_s - \beta_i) = t_s + 1 \ge 1$, which is (16). ∎

Pause on what was just proved. The table $(C,\beta)$ was an arbitrary slice of a hostile low-weight solution — yet every one of its algebraic roots evaluates (in the residue sense of §10.3) to the committed bit $\beta_i$ at the anchor $a_i$, for every variable $i$ of the clause. The mechanism to remember: had the “wrong bit” been committed, the shifted moments would carry a hidden pole at the anchor (the secant picture of Fig. 20); constraint (C4) forces them to be polynomials; and the amplification argument converts “power sums are pole-free up to exponent $T$” into “each individual root is pole-free and lands on $\beta_i$” — with the moment budget $T = N^{30}$ sized precisely so that the $z$-th-power window fits inside it.
