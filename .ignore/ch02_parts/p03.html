3. The new machine: a subspace that moves with each code point

This section contains the one genuinely new idea of the chapter, isolated from all the machinery that feeds it. It is a six-hypothesis proposition whose proof fits on one screen. Everything after this section is the (substantial) craft of manufacturing its inputs.

3.1 From a pinned vector to a moving projection

The classical recipe of §2.3 attaches one unit vector $u_x$ to each candidate point $x$ and forms the Gram kernel $\langle u_x,u_y\rangle$. The new recipe attaches a whole $d$-dimensional subspace — concretely, the orthogonal projection $P_x$ onto that subspace, acting on a fixed $D$-dimensional ambient space $\mathcal{V}$. The subspaces move with the points: a symmetry of $X$ carrying $x$ to $y$ carries the subspace at $x$ to the subspace at $y$. The kernel becomes the overlap of the two projections,

$$K(x,y) \;=\; \operatorname{tr}(P_xP_y),$$

and the code bound will divide by the rank $d$. When $d$ can be made exponentially large in $n$ — and the whole game is that it can — that division subtracts a linear-in-$n$ quantity from the classical exponent.

Three elementary facts about the overlap kernel, each one line, are the foundation. Recall that the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product of two linear maps $A,B$ between inner-product spaces is $\langle A,B\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}} = \operatorname{tr}(A^{*}B)$, with norm $\|A\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2 = \operatorname{tr}(A^*A)$ — the entrywise dot product of matrices, coordinate-free.

  • Nonnegativity: $\operatorname{tr}(P_xP_y) = \operatorname{tr}(P_xP_x P_yP_y) = \operatorname{tr}\bigl((P_xP_y)^*(P_xP_y)\bigr) = \|P_xP_y\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2 \ge 0$, using $P^2=P$, $P^*=P$, and cyclicity of trace. An overlap of projections can never go negative — already a nontrivial constraint that a general Gram kernel does not satisfy.
  • Positive-definiteness: $K(x,y) = \langle P_x, P_y\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}$ is a Gram kernel of the operators $P_x$ themselves, hence psd in the sense of §2.1.
  • Geometric meaning: if the two subspaces meet at principal angles $\theta_1,\dots,\theta_d$ (the singular values of $P_xP_y$ restricted to the subspaces are $\cos\theta_i$), then $\operatorname{tr}(P_xP_y) = \sum_{i=1}^d \cos^2\theta_i$. Full alignment gives $d$; orthogonality gives $0$.

3.2 The projection bound

Here is the abstract theorem. Its cast: a set $X$ of candidate points; a Euclidean space $W$ that encodes closeness, via a unit vector $\ell_x \in W$ for each point with $t(x,y) = \langle \ell_x,\ell_y\rangle$ (on the sphere $W=\mathbb{R}^n$ and $\ell_x = x$ itself; on the cube $W = \mathbb{R}^n$ and $\ell_x = x/\sqrt n$ in $\pm1$ coordinates); the ambient space $\mathcal{V}$ with its moving projections; and one operator $B$ that ties them together. $B$ is where all the future work hides: §§4–5, 7, and 12 exist to build it in each universe.

Proposition 4.1 (projection bound). Let $X$ be a set, and associate with every $x\in X$ a unit vector $\ell_x$ in a Euclidean space $W$; write $t(x,y) = \langle\ell_x,\ell_y\rangle$. Let $\mathcal{V}$ be a $D$-dimensional Hilbert space and, for every $x$, let $P_x$ be a rank-$d$ orthogonal projection on $\mathcal{V}$, where $1\le d\le D$. Suppose there exist a constant $\Lambda \ge 0$ and an isometry $B:\mathcal{V}\to W\otimes\mathcal{V}$ such that for every $x\in X$ and every $u \in \operatorname{im}P_x$, $$B^{*}(\ell_x\otimes u) \;=\; \sqrt{\Lambda}\,u. \tag{49}$$ If $\Lambda \gt s$, then every code $C\subseteq X$ with $t(x,y)\le s$ at distinct points satisfies $$|C| \;\le\; \frac{1-s}{\Lambda-s}\cdot\frac{D}{d}. \tag{50}$$

Read condition (49) as a spectral alignment demand: tensoring a moving-subspace vector with its own point's coordinate vector, then contracting back through $B$, must reproduce the vector, shrunk by the fixed factor $\sqrt\Lambda$ — the same factor for every point and every vector in its subspace. The constant $\Lambda$ will turn out to be the largest eigenvalue of a weighted graph (§9), which is why we keep calling it the spectral gatekeeper: the bound exists only when $\Lambda$ clears the closeness ceiling $s$.

It suffices to bound finite codes: an infinite code contains finite subcodes of arbitrary size. Write $K(x,y)=\operatorname{tr}(P_xP_y) \ge 0$ (shown above). Define, for each $x$, two maps $\mathcal{V}\to W\otimes\mathcal{V}$ and their residual: $$L_x = (\ell_x\otimes\mathrm{id})P_x, \qquad G_x = BP_x, \qquad \Theta_x = L_x - \sqrt{\Lambda}\,G_x.$$ ($L_x$ sends $v \mapsto \ell_x \otimes P_x v$.) We compute all four Hilbert–Schmidt pairings between the $L$'s and $G$'s at two points $x,y$.

Pairing 1. $\langle L_x,L_y\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}} = \operatorname{tr}\bigl[P_x(\ell_x\otimes\mathrm{id})^{*}(\ell_y\otimes\mathrm{id})P_y\bigr]$. Since $(\ell_x\otimes\mathrm{id})^{*}(\ell_y\otimes\mathrm{id}) = \langle\ell_x,\ell_y\rangle\,\mathrm{id} = t(x,y)\,\mathrm{id}$, this is $t(x,y)K(x,y)$.

Pairing 2. $\langle G_x,G_y\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}} = \operatorname{tr}(P_xB^{*}BP_y) = \operatorname{tr}(P_xP_y) = K(x,y)$, because $B$ is an isometry, $B^*B=\mathrm{id}_{\mathcal V}$.

Pairings 3–4. Condition (49) says $B^{*}(\ell_x\otimes\mathrm{id})P_x = \sqrt\Lambda\,P_x$ (apply it to each vector of $\operatorname{im}P_x$). Hence $$\langle G_x, L_y\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}} = \operatorname{tr}\bigl[P_xB^{*}(\ell_y\otimes\mathrm{id})P_y\bigr] = \sqrt\Lambda\operatorname{tr}(P_xP_y) = \sqrt\Lambda\,K(x,y),$$ and the adjoint of the same identity at $x$ gives $\langle L_x,G_y\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}} = \sqrt\Lambda\,K(x,y)$ as well.

Expanding $\langle\Theta_x,\Theta_y\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}} = \langle L_x,L_y\rangle - \sqrt\Lambda\langle L_x,G_y\rangle - \sqrt\Lambda\langle G_x,L_y\rangle + \Lambda\langle G_x,G_y\rangle$ with these four values: $$\langle\Theta_x,\Theta_y\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}} \;=\; \bigl(t(x,y)-\Lambda\bigr)\,K(x,y). \tag{51}$$ This is the pivot of the whole chapter: the closeness function, shifted by the spectral constant, times the overlap kernel, is itself a Gram kernel (of the residuals $\Theta$). Now sum $(51)$ over all ordered pairs of the code $C$, with $N_C = |C|$: $$\sum_{x,y\in C}\bigl(t(x,y)-\Lambda\bigr)K(x,y) \;=\; \Bigl\|\sum_{x\in C}\Theta_x\Bigr\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2 \;\ge\; 0 .$$ Split the left side by the code property. Off-diagonal pairs have $t\le s$ and $K\ge0$, so $(t-\Lambda)K \le (s-\Lambda)K$ there; diagonal pairs have $t=1$, $K(x,x) = \operatorname{tr}P_x = d$. Therefore $$0 \;\le\; N_C\,(1-\Lambda)\,d + (s-\Lambda)\sum_{x\ne y}K(x,y),$$ and adding and subtracting the diagonal of the full double sum $\Sigma := \sum_{x,y\in C}K(x,y) = \bigl\|\sum_x P_x\bigr\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2$ gives $$(\Lambda - s)\,\Bigl\|\sum_{x\in C}P_x\Bigr\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2 \;\le\; N_C\,(1-s)\,d. \tag{3.a}$$ Finally, trace Cauchy–Schwarz on the $D$-dimensional space: for any self-adjoint operator $A$ on $\mathcal V$, $(\operatorname{tr}A)^2 = \langle \mathrm{id}, A\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}^2 \le \|\mathrm{id}\|^2_{\mathrm{HS}}\|A\|^2_{\mathrm{HS}} = D\,\|A\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2$. With $A = \sum_x P_x$, whose trace is $N_C\,d$: $$\Bigl\|\sum_{x\in C}P_x\Bigr\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2 \;\ge\; \frac{(N_C\,d)^2}{D}. \tag{3.b}$$ Chaining (3.a) and (3.b): $(\Lambda-s)N_C^2d^2/D \le N_C(1-s)d$, which is (50). ∎

Sanity checks on the bound $(50)$. (i) With $d=1$, $P_x = u_xu_x^{*}$ is a pinned unit vector and $(50)$ reads $|C| \le \frac{1-s}{\Lambda-s}D$: the classical spectral bound of §2.3, recovered exactly. (ii) The bound improves as $d$ grows with $D$ fixed — but condition (49) gets harder to satisfy: the isometry must $\sqrt\Lambda$-align an ever larger moving family. The constructions ahead balance these forces by taking $\log_2 D \approx H(\text{ambient})\,n$ and $\log_2 d \approx H(\text{subspace})\,n$ with both entropies tuned so that the spectral gatekeeper $\Lambda$ just clears $s$. (iii) The prefactor $\frac{1-s}{\Lambda-s}$ is constant in $n$ as long as $\Lambda - s$ stays bounded away from $0$; it never affects exponents.
Analogy anchor (and where it breaks). If the classical construction is “a function on the symmetric space” (one number per point per symmetry type), the new one is “a section of a vector bundle”: each point carries an internal $d$-dimensional fiber, and the symmetry group acts on the total package. The extra internal dimensions are exactly what the classical certificates could not see, and the exponent gain $\log_2 d$ is the fiber entropy. Break-point of the analogy: the certificate still compresses each pair of points to a single scalar $\operatorname{tr}(P_xP_y)$ — two-point information only. It is not a higher-order (three-point, SDP-hierarchy) method; its feasible region is still Delsarte's. The gain comes from a richer construction inside the same two-point LP, not from a stronger LP.
