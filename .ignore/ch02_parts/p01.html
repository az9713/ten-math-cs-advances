1. Codes and rates made precise, and the two headline theorems

This section defines the players exactly and states what the chapter proves. Everything later hangs off these definitions.

1.1 The binary side

Definition (binary code, Hamming distance, $A_2(n,d)$). A binary code is a subset $C \subseteq \{0,1\}^n$; its elements are codewords. The Hamming distance $d_H(x,y)$ between two words is the number of coordinate positions in which they differ. The minimum distance of $C$ is the smallest $d_H(x,y)$ over distinct $x,y \in C$. Finally, $A_2(n,d)$ denotes the largest possible size $|C|$ of a code of length $n$ with minimum distance at least $d$.

Exact values of $A_2(n,d)$ are hopeless in general (already $A_2(20,8)$ is unknown). The tractable and structurally meaningful question is the growth exponent when the distance scales with the length. For fixed relative distance $0 \lt \delta \lt 1/2$, define the asymptotic rate

$$R_2(\delta) \;=\; \limsup_{n\to\infty}\; \frac1n \log_2 A_2\bigl(n, \lceil \delta n\rceil\bigr).$$

Why stop at $\delta \lt 1/2$? Because beyond it the problem collapses: a classical argument (Plotkin's bound) shows codes with $\delta \gt 1/2$ have at most linearly many words, so the exponent is $0$. All the action is in $0 \lt \delta \lt 1/2$, where $R_2(\delta)$ is strictly between $0$ and $1$ and its exact value is unknown at every single $\delta$.

Two classical functions calibrate everything. The binary entropy $H_2$ and an auxiliary function $g$ (with the convention $0\log_2 0 = 0$) are

$$H_2(u) = -u\log_2 u - (1-u)\log_2(1-u), \qquad g(v) = H_2\!\left(\frac{1-\sqrt{1-v}}{2}\right).$$

$H_2(u)$ is the exponent of binomial counting: the number of binary words of length $n$ with exactly $\lfloor un \rfloor$ ones is $2^{(H_2(u)+o(1))n}$ — a fact we prove and reuse constantly as Lemma B in §4. The best known lower bound on the rate is elementary (greedily pick any word at distance $\ge d$ from all previous picks, and count how much volume each pick excludes); it gives the Gilbert–Varshamov bound $R_2(\delta) \ge 1 - H_2(\delta)$, essentially unimproved since 1952. The upper bounds are where this chapter lives. The first MRRW bound (1977), often called the first linear-programming bound, is

$$M_1(\delta) = H_2\!\left(\tfrac12 - \sqrt{\delta(1-\delta)}\,\right). \tag{1}$$

The second MRRW bound is a strengthening obtained by optimizing the same method over constant-weight sublayers of the cube (we rebuild this mechanism in §7). It is packaged as a one-parameter variational problem: for $0\le\tau\le 1-2\delta$ set

$$F_\delta(\tau) = 1 + g(\tau^2) - g(\tau^2+2\delta\tau+2\delta), \qquad M_2(\delta) = \min_{0\le\tau\le 1-2\delta} F_\delta(\tau). \tag{3}$$

The parameter $\tau$ reparametrizes the degree of the certificate polynomial; its endpoints recover $F_\delta(1-2\delta) = M_1(\delta)$ and $F_\delta(0) = 1-g(2\delta)$, so $M_2(\delta) \le M_1(\delta)$ always, and for small $\delta$ the minimum sits strictly inside the interval (Fig. 3, right panel). Both facts matter for the proof strategy later: beating $M_2$ requires beating the interior minimizers as well as the endpoint, and those are different jobs done by two different constructions.

Theorem 1.1 (binary improvement). For every fixed $0\lt\delta\lt 1/2$, $$R_2(\delta) \;\le\; \kappa_{\mathrm{bin}}(\delta) \;\lt\; M_2(\delta),$$ where $\kappa_{\mathrm{bin}}(\delta) = \min\{\kappa_{\mathrm{H}}(\delta),\, \kappa_{\mathrm{CW}}(\delta)\}$ is the minimum of two new certificate exponents: the whole-cube exponent $\kappa_{\mathrm{H}}$ (defined in equation (2) of §6, proved there to strictly beat $M_1$) and the constant-weight exponent $\kappa_{\mathrm{CW}}$ (defined in equation (8) of §8, proved there to strictly beat every interior minimizer of $F_\delta$).

This is the first improvement of the general binary exponent bound since 1977. The gap $M_2 - \kappa_{\mathrm{bin}}$ is small — of order $10^{-4}$ to $10^{-2}$ depending on $\delta$, computed honestly in Figs. 17 and 21 — but it is strictly positive at every $\delta$, and the barrier it breaks had resisted every general-purpose technique for 47 years.

1.2 The spherical side

Definition (spherical code, $A(n,s)$). A spherical code with maximum inner product $s$ is a subset $C \subseteq S^{n-1} = \{x\in\mathbb{R}^n : \|x\|=1\}$ such that $\langle x,y\rangle \le s$ for all distinct $x,y \in C$. Let $A(n,s)$ denote the largest possible $|C|$. Its rate is $$R_{\mathrm{sph}}(s) = \limsup_{n\to\infty} \frac1n\log_2 A(n,s).$$

Geometrically, $\langle x,y\rangle \le s$ says the angle between any two code directions is at least $\arccos s$; each point owns a private angular cap, and $A(n,s)$ counts how many disjoint caps fit. For $s = 1/2$, $A(n,1/2)$ is exactly the kissing number $\tau_n$ of $n$-dimensional space. The role of binary entropy is played by the spherical harmonic dimension exponent

$$H_{\mathrm{sph}}(u) = (1+u)\log_2(1+u) - u\log_2 u, \qquad H_{\mathrm{sph}}(0)=0, \tag{9}$$

which is the growth exponent of spaces of degree-$un$ spherical harmonics, exactly as $H_2$ is the growth exponent of binomial coefficients (we prove this in §10; the exact dimension formula is (59)). Note one structural difference that echoes through the whole spherical half: $u$ ranges over $[0,\infty)$, not $[0,1]$ — polynomial degree on the sphere has no ceiling, while Fourier degree on the cube stops at $n$.

The classical record has two layers. Applying the 1978 Kabatianskii–Levenshtein method directly on the whole sphere gives $A(n,s) \le 2^{(H_{\mathrm{sph}}(a_0(s))+o(1))n}$ with $a_0(s) = \tfrac12\bigl((1-s^2)^{-1/2}-1\bigr)$. A preliminary reduction strengthens it: first restrict the code to a well-populated spherical cap and project the cap onto a lower-dimensional sphere. This spherical-cap reduction (Sidelnikov's inequality, imported and explained in §11.4) trades the threshold $s$ for any smaller $t \le s$ at an explicit exponential price, giving the optimized classical exponent

$$B_{\mathrm{KL}}(s) = \inf_{0\le t\le s}\left\{ H_{\mathrm{sph}}(a_0(t)) + \tfrac12\log_2\frac{1-t}{1-s} \right\}. \tag{10}$$

Then $R_{\mathrm{sph}}(s) \le B_{\mathrm{KL}}(s)$, and this has been the record at every fixed $0\lt s\lt 1$ since 1978. At the kissing threshold: $B_{\mathrm{KL}}(1/2) = 0.4009442\ldots$ (our computation in §18, check 14, reproduces this to seven digits).

Theorem 1.2 + Corollary 1.3 (spherical improvement). There is an infinite hierarchy of new exponents $\kappa_r(s)$, indexed by a depth $r = 0,1,2,\ldots$ (the number of “rows” of the moving subspace, made precise in §12), with cap-optimized versions $\overline{\kappa}_r(s)$, such that for every $0 \lt s\lt 1$: $$R_{\mathrm{sph}}(s) \;\le\; \inf_{r\ge 0}\overline{\kappa}_r(s), \qquad \overline{\kappa}_{r+1}(s) \lt \overline{\kappa}_r(s) \;\text{ for every } r, \qquad \overline{\kappa}_0(s) = B_{\mathrm{KL}}(s).$$ Level $0$ is exactly the classical bound; every additional level strictly improves it. Already the intermediate “one-row” construction of §11 beats $B_{\mathrm{KL}}$ at every $s$, and at the kissing threshold level $2$ gives $\tau_n \le 2^{(0.39661+o(1))n}$ versus the classical $2^{(0.400944+o(1))n}$.
Corollary 1.4 (sphere packing). Let $\Delta_n$ be the maximal sphere packing density in $\mathbb{R}^n$. Then $$\Delta_n \;\le\; 2^{-(\lambda^*+o(1))n}, \qquad \lambda^* = \tfrac12\log_2\frac{2\pi}{e} = 0.6044005\ldots$$ Equivalently $\Delta_n^{1/n} \to \sqrt{e/2\pi} = 0.65774\ldots$ as an upper bound — the exact optimal rate of the Cohn–Elkies linear program determined independently in Chapter 1. This improves the classical Kabatianskii–Levenshtein packing exponent and settles, from the spherical side, the conjectured LP rate.
