2. The Delsarte two-point certificate (the LP spine, second pass)

Chapter 1 of this series was built on one principle: a single auxiliary function with the right sign properties bounds every packing at once, because summing the function over all pairs of a configuration counts the same quantity two ways. Delsarte's method is the same principle on a compact space, and it is the trunk from which everything in this chapter grows. We state and prove it in the kernel form the chapter actually uses — no orthogonal-polynomial formulas required.

2.1 Positive-definite kernels and the two-point bound

Definition (positive-definite kernel). Let $X$ be a set. A symmetric function $F : X\times X \to \mathbb{R}$ is a positive-definite kernel (we write psd) if for every finite subset $\{x_1,\dots,x_m\}\subseteq X$ and all reals $c_1,\dots,c_m$, $$\sum_{i=1}^m\sum_{j=1}^m c_i c_j\, F(x_i,x_j) \;\ge\; 0.$$ Equivalently: every finite matrix $[F(x_i,x_j)]_{ij}$ sampled from $F$ is positive semidefinite.

The only way we will ever produce psd kernels is the only way anyone should: as Gram kernels. If $x \mapsto T_x$ assigns to each point a vector (or an operator) in some inner-product space, then $F(x,y) = \langle T_x, T_y\rangle$ is psd, because the double sum above becomes $\|\sum_i c_i T_{x_i}\|^2 \ge 0$. Conversely a sum of psd kernels is psd. That is the complete toolkit.

Lemma A (two-point LP bound; Delsarte's principle). Let $X$ carry a symmetric “closeness” function $t: X\times X\to\mathbb{R}$ with $t(x,x)=t_{\max}$ for all $x$, and let a threshold $s \lt t_{\max}$ be given. Call $C\subseteq X$ a code if $t(x,y)\le s$ for all distinct $x,y\in C$. Suppose a kernel $F$ satisfies:
  1. $F = f_0 + G$ where $f_0 \gt 0$ is a constant and $G$ is psd;
  2. $F(x,y) \le 0$ whenever $t(x,y) \le s$;
  3. $F(x,x) \le F_{\mathrm{diag}}$ for all $x$.
Then every finite code satisfies $\;|C| \le F_{\mathrm{diag}}/f_0$.
Sum $F$ over all ordered pairs of the code, and bound the sum from both sides. From below, using (1) and psd-ness of $G$ with all $c_i = 1$: $$\sum_{x\in C}\sum_{y\in C} F(x,y) \;=\; f_0\,|C|^2 + \sum_{x,y} G(x,y) \;\ge\; f_0\,|C|^2 .$$ From above, split the sum into the diagonal and the rest. Every off-diagonal pair of a code has $t(x,y)\le s$, so (2) kills those terms, and (3) bounds the diagonal: $$\sum_{x\in C}\sum_{y\in C} F(x,y) \;\le\; \sum_{x\in C} F(x,x) \;\le\; |C|\, F_{\mathrm{diag}}.$$ Chaining the two displays gives $f_0 |C|^2 \le |C| F_{\mathrm{diag}}$, i.e. $|C| \le F_{\mathrm{diag}}/f_0$. ∎

That is the entire logical content of linear-programming bounds. Every named bound in this chapter — MRRW, KL, and all the new ones — is Lemma A applied to a cleverly built $F$. The craft is entirely in the construction: you want $F$ simultaneously very negative-leaning on the allowed region (condition 2), yet with a large guaranteed constant component $f_0$ relative to its diagonal (conditions 1 and 3). Those pull in opposite directions, and the tension is the whole subject.

Where the classical expansions went. Delsarte's original formulation expands $F(x,y) = \sum_j f_j P_j(t(x,y))$ in the orthogonal-polynomial family adapted to the symmetry of $X$ (Krawtchouk polynomials on the cube, Hahn on a weight layer, Gegenbauer on the sphere) and requires $f_j\ge 0$ for $j\ge1$ and $f_0\gt0$; positivity of each $P_j(t(\cdot,\cdot))$ as a kernel then makes $G = \sum_{j\ge1} f_j P_j$ psd. This chapter never needs the polynomial formulas: it builds $G$ directly as a Gram kernel of explicit operators (“projection Gram factorizations”), which is both simpler and strictly more flexible. When we do meet the polynomial families (Krawtchouk in §4, Hahn in §7, Gegenbauer in §10) it will be through their three-term recurrences, which encode how coordinate multiplication connects neighboring symmetry types — the only property the constructions use.

2.2 A complete certificate, by hand

Toy 2.1 (a tight Delsarte certificate for $n=3$, $d=2$). Take binary words of length $3$ with minimum distance $2$, and encode closeness by the normalized inner product $t(x,y) = 1 - 2d_H(x,y)/3 \in \{1, \tfrac13, -\tfrac13, -1\}$. Distance $\ge 2$ means $t \le -\tfrac13$, so the allowed set is $\{-\tfrac13, -1\}$ and the threshold is $s=-\tfrac13$. Claim: $A_2(3,2) = 4$, witnessed on both sides.

The certificate. Take $$F(t) \;=\; \tfrac12 (1+t)(1+3t) \;=\; 1 \;+\; \tfrac23\,\bigl(3t\bigr) \;+\; \tfrac13\,\underbrace{\tfrac{9t^2-3}{2}}_{K_2(t)} .$$ The two factors vanish exactly at the two allowed values, so $F(-1) = F(-\tfrac13) = 0 \le 0$: condition (2) holds with nothing to spare. The displayed expansion has components $1$, $3t$, and $K_2(t) = (9t^2-3)/2$: these are the Krawtchouk kernels for $n=3$, and each is a Gram kernel — $3t(x,y) = \sum_{r=1}^{3}\chi_r(x)\chi_r(y)$ summing the three coordinate sign characters $\chi_r(x)=(-1)^{x_r}$, and $K_2(t(x,y)) = \sum_{r\lt r'}\chi_r\chi_{r'}(x)\,\chi_r\chi_{r'}(y)$ likewise (a direct check: at $d_H = 0,1,2,3$ the pair-character sum gives $3,-1,-1,3$, matching $K_2$ at $t=1,\frac13,-\frac13,-1$). So $G = \tfrac23(3t)+\tfrac13 K_2$ is psd and $f_0 = 1$: condition (1) holds. The diagonal is $F(1) = \tfrac12\cdot2\cdot4 = 4$. Lemma A: $A_2(3,2) \le 4/1 = 4$.

The matching code. The even-weight code $\{000, 011, 101, 110\}$ has all pairwise distances exactly $2$, so $A_2(3,2) = 4$ exactly, and this tiny LP is tight. Every ingredient of the giant constructions ahead — sign conditions checked on an allowed set, positivity via explicit Gram vectors, the bound as a diagonal-to-constant ratio — is already present here.

2.3 The classical construction, in one paragraph, and its ceiling

Where do good certificates come from in high dimension? The classical spectral recipe (Delsarte, MRRW, KL; formulated spectrally by Barg–Nogin) attaches to each point $x\in X$ a single unit vector per symmetry type: on the cube, the normalized level-$i$ Fourier average $v_{i,x}$ pinned at $x$ (§4 constructs it); on the sphere, the normalized degree-$i$ zonal harmonic centered at $x$ (§10). Any weighted combination $u_x = \sum_i c_i v_{i,x}$ then yields the psd Gram kernel $K(x,y) = \langle u_x, u_y\rangle$, and one arranges the weights $c_i$ (via the Perron eigenvector of a certain tridiagonal matrix, as we will do explicitly in §5) so that $(t(x,y)-s)\,K(x,y)$ is itself a psd-plus-constant kernel. Lemma A applied to $F=(t-s)K$ then delivers the MRRW and KL exponents.

The ceiling of this recipe is built into its rank. Each symmetry type contributes a one-dimensional pin per point, so the diagonal $F(1)$ and the constant $f_0$ are tied together in a fixed ratio governed by the total dimension $D$ of the retained function space, and the exponent that comes out is the entropy of $D$ — full stop. Wanting a better exponent, you have exactly one degree of freedom left to find: make the object attached to each point bigger without breaking positivity or symmetry. That is precisely what the chapter does, and it is the subject of the next section.
