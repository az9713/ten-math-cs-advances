0. Motivation — two record bounds, frozen since 1977 and 1978

Here are two packing problems that run modern communication.

Problem one (binary). You must transmit messages through a channel that flips bits. Your defense is redundancy: instead of using all $2^n$ binary strings of length $n$, you agree in advance on a small dictionary of strings — a code — chosen so that any two dictionary entries differ in many positions. If every pair of codewords differs in at least $d$ positions, then even after $\lfloor (d-1)/2\rfloor$ bit flips the received string is still closer to the intended codeword than to any other, and the receiver can decode by choosing the nearest entry. The engineering question is a trade-off: how many codewords can you have at a given separation $d$? More codewords means a faster channel; more separation means a tougher channel.

Problem two (spherical). Replace bit strings by directions: unit vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$. Two directions are “far apart” when the angle between them is large — equivalently, when their inner product is small. Fix a ceiling $s$ and demand that every pair of chosen points on the unit sphere $S^{n-1}$ has inner product at most $s$. How many points fit? For $s = 1/2$ this is the kissing number problem (how many unit balls can touch one central unit ball), and for general $s$ it is the natural continuum analogue of problem one — and, as Chapter 1 of this series showed, a bound for spherical codes converts directly into a bound for sphere packing density in $\mathbb{R}^n$.

In both problems the interesting regime is $n$ large with the separation demand scaled proportionally: Hamming distance $d = \delta n$ for a fixed relative distance $0 \lt \delta \lt 1/2$, or a fixed inner-product ceiling $0 \lt s \lt 1$. The maximum code size then grows like $2^{Rn}$ for some exponent $R$ depending on $\delta$ or $s$, and the entire subject condenses into one question: what is the exponent? Nobody knows, in either universe. What exists is a gap: constructions give a lower bound on the best exponent, and impossibility proofs give an upper bound. The lower bounds have barely moved since Shannon's era. The upper bounds both date from a two-year burst driven by a single method — Delsarte's linear programming — and then froze:

  • 1977, binary: McEliece, Rodemich, Rumsey, and Welch (MRRW) published two LP-based bounds; the stronger, “optimized” form $M_2(\delta)$ has been the record for unrestricted binary codes at every fixed $\delta$ ever since.
  • 1978, spherical: Kabatianskii and Levenshtein (KL) published the spherical analogue; its cap-optimized form $B_{\mathrm{KL}}(s)$ has been the record for every fixed $s$ ever since.

This freeze was not for lack of attention. Semidefinite-programming hierarchies strengthened the method in fixed small dimensions (they are how the kissing number in dimension $4$ was settled); restricted families like linear codes saw improvements; dimensions $8$ and $24$ became exactly solvable through Viazovska's magic functions. But the general exponent, the headline number for both problems, did not move for either universe — and a line of “limitation” results even proved that several natural strengthenings of the classical certificates cannot move it. The suspicion grew that the 1977–78 records might be the true answers.

They are not. The chapter we walk through constructs new certificates that strictly beat the optimized MRRW exponent at every fixed relative distance $0 \lt \delta \lt 1/2$, and the optimized KL exponent at every fixed inner product $0 \lt s \lt 1$. The improvements are quantitatively small (that is part of the story — §6 and §8 compute them), but they are conceptually decisive: the classical bounds are not the truth, and the mechanism that beats them is new.

The mechanism can be stated in one sentence. Classical LP certificates attach to each code point a single vector in a symmetry-adapted function space, and bound the code through pairwise inner products of those vectors; the new certificates attach to each code point an entire subspace — exponentially large, and moving with the point under the symmetry group — and bound the code through overlaps of the corresponding orthogonal projections. One extra dimension knob appears (the dimension of the moving subspace), it enters the final bound as a straight division, and everything else in the LP framework survives untouched. Section 3 isolates this one idea in a six-line theorem; the rest of the chapter is the craft of making specific subspaces move (Boolean harmonics on the cube, tangential spherical harmonics and their multi-row generalizations on the sphere) and computing the spectral quantity that decides whether the certificate is feasible.

There is a second punchline, and it ties this explainer to the previous one. Pushing the spherical hierarchy to its limit ($s \to 1$, hierarchy depth $\to \infty$) yields a sphere-packing density bound in $\mathbb{R}^n$ of exactly $2^{-(\lambda^*+o(1))n}$ with $\lambda^* = \tfrac12\log_2\tfrac{2\pi}{e}$ — the same constant $\sqrt{e/2\pi} = 0.65774\ldots$ that Chapter 1 proved is the exact limit rate of the Cohn–Elkies linear program, by an entirely different, Euclidean-side argument. Two independent proofs, one working on spheres with representation theory, one working in $\mathbb{R}^n$ with Fourier analysis, meet at the same eight decimal places. §15–§16 prove the spherical side and build the dictionary between the two.
