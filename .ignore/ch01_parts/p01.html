1. Sphere packing made precise, and the two theorems

1.1 What exactly is being maximized

Fix the dimension $d \ge 1$. A packing is a set $\mathcal{P} \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ of centers whose pairwise distances are all at least $1$; around each center we place a ball of radius $\tfrac12$, and the separation condition says exactly that these balls have disjoint interiors. "Fraction of space filled" needs care, because packings are infinite: we measure the fraction inside a large box and let the box grow (Fig. 3). For $R \gt 0$ let $Q_R = [-R,R]^d$ be the cube of side $2R$ and $\operatorname{vol}$ the Lebesgue measure ("volume"). The upper density of the packing $\mathcal{P}$ is

$$\bar\delta(\mathcal{P}) \;=\; \limsup_{R \to \infty} \frac{\operatorname{vol}\!\Bigl(Q_R \cap \bigcup_{x \in \mathcal{P}} B\bigl(x,\tfrac12\bigr)\Bigr)}{\operatorname{vol}(Q_R)},$$

where $B(x,\rho)$ is the ball of radius $\rho$ centered at $x$. The $\limsup$ (the largest limit point) is forced on us: a packing can leave huge empty stadiums at some scales and be crowded at others, so the plain limit may not exist. Finally,

$$\Delta_d \;=\; \sup_{\mathcal{P} \text{ packing}} \bar\delta(\mathcal{P})$$

is the sphere-packing density of dimension $d$ — the number the whole subject is about.

Two dimensions are easy to state: $\Delta_1 = 1$ (unit intervals tile the line) and $\Delta_2 = \pi/\sqrt{12}$ (the honeycomb of Fig. 1; already a hard theorem, due to Thue and Fejes Tóth). Beyond $d = 3$, $8$, $24$, no exact value is known in any dimension.

We will constantly need the volume of a ball. Here the gamma function enters for the first time, so we define it: for $\operatorname{Re} z \gt 0$,

$$\Gamma(z) \;=\; \int_0^\infty t^{z-1} e^{-t}\, dt,$$

the smooth interpolation of the factorial: $\Gamma(n+1) = n!$ for integers $n \ge 0$, and $\Gamma(\tfrac12) = \sqrt{\pi}$ (both proved, along with everything else we need about $\Gamma$, in the workshop of §6). In terms of it, the volume of the unit ball is

Proposition A (ball volume). $\;\operatorname{vol}(B(0,1)) = v_d = \dfrac{\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2+1)}$, so a packing ball of radius $\tfrac12$ has volume $v_d/2^d$. (Proved in §5.1 once polar integration is set up; sanity checks: $v_2 = \pi$, $v_3 = \tfrac43\pi$.)

The formula contains the first high-dimensional shock: $v_d/2^d \to 0$ super-exponentially fast (Fig. 4), because $\Gamma(d/2+1)$ grows like $(d/2e)^{d/2}$. A radius-$\tfrac12$ ball in $\mathbb{R}^{24}$ has volume $1.9\times 10^{-10}$; in $\mathbb{R}^{64}$, about $10^{-40}$. Density statements in high dimension are therefore inherently statements about exponents, which motivates measuring everything on the scale $\Delta_d^{1/d}$.

How large is $\Delta_d$? One direction is nearly free, and worth proving immediately because its "doubling" trick reappears at the heart of the chapter (§4).

Proposition B (easy lower bound). For every $d \ge 1$, $\;\Delta_d \ge 2^{-d}$.

Call a packing $\mathcal{P}$ saturated if no point can be added to it — every $y \in \mathbb{R}^d$ lies within distance $1$ of some center (else $y$ could join $\mathcal{P}$). Saturated packings exist by Zorn's lemma (any chain of packings, ordered by inclusion, has its union as an upper bound; the separation condition survives unions of chains). Fix one. Then the balls $B(x,1)$, $x \in \mathcal{P}$, cover $\mathbb{R}^d$. Inside the window $Q_R$, every point is covered by balls of radius $1$ centered in $Q_{R+1}$, so, writing $N_R = |\mathcal{P} \cap Q_{R+1}|$ (finite, since centers are $1$-separated), $$\operatorname{vol}(Q_R) \;\le\; N_R \cdot v_d.$$ The disjoint radius-$\tfrac12$ balls around those same $N_R$ centers occupy volume $N_R\, v_d/2^d$ inside $Q_{R+2}$, so the covered fraction of $Q_{R+2}$ is at least $$\frac{N_R\, v_d / 2^d}{\operatorname{vol}(Q_{R+2})} \;\ge\; 2^{-d}\,\frac{\operatorname{vol}(Q_R)}{\operatorname{vol}(Q_{R+2})} \;=\; 2^{-d}\Bigl(\frac{R}{R+2}\Bigr)^{d} \;\longrightarrow\; 2^{-d}.$$ Hence $\bar\delta(\mathcal{P}) \ge 2^{-d}$. ∎

Remarkably, a century of effort has improved this greedy bound only by factors polynomial in $d$ (Minkowski, Rogers, Ball, Venkatesh, Campos–Jenssen–Michelen–Sahasrabudhe): the best known packings still have $\Delta_d^{1/d} \to \tfrac12$. Upper bounds are where the action is, and Fig. 5 shows the state of play on the $d$-th-root scale: the truth lives somewhere in the blue interval, and this chapter's contribution is the right endpoint.

1.2 The linear program, and Theorem 1.1

Throughout, the Fourier transform of an integrable $f : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$ uses the convention

$$\widehat{f}(\xi) \;=\; \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f(x)\, e^{-2\pi i x\cdot\xi}\, dx, \tag{1}$$

with $x \cdot \xi$ the usual dot product ($2\pi$ in the exponent keeps all constants clean; §2 develops everything we need from scratch). We write $\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^d;\mathbb{R})$ for the real Schwartz space: infinitely differentiable functions all of whose derivatives decay faster than any power (Definition 2.1 makes this precise). The machine's raw material is the following class of auxiliary functions:

Definition (admissible functions, eq. (2)). $$\mathcal{A}_d \;=\; \Bigl\{\, f \in \mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^d;\mathbb{R}) \;:\; \widehat{f}(0) \gt 0, \quad \widehat{f} \ge 0 \text{ on } \mathbb{R}^d, \quad f \le 0 \text{ on } \{|x| \ge 1\} \,\Bigr\}. \tag{2}$$ Each condition is engineered against a specific failure mode, visible in the proof of the certificate theorem in §3: $f \le 0$ outside radius $1$ makes $f$ "blind" to pairs of distinct centers (their distance is $\ge 1$, so cross terms only help); $\widehat f \ge 0$ makes a certain quadratic form in the packing automatically nonnegative; $\widehat f(0) \gt 0$ (the integral of $f$) normalizes the bound. Fourier inversion forces $f(0) \gt 0$ for such $f$ (shown in §3), so the following ratio is well defined.
Definition (the Cohn–Elkies bound, eq. (3)). $$\mathrm{LP}_d \;=\; \frac{v_d}{2^d}\, \inf_{f \in \mathcal{A}_d} \frac{f(0)}{\widehat{f}(0)}. \tag{3}$$ The name "linear program" is honest: the constraints on $f$ and $\widehat f$ are linear inequalities, and minimizing $f(0)$ at normalized $\widehat f(0)$ is a linear objective — an infinite-dimensional linear program over functions.

The Gorbachev–Cohn–Elkies theorem, proved in full in §3, is the statement that every admissible $f$ certifies a bound on every packing:

$$\Delta_d \;\le\; \mathrm{LP}_d. \tag{4}$$

In dimensions $8$ and $24$ the magic functions of Viazovska et al. make (4) an equality. The chapter's first main theorem determines the machine's exact strength as $d \to \infty$:

Theorem 1.1. As $d \to \infty$, $\quad \mathrm{LP}_d^{1/d} \longrightarrow \sqrt{\dfrac{e}{2\pi}}$.

Feeding Theorem 1.1 into (4) and taking logarithms turns the constant into an exponent: $\Delta_d \le \mathrm{LP}_d = 2^{-(\alpha^* + o(1))d}$ with

$$\alpha^* \;=\; \tfrac12 \log_2\!\frac{2\pi}{e} \;=\; 0.604400544\ldots,$$

improving the Kabatianskii–Levenshtein exponent $0.599055\ldots$ — the first improvement of the general sphere-packing exponent since 1978. (Numbers verified in §13.) Equally important is the other direction hiding in the limit: since the limit is exactly $\sqrt{e/2\pi}$, no admissible function can certify a smaller exponential rate. 1.3 Sign uncertainty, and Theorem 1.2

The second theorem concerns functions that are their own Fourier transform, up to sign. Say $g \in L^1(\mathbb{R}^d;\mathbb{R})$ (real and integrable) is a Fourier eigenfunction with eigenvalue $\varsigma \in \{-1,+1\}$ if $\widehat g = \varsigma g$. For such $g$ the transform $\widehat g = \varsigma g$ is itself in $L^1$, so Fourier inversion (Theorem 2.4 below) supplies a continuous representative of $g$; every pointwise statement about $g$ below refers to that continuous representative. The Gaussian $e^{-\pi|x|^2}$ is the model eigenfunction with $\varsigma = +1$; eigenfunctions with $\varsigma = -1$ exist too, e.g. $\bigl(|x|^2 - \tfrac{d}{4\pi}\bigr)e^{-\pi|x|^2}$ (both verified in §2.4).

Definition (last-sign radius and uncertainty constants, eqs. (5)–(6)). For $g$ as above, $$r(g) \;=\; \inf\{\, R \ge 0 \;:\; g(x) \ge 0 \text{ for all } |x| \ge R \,\}, \tag{5}$$ with $r(g) = \infty$ if no such radius exists — the radius after which $g$ is nonnegative for good (Fig. 6). The sign-uncertainty constant of eigenvalue $\varsigma$ is $$A_\varsigma(d) \;=\; \inf\{\, r(g) \;:\; 0 \ne g \in L^1(\mathbb{R}^d;\mathbb{R}), \;\widehat g = \varsigma g, \;g(0) = 0 \,\}. \tag{6}$$

Why is this an "uncertainty principle"? Without the constraint $g(0) = 0$ the question is empty: the Gaussian has $r = 0$. But $g(0) = 0$ together with $\widehat g = \varsigma g$ pulls in two opposite directions. If $\varsigma = +1$, then $\int g = \widehat g(0) = g(0) = 0, $ so $g$ must carry genuinely negative mass; if it is nonnegative outside a small ball, all that negative mass is trapped near the origin — and the Fourier constraint resists such trapping. Bourgain, Clozel, and Kahane introduced $A_+(d)$ and showed $c\sqrt d \le A_+(d) \le C\sqrt d$; Cohn and Gonçalves introduced the $\varsigma = -1$ twin and connected it to sphere packing (that connection is exactly our §4). The physics literature (modular bootstrap) predicted the sharp constant. The chapter's second theorem confirms it:

Theorem 1.2. $\quad\displaystyle \lim_{d\to\infty} \frac{A_+(d)}{\sqrt d} \;=\; \lim_{d\to\infty} \frac{A_-(d)}{\sqrt d} \;=\; \frac{1}{\pi}.$

The two constants share an asymptotic but are never equal: Appendix A proves the strict inequality $A_+(d) \lt A_-(d)$ in every dimension, by an explicit averaging operator that converts a $(-1)$-eigenfunction into a $(+1)$-eigenfunction while strictly shrinking its sign radius.

Orientation. Keep the two theorems mentally linked by the number $\tfrac1\pi$: the sign radius $\bigl(\tfrac1\pi + o(1)\bigr)\sqrt d$ of Theorem 1.2 is the source of the packing rate, via Stirling's formula: $$\text{sign radius } \tfrac{\sqrt d}{\pi} \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{(3)}\ } \; \mathrm{LP}_d \approx \frac{v_d}{2^d}\Bigl(\frac{\sqrt d}{\pi}\Bigr)^{\!d}, \qquad v_d^{1/d}\sqrt d \to \sqrt{2\pi e} \;\Longrightarrow\; \mathrm{LP}_d^{1/d} \to \frac{\sqrt{2\pi e}}{2\pi} = \sqrt{\frac{e}{2\pi}}.$$ §4 makes both arrows precise. Everything in between is the war over that single radius.
