5. The radial reduction

Both halves of the chapter work exclusively with radial functions — functions of $|x|$ alone — because only for radial functions does the Mellin machinery of §6 exist. This section pays for that convenience honestly: polar integration (§5.1), the averaging that reduces the general problems to the radial ones (§5.2), and the mollification that upgrades merely-integrable eigenfunctions to Schwartz ones (§5.3).

5.1 Polar integration, and the ball volume

Write $S^{d-1} = \{\theta \in \mathbb{R}^d : |\theta| = 1\}$ for the unit sphere. Define the normalized spherical measure $\sigma$ of a (Borel) subset $A \subseteq S^{d-1}$ through the cone over it: $$\sigma(A) \;=\; \frac{\operatorname{vol}\bigl(\{\,r\theta : 0 \lt r \le 1,\ \theta \in A\,\}\bigr)}{v_d},$$ a probability measure. Because Lebesgue measure is rotation invariant and cones transform to cones, $\sigma$ is rotation invariant; because cones are symmetric under $\theta \mapsto -\theta$ up to reflection (a rotation composed with a coordinate flip, all measure preserving), $\sigma$ is also symmetric.

Lemma I (polar decomposition of Lebesgue measure). For every integrable $f : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$, $$\int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f(x)\, dx \;=\; S_d \int_0^\infty \Bigl(\int_{S^{d-1}} f(r\theta)\, d\sigma(\theta)\Bigr)\, r^{d-1}\, dr, \qquad S_d = d\, v_d,$$ and $S_d$ is the surface area of $S^{d-1}$. In particular, for radial $f$ (writing $f(r)$ for the profile): $\int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f = S_d\int_0^\infty f(r)r^{d-1}dr$.

Both sides are measures in $f$ (countably additive, agreeing under monotone limits), so it suffices to check them on a generating family: the "annular sectors" $E = \{r\theta : a \lt r \le b,\ \theta \in A\}$, which generate the Borel sets of $\mathbb{R}^d\setminus\{0\}$. Left side: by scaling of Lebesgue measure, $\operatorname{vol}(E) = (b^d - a^d)\,v_d\,\sigma(A)$ (the cone to radius $b$ minus the cone to radius $a$, each a dilate of the unit cone over $A$). Right side: the inner integral is $\sigma(A)$ for $a \lt r \le b$ and $0$ otherwise, so the right side equals $d\,v_d\,\sigma(A)\int_a^b r^{d-1}dr = v_d\,\sigma(A)(b^d - a^d)$. They agree; the standard machine (simple functions, monotone convergence, dominated convergence) extends the identity to all integrable $f$. That $S_d$ is the sphere's area is the case $f = \mathbf{1}_{\{1 \lt |x| \le 1+\epsilon\}}$, $\epsilon \to 0$: $\operatorname{vol} = v_d((1+\epsilon)^d - 1) = S_d\,\epsilon + O(\epsilon^2)$. ∎

Proof of Proposition A (ball volume). Apply Lemma I to the Gaussian and use Proposition 2.3 at $\xi = 0$ (i.e. $\int e^{-\pi|x|^2}dx = 1$): $$1 = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} e^{-\pi|x|^2}dx = S_d\int_0^\infty e^{-\pi r^2}r^{d-1}dr = S_d\cdot\frac{\Gamma(d/2)}{2\pi^{d/2}},$$ where the last integral is the substitution $t = \pi r^2$: $\int_0^\infty e^{-t}(t/\pi)^{d/2-1}\frac{dt}{2\pi} = \Gamma(d/2)/(2\pi^{d/2})$. Hence $$S_d = \frac{2\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2)}, \qquad v_d = \frac{S_d}{d} = \frac{\pi^{d/2}}{(d/2)\Gamma(d/2)} = \frac{\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2+1)},$$ the last step using the recurrence $\Gamma(z+1) = z\Gamma(z)$ (proved as (7a) in §6.2). ∎

5.2 Rotational averaging

The reduction to radial functions is by averaging over spheres (Fig. 13). Using the measure $\sigma$ just built, define the rotational average of a continuous integrable $f : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$ by $$\mathcal Rf(x) \;=\; \int_{S^{d-1}} f\bigl(|x|\,\theta\bigr)\, d\sigma(\theta)$$ — the mean of $f$ over the sphere through $x$. By construction $\mathcal Rf$ is radial, $\mathcal Rf(0) = f(0)$, and $|\mathcal Rf(x)| \le \sup_{|y| = |x|}|f(y)|$, so $\mathcal Rf$ inherits continuity (dominated convergence) and any radial decay bound $f$ satisfies. Lemma I gives $\int \mathcal Rf = \int f$ at once. The two substantive facts are that averaging commutes with the Fourier transform, and that the average of a Schwartz function is Schwartz.

Lemma L (averaging commutes with the transform). For continuous $f$ with $|f(x)| \le C(1+|x|)^{-d-1}$, $$\widehat{\mathcal Rf} \;=\; \mathcal R\widehat f.$$

Define the spherical character integral $K(s) = \int_{S^{d-1}} e^{-2\pi i s\,\omega\cdot e_1}\,d\sigma(\omega)$ for $s \ge 0$, where $e_1$ is the first standard basis vector. Rotation invariance of $\sigma$ gives, for any $\xi \ne 0$ and $r \ge 0$, $$\int_{S^{d-1}} e^{-2\pi i r\,\omega\cdot\xi}\, d\sigma(\omega) \;=\; K\bigl(r|\xi|\bigr)$$ (rotate $\xi/|\xi|$ to $e_1$). Now compute both sides of the claim against this kernel. First, $$\widehat{\mathcal Rf}(\xi) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d}\mathcal Rf(x)\,e^{-2\pi i x\cdot\xi}dx \overset{\text{Lem. I}}{=} S_d\int_0^\infty r^{d-1}\,\mathcal Rf(r e_1)\Bigl(\int_{S^{d-1}} e^{-2\pi i r\omega\cdot\xi}d\sigma(\omega)\Bigr)dr = S_d\int_0^\infty r^{d-1}\varphi(r)\,K(r|\xi|)\,dr,$$ where $\varphi(r) = \int f(r\theta)d\sigma(\theta)$ is the profile of $\mathcal Rf$; we used that $\mathcal Rf(x)$ depends only on $r = |x|$ to pull it out of the $\omega$-average. Second, $$\mathcal R\widehat f(\xi) = \int_{S^{d-1}}\widehat f\bigl(|\xi|\theta\bigr)d\sigma(\theta) = \int_{S^{d-1}}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f(x)\,e^{-2\pi i |\xi|\,x\cdot\theta}\,dx\,d\sigma(\theta) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f(x)\,K\bigl(|x||\xi|\bigr)\,dx,$$ by Fubini (dominant $|f(x)|$, integrable) and the kernel identity with the roles of the two vectors exchanged ($x\cdot\theta$ is symmetric). Applying Lemma I once more to the radial-kernel integral: $$\mathcal R\widehat f(\xi) = S_d\int_0^\infty r^{d-1}\Bigl(\int_{S^{d-1}} f(r\theta)d\sigma(\theta)\Bigr) K(r|\xi|)\,dr = S_d\int_0^\infty r^{d-1}\varphi(r)K(r|\xi|)\,dr.$$ The two expressions are identical. (At $\xi = 0$ both sides are $\int f$.) ∎

Lemma M (the average of a Schwartz function is Schwartz). If $f \in \mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^d;\mathbb{R})$ then $\mathcal Rf \in \mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{rad}}(\mathbb{R}^d;\mathbb{R})$.

The profile $\varphi(r) = \int_{S^{d-1}} f(r\theta)\,d\sigma(\theta)$ makes sense for all $r \in \mathbb{R}$ and is smooth: differentiating under the (probability) integral, $\varphi^{(k)}(r) = \int (\theta\cdot\nabla)^k f(r\theta)\,d\sigma(\theta)$, dominated uniformly by Schwartz seminorms of $f$; moreover $|\varphi^{(k)}(r)| \le \sup_{|y|=|r|}|(\nabla^k f)(y)|\cdot 1 \le C_{k,N}(1+|r|)^{-N}$ for every $N$. And $\varphi$ is even, because $\sigma$ is symmetric under $\theta \mapsto -\theta$ (§5.1).

It remains to see that $x \mapsto \varphi(|x|)$ is smooth at the origin (elsewhere it is a composition of smooth maps). Since $\varphi$ is even and smooth, every odd derivative vanishes at $0$, so Taylor's theorem with integral remainder gives, for each $N$, $$\varphi(r) = \sum_{k=0}^{N} a_k\, r^{2k} + r^{2N+2}\,\rho_N(r), \qquad \rho_N(r) = \frac{1}{(2N+1)!}\int_0^1 (1-t)^{2N+1}\varphi^{(2N+2)}(tr)\,dt,$$ with $\rho_N$ smooth (differentiate under the $t$-integral) and even. Hence $\varphi(|x|) = \sum_k a_k |x|^{2k} + |x|^{2N+2}\rho_N(|x|)$; the polynomial part is a polynomial in the coordinates ($|x|^{2k} = (x_1^2+\cdots+x_d^2)^k$), and the remainder term $x \mapsto |x|^{2N+2}\rho_N(|x|)$ is of class $C^{N}$ on all of $\mathbb{R}^d$: away from the origin it is smooth, and each of its partial derivatives of order $j \le N$ is a finite sum of terms (chain and product rule) carrying at least $2N+2-2j \ge 2$ surviving powers of the coordinates, hence extends continuously by $0$ to the origin. Since $N$ was arbitrary, $\varphi(|x|)$ has continuous partial derivatives of every order: $\mathcal Rf$ is smooth. The decay bounds $|\varphi^{(k)}(r)| \le C_{k,N}(1+|r|)^{-N}$ then bound every Schwartz seminorm of $\mathcal Rf$ via the chain rule on $|x| \ge 1$ and the smoothness just proved on $|x| \le 1$. ∎

Proposition J (averaging preserves everything relevant). (i) If $f \in \mathcal{A}_d$ then $\mathcal Rf \in \mathcal{A}_d^{\mathrm{rad}} := \mathcal{A}_d \cap \mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{rad}}$, with $\mathcal Rf(0) = f(0)$ and $\widehat{\mathcal Rf}(0) = \widehat f(0)$; hence the infimum in (3) is unchanged when restricted to radial admissible functions. (ii) If $g \in L^1$ is continuous with $\widehat g = \varsigma g$, then $\widehat{\mathcal Rg} = \varsigma\,\mathcal Rg$ and $r(\mathcal Rg) \le r(g)$. (iii) If moreover $g \ne 0$ and $r(g) \lt \infty$, then $\mathcal Rg \ne 0$.

(i) $\mathcal Rf$ is radial Schwartz by Lemma M. Signs: for $|x| \ge 1$, $\mathcal Rf(x)$ averages values $f(|x|\theta) \le 0$; $\widehat{\mathcal Rf} = \mathcal R\widehat f \ge 0$ pointwise (average of nonnegative values); $\widehat{\mathcal Rf}(0) = \mathcal R\widehat f(0) = \widehat f(0) \gt 0$; and $\mathcal Rf(0) = f(0)$. So $\mathcal Rf$ is an admissible radial function with the same objective ratio, and conversely every radial admissible function competes in (3); the infima agree.

(ii) Lemma L applies ($g$ continuous; the decay hypothesis of Lemma L can be replaced by $g \in L^1$ plus continuity, since its proof only used Fubini with dominant $|g| \in L^1$ and continuity to define values): $\widehat{\mathcal Rg} = \mathcal R\widehat g = \varsigma\mathcal Rg$. If $g \ge 0$ outside radius $r(g)$, so are its spherical means: $r(\mathcal Rg) \le r(g)$.

(iii) Suppose $\mathcal Rg = 0$. Outside $B(0, r(g))$ the function $g$ is continuous, nonnegative, with vanishing mean on every sphere of radius $\rho \gt r(g)$; a nonnegative continuous function with zero $\sigma$-mean vanishes on that sphere, because $\sigma$ charges every nonempty relatively open subset (its cone has positive volume). Hence $g = 0$ outside a compact ball, so $\widehat g$ extends to a function real-analytic along every line (the compact-support analyticity argument in Proposition F), and $\widehat g = \varsigma g$ also vanishes outside that ball; by the identity theorem along lines through the origin, $\widehat g \equiv 0$, so $g = \varsigma \widehat g \equiv 0$ — contradiction. ∎

5.3 From $L^1$ eigenfunctions to Schwartz eigenfunctions

The obstruction of §7 will be proved for radial Schwartz eigenfunctions. Definition (6) allows arbitrary $L^1$ eigenfunctions, so we need an approximation that preserves the three constraints — eigenvalue, real radial class, vanishing at the origin — while landing in $\mathcal{S}$. Gaussians do all the work.

Lemma K (eigenfunction mollification). Let $g \in L^1_{\mathrm{rad}}(\mathbb{R}^d;\mathbb{R})$ satisfy $\widehat g = \varsigma g$ and $g(0) = 0$ (continuous representative, §2.3). For $n \ge 1$ set $$\kappa_n(x) = n^d e^{-\pi n^2|x|^2}, \qquad \eta_n(x) = e^{-\pi|x|^2/n^2}, \qquad q_n = \eta_n\,(g * \kappa_n),$$ $$p_n = \frac{q_n + \varsigma\,\widehat{q_n}}{2}, \qquad g_n = p_n - \frac{p_n(0)}{\psi_\varsigma(0)}\,\psi_\varsigma,$$ with $\psi_\pm$ from Proposition 2.6. Then $g_n \in \mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{rad}}(\mathbb{R}^d;\mathbb{R})$, $\widehat{g_n} = \varsigma g_n$, $g_n(0) = 0$, and $g_n \to g$ in $L^1$.

$q_n$ is radial Schwartz. $g * \kappa_n$ is smooth (differentiate under the convolution integral; $\kappa_n$ Schwartz, $g \in L^1$) with all derivatives bounded by $\|g\|_1$ times seminorms of $\kappa_n$; multiplying by the Gaussian $\eta_n$ imposes super-polynomial decay on the product and all its derivatives (Leibniz), giving Schwartz. Radial: convolution of radial functions is radial ($g*\kappa_n(Ux) = \int g(Ux - y)\kappa_n(y)dy$, substitute $y = Uz$), and $\eta_n$ is radial. Real: all three factors are real.

Convergence. $\|q_n - g\|_1 \le \|\eta_n(g*\kappa_n - g)\|_1 + \|(\eta_n - 1)g\|_1 \le \|g*\kappa_n - g\|_1 + \|(1-\eta_n)g\|_1 \to 0$: the first term by the approximate-identity property of $\kappa_n$ (mass concentrating at $0$; same estimate as in Theorem 2.4), the second by dominated convergence ($\eta_n \uparrow 1$ pointwise).

Transform. By Proposition 2.3 (with $s = n^2$ and the dilation rule), $\widehat{\kappa_n} = \eta_n$ and $\widehat{\eta_n} = \kappa_n$. Hence, by Lemma 2.2(iv) and the multiplication–convolution duality applied in the other direction (for $q_n = \eta_n\cdot(g*\kappa_n)$, whose factors are in $L^1$ with integrable transforms, one checks via Fubini exactly as in Lemma 2.2 that $\widehat{q_n} = \widehat{\eta_n} * \widehat{(g*\kappa_n)}$): $$\widehat{q_n} = \kappa_n * (\eta_n\, \widehat g\,) \longrightarrow \widehat g \text{ in } L^1,$$ by the same two-term estimate. Note $\widehat{q_n}$ is again real, radial, and Schwartz.

Origin values. $q_n(0) = (g*\kappa_n)(0) = \int g(-y)\kappa_n(y)dy \to g(0) = 0$, since $g$'s continuous representative is continuous at $0$ and bounded ($\|\widehat g\|_\infty \le \|g\|_1$ plus inversion) — the approximate-identity limit at a continuity point. Likewise $\widehat{q_n}(0) = \int q_n \to \int g = \widehat g(0) = \varsigma g(0) = 0$.

Symmetrize and correct. $\widehat{p_n} = \tfrac12(\widehat{q_n} + \varsigma\,\widehat{\widehat{q_n}}) = \tfrac12(\widehat{q_n} + \varsigma q_n) = \varsigma p_n$, using Lemma H on the radial $q_n$. And $p_n \to \tfrac12(g + \varsigma\widehat g) = g$ in $L^1$, with $p_n(0) = \tfrac12(q_n(0) + \varsigma\widehat{q_n}(0)) \to 0$. Since $\widehat{\psi_\varsigma} = \varsigma\psi_\varsigma$ and $\psi_\varsigma(0) \ne 0$ (Proposition 2.6), the corrected $g_n$ is a radial Schwartz $\varsigma$-eigenfunction with $g_n(0) = 0$ exactly, and $\|g_n - p_n\|_1 = |p_n(0)/\psi_\varsigma(0)|\cdot\|\psi_\varsigma\|_1 \to 0$, so $g_n \to g$ in $L^1$. ∎

Why $L^1$ convergence is the right currency. The obstruction of §7 is an $L^1$-mass statement ("exponentially little of $\|g\|_1$ lives inside the ball"), and §8 will apply it to the approximants $g_n$ and pass to the limit. Pointwise convergence is neither available nor needed.
