§1 What this site is

OpenAI published Ten Advances in Mathematics and Theoretical Computer Science — a 249-page survey of ten recent theorems, one independent chapter each, written for readers already inside each result's subfield. The PDF is not redistributed here; download it from that link. Every page range cited below refers to that PDF.

This site rewrites each chapter for a different reader: someone with a PhD in some field, but not this field. A probabilist reading the operator-algebras chapter; a systems person reading the lattice chapter. The premise: such a reader's gap is vocabulary and objects, not reasoning ability — so the explainers hold the logic at full rigor and rebuild only the objects, from a "common graduate core" baseline (linear algebra, basic measure theory and probability, basic group and point-set topology, asymptotics, comfort with proofs).

Each explainer is a single self-contained HTML file: open it offline, no network, and read end to end. Every formula in every chapter was verified against the rendered PDF page before the chapter was marked done. This index page links to all ten; the full build specification is also live as implementation-plan.html, and the source lives at github.com/az9713/ten-math-cs-advances.

What "difficulty" means here. The rating on each chapter is outsider difficulty: the estimated load on a reader with a PhD outside the chapter's field, taken from the build spec's prerequisite audit — how many objects sit above the common graduate core and how foreign they are. It rates the object gap, not the reader, and not the depth of the original proof.

§3 Shared machinery — what reuses what

The ten results are independent, but their explainers are not: four pieces of machinery were written once, in the chapter that needs them first, and reused by reference. Fig. 2 maps every such dependency. Solid arrows are primers flowing forward ("read the source section first, or accept it as a black box"); the dashed arrow is the one place a result flows backward — Chapter 2's spherical-code bound independently recovers Chapter 1's sphere-packing exponent in a limit, a genuine cross-check between two chapters of the survey.

The four reused pieces, with their home sections:

  • LP certificates (Delsarte / Cohn–Elkies) — built across Chapter 1, which is spent entirely inside the Cohn–Elkies linear program. Chapter 2 runs the same certificate logic a second time, on Delsarte's LP for codes; by then the reader reads LP certificates as a practiced language.
  • Codes (Hamming and spherical) — Chapter 2 §§1–2 defines binary and spherical codes from scratch; Chapter 7's nearest-codeword and syndrome-decoding corollaries lean on the same vocabulary.
  • Property (T) and expanders — Chapter 3 §3 is a standalone primer (written for the non-soficity proof's expander machinery); Chapter 4's rigidity construction reuses it directly.
  • Lattices in ℝⁿ — Chapter 8 §2 builds the lattice primer with Blichfeldt's and Minkowski's theorems proved in full; Chapter 7's CVP chapter reuses it (and Chapter 1's packing problem echoes it).

Chapters 5, 6, 9, and 10 import nothing from their siblings — each is fully self-contained.

§4 The ten chapters — result, significance, and what each explainer adds

One card per chapter, in PDF order. The Result box states the theorem as the build spec records it (each claim traceable to the card's PDF page range); Why it matters places it; In the explainer says what the artifact itself does beyond the paper. The colored left edge of each card encodes the difficulty rating from §2.

Ch. 1 — High-dimensional sphere packing

pp. 3–28 · 26 ppdifficulty: medium32 figures

Result. The Cohn–Elkies linear program's exact exponential rate: LPd1/d → √e/(2π). Consequence: sphere-packing density Δd ≤ 2−(0.6044…+o(1))d — the first improvement to the general sphere-packing exponent since Kabatianskii–Levenshtein 1978 (0.5990…), plus a matching lower bound showing no Cohn–Elkies function can do better, and the asymptotic solution of the Fourier sign-uncertainty problem (radii (1/π+o(1))√d).

Why it matters. The upper-bound exponent for packing density in high dimension had not moved in 45 years, and the Cohn–Elkies LP is the method behind the celebrated dimension-8 and dimension-24 solutions. This chapter both improves the exponent and closes the question of how far that method can ever go.

In the explainer. 32 figures; a 46-check machine battery; the numeric-validation section runs the Cohn–Elkies rate out to dimension 6×10⁶. Home of the LP-certificate spine that Chapter 2 reuses.

Ch. 2 — Binary and spherical codes

pp. 29–78 · 50 ppdifficulty: high (longest)34 figures

Result. First improvements since 1977/1978 to the general asymptotic upper-bound exponents for binary codes (beating the optimized McEliece–Rodemich–Rumsey–Welch bound for every fixed relative distance 0 < δ < 1/2) and spherical codes (beating optimized Kabatianskii–Levenshtein for every 0 < s < 1). The s → 1 limit independently recovers Chapter 1's sphere-packing exponent.

Why it matters. The MRRW and Kabatianskii–Levenshtein bounds were the textbook frontier of coding theory for nearly five decades; this is the first strict improvement at every distance, and its limiting case cross-checks Chapter 1 by an independent route.

In the explainer. 34 figures; a 19-check exact battery that verifies the imported Kravchuk/Weyl representation-theory formulas in exact rational arithmetic and certifies the paper's kissing-number exponent 0.39661 from explicit five-parameter tuples. §§1–2 are the codes primer reused by Chapter 7; §15's s → 1 limit re-derives Chapter 1's exponent in-page.

Ch. 3 — A non-sofic group

pp. 79–95 · 17 ppdifficulty: high24 figures

Result. The unit group of the binary Leavitt algebra L𝔽₂(1,2) is not sofic — the first group ever shown to admit no finite permutation approximations, resolving the Gromov–Weiss soficity conjecture with no unproved stability hypotheses.

Why it matters. Whether every group is sofic was a central open question connecting group theory, dynamics, and operator algebras (Aldous–Lyons, MIP* = RE); this is the first outright counterexample, with no conditional hypotheses left standing.

In the explainer. 24 figures, 30 checks; §3 is the property-(T)/expander primer that Chapter 4 reuses; the entry toy verifies a permutation approximation by hand ("finite groups are sofic, exactly" — left multiplication is the approximation) before the general machinery starts.

Ch. 4 — Connes's rigidity conjecture disproved

pp. 96–113 · 18 ppdifficulty: highest22 figures

Result. Infinitely many pairwise nonisomorphic, finitely generated ICC property-(T) groups Γ₀, Γ₁, … with L(Γₙ) ≅ L(Λ) — one von Neumann algebra, infinitely many groups. Disproves Connes's 1982 conjecture and answers Popa's finite-to-one question negatively, showing Popa's countability bound is sharp.

Why it matters. Connes conjectured in 1982 that a property-(T) group can be recovered from its von Neumann algebra; forty years of rigidity theory was built toward that expectation. This construction refutes it in the strongest form: infinitely many groups collapse to one algebra.

In the explainer. 22 figures, ~25 exact checks; leans on Chapter 3 §3 for property (T) and expanders, and builds the binary-carry toy — two different compact group structures on the same probability space — before the general machine.

Ch. 5 — Circuit and formula lower bounds for the permanent

pp. 114–153 · 40 ppdifficulty: medium29 figures

Result. Division-free arithmetic circuits for pern require Ω(n² log log n) gates — the first superlinear-in-n² bound, i.e. C(pern)/n² → ∞; arithmetic formulas require Ω(n⁴/log n) leaves (previous record Ω(n³)). Both proofs use properties specific to the permanent and provably do not transfer to the determinant — itself an instructive fact.

Why it matters. Superlinear circuit lower bounds for explicit polynomials are among the hardest targets in complexity theory; budging the permanent past n² is the first movement on this front in decades, and the determinant obstruction sharpens where permanent-vs-determinant hardness actually lives.

In the explainer. 29 figures; an 11-check exact battery that re-runs the coefficient-independence construction at n = 13 — all 2,401 entries of a 49×49 Jacobian rebuilt by brute-force permanents, factored, and inverted in exact arithmetic — plus the block-cancellation identity in exact ℤ[ζ]; 7 Import boxes mark exactly which external theorems are taken on faith.

Ch. 6 — Quantum parallel repetition

pp. 154–182 · 29 ppdifficulty: high29 figures

Result. For every finite two-player entangled game with ω*(G) = 1−ε < 1: ω*(G⊗n) ≤ exp(−c·ε¹³/(ε+log|A||B|)·n) — exponential decay for all entangled games, open since ≈2004; previously only polynomial decay (Yuen) or exponential for special classes (anchored games, Bavarian–Vidick–Yuen). New ingredient: a postselection-stable quantum sampleability estimate.

Why it matters. Parallel repetition is the standard amplification tool for interactive proofs and nonlocal games; whether it decays exponentially against entangled players was the field's basic open question for two decades. This settles it in full generality.

In the explainer. 29 figures, 34 checks; the CHSH game is worked as the entry toy; only one Import box in the whole chapter — everything else is proved in-page from the graduate core.

Ch. 7 — Hardness of the closest vector problem

pp. 183–218 · 36 ppdifficulty: medium–high38 figures

Result. A deterministic polynomial-time reduction from 3SAT proves NP-hardness of approximating Euclidean CVP within n1/400 — polynomial-factor hardness without the PCP theorem and without assuming the Projection Games Conjecture. Same construction: n1/200 for nearest codeword and syndrome decoding, n1/(200p) for every ℓp.

Why it matters. CVP hardness underpins the security story of lattice cryptography, and polynomial-factor NP-hardness had only been known through heavy PCP machinery or unproved conjectures. A direct, deterministic, PCP-free reduction changes what the hardness rests on.

In the explainer. 38 figures (the project maximum) and a 44-check battery; §17 executes the entire 3SAT→lattice construction on a toy formula in F₁₆ — 3,120 unknowns, 8,949 equations, rank computed exactly — and re-proves the paper's parameter inequalities at N = 100 in exact integer arithmetic. Reuses Chapter 8 §2's lattice primer and Chapter 2's codes vocabulary.

Ch. 8 — Ehrhart's volume conjecture

pp. 219–228 · 10 ppdifficulty: high (dense)13 figures

Result. A convex body K ⊂ ℝⁿ whose barycenter is its unique interior lattice point has vol(K) ≤ (n+1)ⁿ/n! — sharp (the centered dilated simplex attains it). Open since Ehrhart 1964; previous best was 4ⁿe−cn, exponentially far from sharp.

Why it matters. A 60-year-old conjecture at the meeting point of convex geometry and lattice-point counting, solved sharply — the extremal body is identified, not just bounded. The previous best bound was off by an exponential factor.

In the explainer. 13 figures; §2 is the lattice primer with Blichfeldt's and Minkowski's theorems proved in full (reused by Chapter 7); the n = 1 case is checked by hand as the entry toy.

Ch. 9 — Multicolor Ramsey numbers

pp. 229–235 · 7 ppdifficulty: low15 figures

Result. Rk(3) ≥ (ck1/3/log k)k, establishing Rk(3) = kΘ(k) — the first super-exponential lower bound, settling a question with Erdős prize money attached ($100: is lim Rk(3)1/k finite? Answer: no). Corollary: Shannon capacity of graphs with independence number 2 is unbounded.

Why it matters. The classical constructions (sum-free sets, Schur colorings) cap out at exponential growth ck; whether multicolor triangle Ramsey numbers grow faster was open long enough for Erdős to put money on it. The recursive-coloring idea that breaks the barrier is genuinely new.

In the explainer. 15 figures; the calibration chapter — it set the template depth, prose-to-formalism ratio, and figure density for the other nine. Gentlest full pass through the format; start here.

Ch. 10 — Compactness and degeneracy conjectures

pp. 236–249 · 14 ppdifficulty: low–medium20 figures

Result. Two counterexamples: (i) a finite family 𝓕 of connected bipartite graphs with cycles where ex(n,𝓕) = O(n4/3−1/48) yet every single member F has ex(n,F) = Ω(n4/3) — killing the Erdős–Simonovits compactness conjecture; (ii) a connected bipartite 2-degenerate H with ex(n,H) ≥ cn3/2+ε — killing Erdős's degeneracy conjecture at r = 2.

Why it matters. Both conjectures shaped how extremal graph theorists expected Turán exponents to behave — that a family is only as hard as its easiest member, and that sparse (degenerate) graphs have small exponents. Both expectations fail, by construction.

In the explainer. 20 figures; the W(2) generalized quadrangle (the "doily") behind the construction is computed directly in-page rather than cited.

§5 How the explainers were built and verified

All ten chapters follow one four-layer template, fixed during the Chapter 9 calibration pass: Layer 0, the problem in plain terms with no new notation; Layer 1, the precise statement with every symbol defined just before first use; Layer 2, the proof architecture as an SVG dependency diagram; Layer 3, the full walkthrough with just-in-time background boxes. Standing devices throughout: a toy instance before every general definition, analogy anchors with declared break-points, and an honest-scope statement of what the reader will and will not be able to do afterward.

The verification standard, uniformly applied:

  • Every provable step is proved. Anything reachable from the graduate core is written out in full; genuinely deep external theorems are stated precisely in boxed "Import N" form with citations, so the reader always knows exactly which black boxes the argument rests on. Chapter 6 needs one import; Chapter 5, seven.
  • Formulas verified against the rendered PDF page — not against extracted text, which flattens sub- and superscripts — before a chapter is marked done.
  • Numeric validation with real computed numbers, and the runnable listing shipped in-page — see the per-card highlights in §4 (dimension 6×10⁶ in Chapter 1, exact rational Kravchuk/Weyl checks in Chapter 2, the full F₁₆ construction in Chapter 7, the 49×49 exact Jacobian in Chapter 5, the computed doily in Chapter 10).
  • Mechanical gates on every file: LaTeX well-formedness, SVG validity, link integrity, element overlap and frame containment in a headless browser, plus a dark-mode figure audit of every figure.
  • 256 hand-built SVG figures across the ten chapters (13 to 38 per chapter), each generated from computed data by the tracked scripts in figgen/, not sketched by eye.
  • Offline by construction: MathJax is inlined into each chapter file (~2.3 MB each), so the math renders with no CDN and no fetched scripts. This index page uses no MathJax and is about 40 kilobytes.

Scope note: the explainers contain no problem sets — a deliberate decision recorded in the build spec. The spec itself, including the per-chapter prerequisite audits these difficulty ratings come from, is implementation-plan.html.
