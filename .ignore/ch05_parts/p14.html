13. Why the determinant slips both nets

A healthy suspicion about any permanent lower bound: would the same argument “prove” a false bound for the determinant, which has polynomial-size circuits and quasipolynomial formulas? This section shows that both spines break for the determinant — for two different, structural reasons. This is the strongest sanity check a lower-bound method can pass, and it also teaches exactly which permanent-specific inputs did the work.

13.1 The formula spine fails: Schur compression

Let $Y$ be a matching of $k \lt n$ entries of the generic matrix $X$; as in §11, permute it to the first $k$ diagonal positions and block-decompose $$X = \begin{pmatrix} \operatorname{diag}(Y) + D_0 & U\\ V & W \end{pmatrix},$$ where $\operatorname{diag}(Y)$ is the $k\times k$ diagonal of marked variables, $D_0$ the unmarked entries of that block (zero diagonal), and $U, V, W$ unmarked. The block $W$ is a generic $(n-k)\times(n-k)$ matrix, so $\delta = \det W$ is a nonzero polynomial and $W^{-1} = \operatorname{adj}(W)/\delta$ exists over the rational-function field of the unmarked variables. Define the Schur complement $\Sigma = D_0 - UW^{-1}V$.

Lemma D (Schur identity and coefficient compression). In the rational-function field, $$\det X = \delta\,\det\bigl(\operatorname{diag}(Y) + \Sigma\bigr),$$ and for every $T \subseteq [k]$, $$\Bigl[\prod_{i\in T} y_i\Bigr]\det X = \delta\,\det \Sigma_{[k]\setminus T,\,[k]\setminus T}.$$ Consequently $\operatorname{td}_Y(\det X) \le k^2 + 1$.

Write $A = \operatorname{diag}(Y) + D_0$. The block factorization $$\begin{pmatrix} A & U\\ V & W\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} I_k & UW^{-1}\\ 0 & I_{n-k}\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} A - UW^{-1}V & 0\\ V & W\end{pmatrix}$$ is verified by block multiplication (top-left: $(A - UW^{-1}V) + UW^{-1}V = A$; top-right: $UW^{-1}W = U$; bottom row: $V, W$). Determinants multiply; block-triangular determinants are products of the diagonal blocks (expand along the columns of the zero block); the first factor has determinant $1$. Hence $\det X = \det(A - UW^{-1}V)\cdot\det W = \delta \det(\operatorname{diag}(Y) + \Sigma)$, since $A - UW^{-1}V = \operatorname{diag}(Y) + \Sigma$.

Now expand $\det(\operatorname{diag}(Y) + \Sigma)$ in the marked variables. Each $y_i$ occupies its own diagonal cell, so the determinant is multilinear in them, and the coefficient of $\prod_{i \in T}y_i$ deletes rows and columns $T$ (each chosen $y_i$ forces $\sigma(i) = i$ and contributes sign $+1$ for that fixed point) and sets the remaining marked cells' variable part to zero, leaving the principal minor $\det \Sigma_{[k]\setminus T, [k]\setminus T}$. Multiply by $\delta$ for the claimed coefficient formula. (Both sides are polynomials in the unmarked variables even though $\Sigma$ has denominators — the identity holds in the field, and the left side is a polynomial.)

Compression: every coefficient of $\det X$ with respect to $Y$ is thus a polynomial expression in the $k^2$ entries of $\Sigma$ and the single element $\delta$ — a field generated by $k^2 + 1$ elements. A field with $k^2+1$ generators has transcendence degree at most $k^2 + 1$ (§10.1, fact (i)), so $\operatorname{td}_Y(\det X) \le k^2 + 1$. ∎

For $k = \Theta(\log n)$ this yields $O(\log^2 n)$ where Lemma 9.1 gave the permanent $\Omega(n^2)$ — the formula spine's engine simply has nothing to work with. And the obstruction is robust in two directions, both proved by the same computation:

  • Arbitrary blocks, not just matchings. If $Y$ is any set of $1 \le k \lt n$ marked entries, its occupied rows and columns have some common size $s \le k$ after padding the smaller set; permuting them into the top-left $s \times s$ block and running Lemma D's argument verbatim (with $B(Y) + D_0$ in place of $\operatorname{diag}(Y) + D_0$, where $B(Y)$ holds the marked variables in their positions) shows every $Y$-coefficient lives in the field generated by $\delta$ and the $s^2$ entries of $D_0 - UW^{-1}V$: $\operatorname{td}_Y(\det X) \le s^2 + 1 \le k^2+1 \le nk$ (the last step: $s \le k \lt n$ gives $s^2 + 1 \le k^2+1 \le nk$; and for $k \ge n$ the trivial bound $\operatorname{td}_Y \le n^2 \le nk$ holds — there are only $n^2$ variables in total).
  • Summing cannot beat cubic. For pairwise entry-disjoint blocks $Y_1,\dots,Y_q$, total size $\le n^2$, summing gives $$\sum_{j=1}^{q}\operatorname{td}_{Y_j}(\det X) \;\le\; n\sum_{j=1}^{q}|Y_j| \;\le\; n^3. \tag{11.1}$$ So no choice of disjoint blocks makes the Nečiporuk–Kalorkoti scheme yield a supercubic determinant formula bound.

The ceiling is attained. The bound (11.1) is not just a limit — full matchings achieve it, which is exactly Kalorkoti's classical cubic bound in this language. Take $Y = $ all $n$ diagonal entries, $X = \operatorname{diag}(Y) + A$ with $a_{ii} = 0$; the $Y$-coefficients are the principal minors $\det A_{I,I}$. Diagonal conjugation $A \mapsto DAD^{-1}$ preserves every principal minor ($(DAD^{-1})_{I,I} = D_{I,I}A_{I,I}D^{-1}_{I,I}$), so on the open set $a_{1i} \ne 0$ one can normalize $b_{1i} = 1$ ($i \gt 1$) via $D = \operatorname{diag}(1, a_{12},\dots,a_{1n})$; the $(n-1)^2$ remaining normalized entries generate a field containing all principal minors, giving $\operatorname{td}_Y \le (n-1)^2$. Conversely the minors recover the normalized entries up to algebra: with $t_i = a_{i1}$, $$\det A_{\{1,i\},\{1,i\}} = -t_i, \qquad \det A_{\{i,j\},\{i,j\}} = -a_{ij}a_{ji}, \qquad \det A_{\{1,i,j\},\{1,i,j\}} = a_{ij}t_j + a_{ji}t_i$$ (direct $2\times2$ and $3\times3$ expansions with the normalized first row/zero diagonal), so the minor field contains $t_i$, $p_{ij} = a_{ij}a_{ji}$, and $u_{ij} = a_{ij}t_j + a_{ji}t_i$; multiplying the last by $a_{ij}$ gives $t_ja_{ij}^2 - u_{ij}a_{ij} + p_{ij}t_i = 0$, a quadratic with nonzero leading coefficient — each $a_{ij}$ is algebraic over the minor field. Algebraic extensions preserve transcendence degree, so $\operatorname{td}_Y(\det X) = (n-1)^2$ exactly. Partitioning the matrix into the $n$ cyclic full diagonals $Y_\tau = \{x_{i,(i+\tau)\bmod n}\}$ and applying Lemmas 8.1/10.2 per diagonal: $$L_{\mathrm{var}}(\Phi) \ge \frac{n\bigl((n-1)^2+2\bigr)}{4} \quad\text{(no division)}, \qquad L_{\mathrm{var}}(\Phi) \ge \frac{n\bigl((n-1)^2+3\bigr)}{6} \quad\text{(valid division)},$$ using $(n-1)^2 \le 4t_Y - 2$ resp. $\le 6t_Y - 3$ and summing. The method thus tops out for the determinant at cubic — and reaches it.

13.2 The circuit spine fails: critical loci refuse to shrink

First, the concrete disappointment. Replacing per by det in the $4\times4$ example of §8.1: $$\det\begin{pmatrix} u & v & 1 & 1\\ w & z & 1 & 1\\ p & q & 2 & -2\\ r & s & 2 & -2 \end{pmatrix} = 4\bigl((u - w)(q - s) - (v - z)(p - r)\bigr)$$ (verified exactly in §15, check 9) — a single $2\times2$ determinant in differences of the variables, not a sum of independent block determinants: cancellation has compressed all the variable dependence into a $2\times2$ core. This is general. Consider any bordered block matrix $\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} X & U\\ V & 0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$ with $X$ an $r\times s$ variable block, $U, V$ constant, sizes $r\times(r-d)$ and $(s-d)\times s$, $2 \le d \le \min\{r,s\}$. If $U$ lacks full column rank or $V$ full row rank, the determinant vanishes identically (dependent constant columns/rows). Otherwise pick a full-row-rank $d \times r$ matrix $A$ with $AU = 0$ (rows spanning the left kernel of $U$) and a full-column-rank $s\times d$ matrix $C$ with $VC = 0$; constant changes of basis separating kernels from complements give $$\det\begin{pmatrix} X & U\\ V & 0\end{pmatrix} = c\,\det(AXC), \qquad c \ne 0:$$ the bordered determinant sees only the $d\times d$ compression $AXC$. Now the map $X \mapsto AXC$ is surjective onto $d\times d$ matrices ($A$ has a right inverse $A'$, $C$ a left inverse $C'$; $X = A'HC'$ hits any $H$), and the critical locus of $\det_d$ is the set of matrices of rank $\le d-2$ (the first derivatives of a determinant are its $(d{-}1)\times(d{-}1)$ cofactors, which vanish simultaneously exactly at rank $\le d-2$). The rank-$\le\rho$ locus in $d\times d$ matrices has dimension $\rho(2d - \rho)$ — parametrize by a factorization $H = FG^{\mathsf T}$ with $F, G \in \mathbb{C}^{d\times\rho}$, subtract the $\rho^2$-dimensional common reparametrization $F \mapsto FM$, $G \mapsto GM^{-\mathsf T}$ — so at $\rho = d-2$ its codimension is $$d^2 - (d-2)(d+2) = 4.$$ Pulling back along the surjective linear map $L(X) = AXC$: the chain rule gives $d(\det\circ L)_X = L^*(d\det_{L(X)})$, and surjectivity of $L$ makes $L^*$ injective, so $\operatorname{Crit}(\det\circ L) = L^{-1}(\operatorname{Crit}(\det_d))$; choosing a complement of $\ker L$ identifies the domain with $\ker L \oplus \mathbb{C}^{d\times d}$, under which the inverse image is a product with $\ker L$ — codimension is preserved. The determinant analogue of §8's construction has critical-locus codimension exactly 4, where Proposition 4.3 needed $\Theta(n^2)$.

Could a cleverer specialization do better? No — and this is where the one remaining import enters.

Import 7 (Alper–Bogart–Velasco; [ABV17, Thm. 1.2]). Define the determinantal complexity $\operatorname{dc}(P)$ of a polynomial $P$ as the smallest $n$ such that $P = \det(A)$ for an $n \times n$ matrix $A$ of affine linear forms in $P$'s variables. If $P$ is homogeneous with $\operatorname{codim} \operatorname{Crit}(P) \gt 4$ (codimension in the ambient affine space of $P$'s variables), then $$\operatorname{codim}\operatorname{Crit}(P) + 1 \;\le\; \operatorname{dc}(P).$$
Proposition E (no good determinant specialization exists). Let $n \ge 5$ and let $P$ be a homogeneous polynomial of degree $\ge 3$ obtained from $\det_n$ by substituting affine linear forms. Then $\operatorname{codim}\operatorname{Crit}(P) \le n-1$, and the best circuit bound obtainable from Proposition 4.3 via determinant specializations is $O(n\log n)$ — below even the trivial $n^2 - 1$.

First, for homogeneous $P$ of degree $d \ge 1$, Euler's identity $d\,P(x) = \sum_i x_i\,\partial P/\partial x_i(x)$ holds (check it on each monomial: a monomial of degree $d$ is returned $d$ times, once per variable occurrence, by the right side). In characteristic zero it shows every critical point of $P$ satisfies $P(x) = 0$: $\operatorname{Crit}(P)$ is the singular locus of the hypersurface $P = 0$, matching Import 7's setting.

The substitution exhibiting $P$ is an $n\times n$ determinantal representation, so $\operatorname{dc}(P) \le n$. Two cases: if $\operatorname{codim}\operatorname{Crit}(P) \gt 4$, Import 7 gives $\operatorname{codim}\operatorname{Crit}(P) \le \operatorname{dc}(P) - 1 \le n - 1$; otherwise $\operatorname{codim}\operatorname{Crit}(P) \le 4 \le n-1$. Either way the slice dimension in Proposition 4.3 obeys $k \le \operatorname{codim}\operatorname{Crit}(P) \le n-1$ (its hypothesis is exactly $\dim\operatorname{Crit}(P) \le m - k$), and the degree obeys $d \le n$ (affine substitution cannot raise degree). The certificate is then at most $$\frac{k\log_2(d-1)}{3} \le \frac{(n-1)\log_2(n-1)}{3} = O(n\log n)$$ (and zero when $d = 2$). Since the determinant depends on all $n^2$ variables, even the trivial bound $n^2 - 1$ exceeds this for large $n$: the geometric method cannot prove anything nontrivial for the determinant. ∎

Summary of the two failures. Formula spine: the determinant's coefficients compress through the Schur complement ($\operatorname{td} \le k^2+1$); the permanent's do not (Lemma 9.1: $\ge m^2$). Circuit spine: determinant specializations have critical loci of codimension $\le n-1$ (Import 7); the permanent's engineered specialization achieves $\Theta(n^2)$ (Corollary 5.2 + Lemma 6.1). Deeper still, the reason Lemma 6.1's cancellation trick has no determinant analogue: complementary maximal determinantal minors satisfy the Plücker relations, which obstruct the block-supported weight pattern that roots of unity produced for permanental minors [see §14].
