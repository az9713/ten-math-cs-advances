4. Many preimages force many multiplications (Lemma 4.1)

The circuit spine begins with its counting engine: a polynomial map with many preimages cannot be computed with few multiplications. This section proves that statement in the exact form the later sections consume.

4.1 The multiplicative-complexity model

Definition (multiplicative complexity). Throughout the circuit spine we count only multiplications: affine operations are free. Concretely, a computation is a sequence of steps, each producing either an arbitrary $\mathbb{C}$-affine combination of inputs and previously computed values (cost $0$), or a product of two previously computed values (cost $1$). The multiplicative complexity of a task is the minimum number of costed steps.

Since an ordinary size-$L$ arithmetic circuit contains at most $L$ multiplication gates — its additions, subtractions, and scalar multiples are all affine — its multiplicative complexity is at most $L$. So a lower bound of $q$ on multiplicative complexity is a lower bound of $q$ on circuit size, and we get two structural freedoms for free: substituting affine linear forms for the inputs, and taking linear combinations of outputs, neither of which will ever cost a gate. These freedoms are exactly what §5 and §6 spend.

4.2 Warm-up: the power-sum gradient

Consider the homogeneous polynomial $$H(z_1,\dots,z_k) = \frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{k} z_i^{d}, \qquad \nabla H(z) = \bigl(z_1^{\,d-1},\dots,z_k^{\,d-1}\bigr), \qquad d \ge 2,$$ where $\nabla H$ is the gradient, the vector of first partial derivatives. Fix a target $a = (a_1,\dots,a_k)$ with all coordinates nonzero. The equation $z_i^{\,d-1} = a_i$ has exactly $d-1$ complex solutions for each coordinate independently (the $(d-1)$-st roots of $a_i$), so the fiber $\nabla H^{-1}(a)$ has exactly $(d-1)^k$ points — exponentially many in $k$. Fig. 6 draws the case $k = 2$, $d = 4$.

Against this count stands a counting ceiling: a circuit computing the gradient coordinates with $q$ multiplications describes the same fiber by $q$ quadratic equations plus some affine ones, and Bézout-type bounds cap the number of isolated solutions of such a system at $2^q$. Comparing $(d-1)^k \le 2^q$ gives $q \ge k \log_2 (d-1)$: logarithmically many multiplications per output coordinate, with the logarithm of the degree as the per-coordinate rate.

The warm-up map $(z_i^{d-1})_i$ is special, but the argument is not: the only two properties used are that the coordinates are homogeneous of a common degree and that the origin is the only common zero. That general form is what the permanent argument needs, because the gradient handed to it in §5 will certainly not consist of pure powers. Here is the general statement, with its full proof.

4.3 The counting lemma

Lemma 4.1. Let $F = (F_1,\dots,F_k) : \mathbb{C}^k \to \mathbb{C}^k$ have homogeneous coordinates of a common degree $e \ge 1$. If $F^{-1}(0) = \{0\}$ and a circuit in the multiplicative-complexity model computes all $F_i$ using $q$ multiplications, then $$e^k \le 2^{q}.$$

The proof has two halves: (I) some fiber of $F$ has exactly $e^k$ distinct points; (II) the circuit caps every fiber's isolated-point count at $2^q$.

(I.a) Counting with multiplicity, via projective Bézout. Fix any target $a = (a_1,\dots,a_k)$ and consider the system $F(u) = a$. Introduce one homogenizing variable $z$ and pass to the equations $$F_i(u) - a_i z^{e} = 0, \qquad i \in [k], \tag{4.1}$$ in the projective space $\mathbb{P}^k$ with coordinates $[u_1 : \cdots : u_k : z]$. Each left side is homogeneous of degree $e$ (each $F_i$ is, and $z^e$ is), so the system is well defined on $\mathbb{P}^k$. Points with $z \ne 0$ can be scaled to $z = 1$ and are exactly the affine solutions of $F(u) = a$; points with $z = 0$ are the potential solutions “at infinity.”

There are none: if $[u_1 : \cdots : u_k : 0]$ satisfied (4.1), then $u \ne 0$ (a projective point is a nonzero vector, and its last coordinate vanishes) and $F(u) = 0$ — contradicting the hypothesis $F^{-1}(0) = \{0\}$.

Next, the common zero set of (4.1) is zero-dimensional. If it had a positive-dimensional component, that component would meet the hyperplane $\{z = 0\}$ by Import 1(g) — but we just showed the zero set avoids that hyperplane entirely. So the zero set is finite, and Import 3 (projective Bézout) applies: the number of solutions, counted with multiplicity, is the product of the $k$ degrees, $$\underbrace{e \cdot e \cdots e}_{k \text{ factors}} = e^k.$$ Since none lie at infinity, this is the multiplicity-weighted count of the affine solutions of $F(u) = a$ — for every target $a$.

(I.b) Upgrading to $e^k$ distinct points, via the Nullstellensatz and generic smoothness. Multiplicity-weighted counts are not yet enough (a double root is one point). We show that a suitably chosen $a$ has a reduced fiber — all multiplicities one. Put $$R = \mathbb{C}[u_1,\dots,u_k], \qquad B = \mathbb{C}[F_1,\dots,F_k] \subseteq R.$$ Because the origin is the only common zero of the $F_i$, Import 2 gives an integer $h$ with $(u_1,\dots,u_k)^h \subseteq (F_1,\dots,F_k)$: every monomial of total degree $\ge h$ lies in the ideal generated by the $F_i$. That ideal is homogeneous (its generators are), so a monomial of degree $j \ge h$ can be written $\sum_i F_i G_i$ with each nonzero $G_i$ homogeneous of degree $j - e \lt j$. Iterating this rewriting on the $G_i$'s own high-degree monomials strictly decreases degree at each pass, so it terminates: every element of $R$ is a $B$-linear combination of the finitely many monomials of total degree $\lt h$. Hence $R$ is a finite $B$-module.

By Import 1(c), $\dim B = \dim R = k$. But $B$ is generated as a $\mathbb{C}$-algebra by the $k$ elements $F_1,\dots,F_k$; a $k$-generated algebra has Krull dimension $k$ only if the generators satisfy no nonzero polynomial relation (a relation would exhibit $B$ as a quotient of a polynomial ring in $k$ variables by a nonzero ideal, of dimension $\lt k$ by Import 1(a,b)). So $F_1,\dots,F_k$ are algebraically independent and $B \cong \mathbb{C}[y_1,\dots,y_k]$, a polynomial ring. Geometrically, the inclusion $B \subseteq R$ is the map $F$: it makes $F : \mathbb{C}^k \to \mathbb{C}^k$ a finite dominant morphism — finite because $R$ is a finite $B$-module (so all fibers are finite), dominant because $B \hookrightarrow R$ is injective (the image is dense). Import 5 (characteristic zero) now supplies a nonempty open set of targets $a$ whose fibers are reduced. Choose such an $a$, additionally requiring $a$ outside the finitely many hyperplanes $a_i = 0$ if desired (open conditions intersect). Its fiber consists of exactly $e^k$ distinct points by part (I.a).

(II) The circuit's ceiling. Order the $q$ multiplication gates topologically and introduce a fresh variable $v_j$ for the output of gate $j$. Because affine operations are free (and cost-free steps compose), the two inputs of gate $j$ are affine functions $A_j(u, v_{\lt j})$ and $B_j(u, v_{\lt j})$ of the original inputs and the earlier products. The gate is thus described by the gate equation $$v_j = A_j(u, v_{\lt j})\, B_j(u, v_{\lt j}), \qquad j \in [q],$$ of degree at most two. Each circuit output is an affine function $H_i(u, v)$, so requiring output $= a$ adds the $k$ affine equations $H_i(u,v) = a_i$. Altogether: $q$ equations of degree $\le 2$ and $k$ of degree $\le 1$ in the $q + k$ unknowns $(u, v)$, as in Fig. 7.

For every fixed $u$, the gate equations determine $v_1, v_2, \dots$ successively and uniquely (gate $j$'s right side involves only $u$ and $v_{\lt j}$). Hence the solution set of the full system maps bijectively onto the fiber $F^{-1}(a)$ under $(u,v) \mapsto u$: every fiber point extends uniquely, and every system solution projects to a fiber point. The fiber from (I.b) is finite, so all $e^k$ corresponding system solutions are isolated. Import 4 bounds the number of isolated solutions by the degree product $2^q \cdot 1^k = 2^q$. Therefore $e^k \le 2^q$. ∎

Toy check. $F(z_1,z_2) = (z_1^3, z_2^3)$: $e = 3$, $k = 2$, only common zero $0$. The lemma says any circuit computing both cubes needs $q \ge \log_2 9 \approx 3.17$, so $q \ge 4$ multiplications. (Two repeated squarings per coordinate give $q = 4$: $z^2 = z\cdot z$, $z^3 = z^2\cdot z$, twice — the bound is tight here.) The point of the general lemma is that it never looked at the formulas for the $F_i$ — only at their degree and their common zero — which is what lets §5 apply it to a gradient it cannot write down.
