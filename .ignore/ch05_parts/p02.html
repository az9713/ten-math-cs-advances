1. The two polynomials and the three machines

Fix an integer $n \ge 1$ and let $X = (x_{ij})_{i,j\in[n]}$ be an $n \times n$ matrix of algebraically independent variables over $\mathbb{C}$, where $[j] = \{1,\dots,j\}$. “Algebraically independent” means the $x_{ij}$ satisfy no polynomial relation — they are genuine formal symbols, and two polynomials in them are equal only if all their coefficients match. $S_n$ denotes the set of permutations (bijections) of $[n]$, and $\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma) \in \{\pm 1\}$ is the sign of a permutation.

Definition (permanent and determinant). $$\operatorname{per}_n(X) = \sum_{\sigma \in S_n} \prod_{i=1}^{n} x_{i,\sigma(i)}, \qquad {\det}_n(X) = \sum_{\sigma \in S_n} \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma) \prod_{i=1}^{n} x_{i,\sigma(i)}.$$ Each monomial chooses exactly one entry from each row and each column — a perfect matching of rows to columns (Fig. 1). Both polynomials are multilinear: no variable ever appears squared, because a permutation uses each row once.

One phrase in the chapter's setup deserves emphasis before anything else: throughout, “compute” means compute the polynomial exactly as a formal expression over $\mathbb{C}$, not merely evaluate it on particular numeric matrices. A device computes $\operatorname{per}_n$ only if its output, expanded symbolically, is coefficient-for-coefficient the polynomial above. This is the natural setting for algebraic complexity, and it is what makes tools like differentiation and coefficient extraction available to the lower-bound arguments.

1.1 Machine one: arithmetic circuits

Definition (arithmetic circuit; the size measure $C(f)$). An arithmetic circuit is a finite directed acyclic graph with:
  • input gates (no incoming edges) labeled by individual variables or by arbitrary complex constants;
  • binary internal gates labeled $+$, $-$, or $\times$, each combining the outputs of two earlier gates;
  • one designated output gate.
Intermediate values may be reused arbitrarily: a gate can feed its output to any number of later gates. The circuit is division-free. Its size is the number of arithmetic gates; input gates are not counted. $C(f)$ is the minimum size of a circuit whose output is the polynomial $f$. There are no restrictions on depth, intermediate degrees, fan-out, or cancellations.

The generosity of this model is the point: a lower bound against it rules out every division-free algebraic algorithm, however clever its sharing and cancellation. The permanent depends on all $N = n^2$ variables (deleting a variable changes the polynomial — its coefficient of the corresponding monomial is a nonzero subpermanent), and combining $N$ inputs into one output through binary gates needs at least $N-1$ gates; that is the trivial $\Omega(n^2)$ floor that Theorem 1.1 will finally beat.

1.2 Machines two and three: formulas, with and without division

Definition (arithmetic formula; the measures $L, L_{\mathrm{var}}, G$). An arithmetic formula $\Phi$ has the same inputs and binary arithmetic gates as a circuit, but its underlying graph is a rooted tree: every gate feeds exactly one later gate. Consequently, two uses of the same intermediate polynomial must be computed by separate subformulas — a formula cannot remember. Write:
  • $L(\Phi)$ — its total number of leaves;
  • $L_{\mathrm{var}}(\Phi)$ — the number of leaves labeled by variables, with repeated occurrences counted separately;
  • $G(\Phi)$ — its number of internal arithmetic gates. For a binary tree, $G(\Phi) = L(\Phi) - 1$.
A formula with division additionally permits $\div$ gates and is interpreted in the field $\mathbb{C}(x_{ij} : i,j \in [n])$ of rational functions (ratios of polynomials). Such a formula is valid if every denominator is a nonzero element of this field. Its final output must equal the polynomial being computed; intermediate results are allowed to be genuinely rational.

Why study both models? Because they fail differently, and the chapter exploits each failure with a different weapon. A circuit's strength is reuse: the circuit bound (§6) actually leans on this strength, using the fact that reuse lets one differentiate an entire circuit cheaply. A formula's weakness is amnesia: the formula bound (§10–§12) charges independent pieces of information to distinct leaf occurrences, which is only possible because a tree cannot share. The two proofs are logically independent, and you can read either spine first.
