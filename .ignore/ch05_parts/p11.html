10. Formulas I: how much a tree can know (Lemma 8.1)

The formula spine starts fresh — nothing from §4–§9 is needed. Its currency is not geometry but information: how many independent parameters a polynomial carries in the coefficients attached to a chosen group of variables, and how few of them a small tree can produce.

10.1 The coefficient transcendence degree

Let $X$ be a finite set of variables, $\emptyset \ne Y \subseteq X$ the selected variables, and $Z = X \setminus Y$ the remaining ones. Every $f \in \mathbb{C}[X] = \mathbb{C}[Z][Y]$ expands uniquely as a polynomial in $Y$ with coefficients that are polynomials in $Z$: $$f = \sum_{\alpha \in \mathbb{N}^{Y}} f_\alpha\, Y^{\alpha}, \qquad f_\alpha \in \mathbb{C}[Z], \qquad Y^\alpha = \prod_{y \in Y} y^{\alpha(y)},$$ with only finitely many $f_\alpha$ nonzero. ($\mathbb{N}^Y$ is the set of exponent vectors; $\alpha = 0$ gives $f_0$, the result of setting all of $Y$ to zero.) The coefficients $f_\alpha$ are how $f$ “uses” the variables $Y$.

How much independent information do the coefficients contain? Counting distinct or linearly independent ones overestimates: coefficients can be polynomially related. The right count is algebraic:

Definition (algebraic independence; coefficient transcendence degree). Polynomials $c_1,\dots,c_r \in \mathbb{C}[Z]$ are algebraically independent over $\mathbb{C}$ if $Q(c_1,\dots,c_r) \ne 0$ for every nonzero polynomial $Q \in \mathbb{C}[T_1,\dots,T_r]$ — no polynomial relation whatsoever holds among them. For a field $\mathbb{C} \subseteq K$, the transcendence degree $\operatorname{trdeg}_{\mathbb{C}} K$ is the largest number of elements of $K$ algebraically independent over $\mathbb{C}$. Define the coefficient transcendence degree of $f$ with respect to $Y$ as $$\operatorname{td}_Y(f) = \operatorname{trdeg}_{\mathbb{C}}\, \mathbb{C}\bigl(f_\alpha : \alpha \in \mathbb{N}^Y\bigr),$$ the transcendence degree of the smallest subfield of $\mathbb{C}(Z)$ containing all the coefficients.

Two facts about transcendence degree are used repeatedly; both are immediate from the definition plus standard field theory. (i) A field generated by $r$ elements has transcendence degree at most $r$ — whether or not the generators are independent: among any $r+1$ elements of $\mathbb{C}(g_1,\dots,g_r)$ there is always a polynomial relation (else the field would contain a polynomial ring in $r+1$ variables, which cannot embed in a field of transcendence degree $\le r$; this is the exchange property of transcendence bases). (ii) Passing to a subfield cannot increase it.

Two tiny examples. For $Y = \{y\}$, $Z = \{z_1, z_2\}$: the polynomial $f = y z_1 + z_2$ has coefficients $c_1 = z_1$, $c_0 = z_2$, algebraically independent — $\operatorname{td}_Y(f) = 2$. The polynomial $f = y z^2 + z$ has coefficients $c_1 = z^2$, $c_0 = z$ satisfying $T_1 - T_0^2 \mapsto z^2 - (z)^2 = 0$: one independent parameter, $\operatorname{td}_Y(f) = 1$. Relations, not appearances, are what count.

10.2 The occurrence bound

For a formula $\Phi$ and the selected set $Y$, let $t_Y(\Phi)$ be the number of leaves of $\Phi$ labeled by variables from $Y$, counting repeats separately. The theorem of this section: a division-free formula that touches $Y$ only $t$ times computes a polynomial whose entire coefficient family lives in a field with at most $4t - 2$ generators. The method is Nečiporuk–Kalorkoti-style accounting [Nec66, Kal85, KS14 §3.2.1]; the proof below supplies the explicit constant.

Lemma 8.1. If a division-free formula $\Phi$ computes $f \in \mathbb{C}[X]$ and $t = t_Y(\Phi) \ge 1$, then $$\operatorname{td}_Y(f) \;\le\; 4t - 2 \;\le\; 4t.$$

Mark every vertex of $\Phi$ whose subformula contains a leaf labeled by a $Y$-variable. The marked vertices form the subtree connecting the $t$ selected leaves to the root (Fig. 19). A marked gate has one or two marked children, and the two cases play opposite roles.

One marked child: an affine wrapper. Say the marked child outputs $u$ and the other child — containing no $Y$-leaf — computes some $h \in \mathbb{C}[Z]$. Depending on the gate's operation and operand order, the output is one of $$u + h, \qquad u - h, \qquad h - u, \qquad h\,u,$$ each of the form $Au + B$ with $A, B \in \mathbb{C}[Z]$. Moreover a whole path of such gates collapses into one affine map, since $$A_2(A_1 u + B_1) + B_2 = (A_2A_1)\,u + (A_2B_1 + B_2),$$ with slope and intercept still in $\mathbb{C}[Z]$ (Fig. 20). An arbitrarily long one-marked-child path therefore costs no parameters yet — its accumulated $A, B$ will be charged only when the path ends.

The invariant. We prove by induction on the marked subtree: every marked subformula with $s \ge 1$ selected leaves outputs a value representable as $$A\,g + B, \qquad A, B \in \mathbb{C}[Z], \qquad g \in \mathbb{C}(\Gamma)[Y], \qquad \Gamma \subseteq \mathbb{C}(Z),\ |\Gamma| \le 4s - 4, \tag{8.1}$$ where $\mathbb{C}(\Gamma)$ is the subfield of $\mathbb{C}(Z)$ generated by the finite set $\Gamma$. The outer pair $A, B$ is deliberately not yet counted in $\Gamma$: it stays “unpaid” until the subformula meets another marked subformula or reaches the root.

  • Base, $s = 1$: at a marked leaf labeled $y \in Y$, take $g = y$, $A = 1$, $B = 0$, $\Gamma = \emptyset$; and $4\cdot 1 - 4 = 0$. ✓
  • One marked child: compose the gate's affine map with the child's outer map, as above; $g$ and $\Gamma$ are unchanged. ✓
  • Two marked children, with $s_1, s_2 \ge 1$ selected leaves and representations $A_ig_i + B_i$, $|\Gamma_i| \le 4s_i - 4$: adjoin the four outer coefficients, $$\Gamma = \Gamma_1 \cup \Gamma_2 \cup \{A_1, B_1, A_2, B_2\}.$$ Both children's outputs now lie in the single ring $\mathbb{C}(\Gamma)[Y]$, and applying the gate's operation ($+$, $-$, or $\times$ — no division here) keeps the result inside it. Represent the output as $1\cdot g + 0$ with this new inner $g$. The count, with $s = s_1 + s_2$: $$|\Gamma| \le (4s_1 - 4) + (4s_2 - 4) + 4 = 4s - 4. \; \checkmark$$

Equivalently: the marked tree has exactly $t - 1$ two-marked-children gates (each merge reduces the number of marked components by one, from $t$ leaves down to $1$ root), each charged $\le 4$ parameters, and the one-child stretches are free.

Conclusion. Apply (8.1) at the root ($s = t$, marked since $t \ge 1$): $f = Ag + B$ with $|\Gamma| \le 4t - 4$. Adjoin the final unpaid pair: $\Gamma' = \Gamma \cup \{A, B\}$, so $|\Gamma'| \le 4t - 2$ and $f \in \mathbb{C}(\Gamma')[Y]$. Expanding $f$ in $Y$ inside this ring, every coefficient $f_\alpha$ lies in $\mathbb{C}(\Gamma')$. A field generated by $|\Gamma'|$ elements has transcendence degree at most $|\Gamma'|$ (fact (i) of §10.1), and subfields only shrink it (fact (ii)): $$\operatorname{td}_Y(f) \le \operatorname{trdeg}_\mathbb{C} \mathbb{C}(\Gamma') \le 4t - 2.$$ ∎

Why this is a formula theorem. The count “$t$ marked leaves $\Rightarrow$ $t-1$ merge gates” is a fact about trees. In a circuit (a DAG), one marked subcomputation can feed many gates, and the accounting collapses: a circuit with few multiplication gates can manufacture coefficient families of enormous transcendence degree by reuse. That reuse is exactly what the circuit spine leaned on (Baur–Strassen, §6) — each spine converts the defining feature of its own model into a weapon.

To use Lemma 8.1 against the permanent we need its counterpart: a certificate that $\operatorname{td}_Y(\operatorname{per}_n)$ is large for cleverly chosen small $Y$. The certificate machine is Import 6 (the Jacobian criterion, §3.2): to prove $r$ chosen coefficients algebraically independent, exhibit $r$ remaining variables and one point where the corresponding $r \times r$ Jacobian minor is a nonzero number. Constructing that point for the permanent — with $r = \Omega(n^2)$ from a matching of only $O(\log n)$ entries — is the next section's work, and it is where the permanent's structure does all the heavy lifting.
