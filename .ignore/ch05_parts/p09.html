8. Hiding the block sum inside one permanent (Lemma 6.1)

Corollary 5.2 controls the critical locus of a sum of blockwise minor sums. Proposition 4.3, however, insists on a single affine specialization of one permanent. This section closes the gap: it constructs a constant matrix $U$ such that bordering a variable matrix by $U$, an all-ones block, and a zero block produces a permanent equal (up to a nonzero constant) to exactly the desired block sum. The difficulty is that a permanent adds up everything — including cross-block matchings we do not want. The construction makes all cross-block contributions cancel, using $d$-th roots of unity.

8.1 The cancellation, visible at $4 \times 4$

The required block-selective cancellation is already visible in a hand-checkable identity: $$\operatorname{per}\begin{pmatrix} u & v & 1 & 1\\ w & z & 1 & 1\\ p & q & 2 & -2\\ r & s & 2 & -2 \end{pmatrix} = -8(uz + vw) + 2(ps + qr).$$ Expand the permanent over which two rows are matched to the first two columns (the analogue of Laplace expansion — legal for permanents with no signs to track). The complementary two rows are matched to the constant columns, contributing the permanent of a constant $2\times 2$ block; Fig. 14 traces the three cases.

This example has degree $d = 2$, so $\log_2(d-1) = 0$ and it proves nothing by itself; its role is to exhibit the target phenomenon. The general construction must reproduce the same selectivity at degree $d \approx \tfrac{1}{4}\log_2 n$ with $b$ blocks — and the ad hoc constants $\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\2&-2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$ become roots of unity.

8.2 The construction

Let $b, t, s \ge 1$; partition $[r]$, $r = bt$, into $b$ blocks $R_1,\dots,R_b$ of $t$ consecutive rows; and let $1 \le d \le \min\{t,s\}$. For $T \subseteq [r]$, let $\mathbf{1}_T \in \mathbb{C}^r$ be the indicator vector ($1$ on $T$, $0$ elsewhere). Fix a primitive $d$-th root of unity $\zeta = e^{2\pi i/d}$ and set $$\theta = (-1)^{d+1} 2^d.$$ Define the constant matrix $U \in \mathbb{C}^{r \times (r-d)}$ by listing its columns:

  • saturator columns: for each $h \in [b]$, take $t - d$ copies of $\mathbf{1}_{R_h}$;
  • filter columns: for each $h = 2,\dots,b$ and each $j = 0,\dots,d-1$, take $\mathbf{1}_{R_1 \cup \dots \cup R_{h-1}} + 2\zeta^j\,\mathbf{1}_{R_h}$.

The first family has $b(t-d)$ columns and the second $(b-1)d$, totalling $b(t-d) + (b-1)d = bt - d = r - d$ as required. (If $t = d$ the first family is empty; if $b = 1$ the second is.) Fig. 15 draws the bordered matrix these columns live in.

Lemma 6.1. There are $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_b \in \mathbb{C}^\times$ such that for the square matrix of size $r + s - d$ $$B(X) = \begin{pmatrix} X & U \\ \mathbf{1} & 0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad X = (x_{ia})_{i \in [r],\, a \in [s]},$$ (lower-left: the $(s-d)\times s$ all-ones matrix; lower-right: the $(s-d)\times(r-d)$ zero matrix), one has $$\operatorname{per}(B(X)) \;=\; c \sum_{h=1}^{b} \lambda_h\, M_{t,s,d}\bigl(X_{R_h, [s]}\bigr), \qquad c = (s-d)!\,(t-d)!\,(t!)^{b-1} \ne 0. \tag{6.1}$$ Explicitly, $\lambda_1 = \theta^{b-1}$ and $\lambda_h = \theta^{b-h}(1+\theta)^{h-2}$ for $2 \le h \le b$. In particular $\operatorname{per}(B(X))$, a polynomial of degree $d$, is a nonzero scalar multiple of an affine specialization of $\operatorname{per}_{r+s-d}$ — the specialization fixes constants and keeps the variables $x_{ia}$.

Step 1: expand over the variable columns. A permanent term of $B(X)$ is a bijection between rows and columns. The lower-right block is zero, so each of the $s-d$ lower rows must be matched into the first $s$ columns. That leaves exactly $d$ of the variable columns — call the set $J$, $|J| = d$ — for the upper rows, matched by some $d$-set $I \subseteq [r]$ of upper rows; the remaining $r - d$ upper rows (the set $I^{c}$) must take the $r-d$ columns of $U$; and the lower rows fill the remaining $s - d$ variable columns, an all-ones square block with permanent $(s-d)!$. Since the permanent has no signs, the sum factors over this three-way split: $$\operatorname{per}(B(X)) = (s-d)! \sum_{\substack{I \subseteq [r],\, J \subseteq [s] \\ |I| = |J| = d}} \operatorname{per}\bigl(U_{I^c, [r-d]}\bigr)\, \operatorname{per}\bigl(X_{I,J}\bigr). \tag{6.2}$$ Everything now rides on the constant minors $\operatorname{per}(U_{I^c})$: we must show they vanish unless $I$ lies inside a single block $R_h$, and compute them when it does.

Step 2: a generating algebra for all minors at once. Introduce the commutative square-zero algebra $$S = \mathbb{C}[z_1,\dots,z_r]/(z_1^2,\dots,z_r^2).$$ Its monomials $z_K = \prod_{i \in K} z_i$, indexed by subsets $K \subseteq [r]$, form a vector-space basis; any product revisiting a variable dies. The utility: for an $r \times \nu$ matrix $A$ with column forms $\sum_{i=1}^{r} A_{ij} z_i$, expanding the product of all column forms and keeping the coefficient of $z_K$ ($|K| = \nu$) enumerates exactly the ways to give each column a distinct row from $K$: $$[z_K] \prod_{j=1}^{\nu}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{r} A_{ij} z_i\Bigr) = \operatorname{per}(A_{K, [\nu]}). \tag{6.3}$$ (Choosing row $i$ twice contributes $z_i^2 = 0$; each surviving assignment is a bijection $[\nu] \to K$ weighted by its entries — the permanent. For $\nu = 0$ both sides are $1$.) So one product in $S$ encodes all the minors of $U$ simultaneously. Set, for each block, $$y_h = \sum_{i \in R_h} z_i.$$ Because the $z_i$ commute and square to zero, the multinomial theorem collapses: $$y_h^{\,j} = j! \sum_{\substack{K \subseteq R_h \\ |K| = j}} z_K \quad (0 \le j \le t), \qquad y_h^{\,t+1} = 0. \tag{6.4}$$ In particular $y_h^t = t!\, z_{R_h}$: the $t$-th power saturates the block — multiplying it by any further $z_i$ with $i \in R_h$ gives zero. Saturation is the mechanism of the whole proof: a saturated block can absorb nothing more.

Step 3: the root-of-unity filter. The column form of a saturator column $\mathbf{1}_{R_h}$ is $y_h$; that of a filter column $\mathbf{1}_{R_1\cup\cdots\cup R_{h-1}} + 2\zeta^j \mathbf{1}_{R_h}$ is $Y_{h-1} + 2\zeta^j y_h$, where $Y_{h-1} = y_1 + \cdots + y_{h-1}$. The classical factorization $\prod_{j=0}^{d-1}(T - \zeta^j) = T^d - 1$ (both sides are monic of degree $d$ with the same $d$ roots), homogenized with $T = A/C$ and cleared of denominators, gives the polynomial identity $\prod_{j=0}^{d-1}(A - \zeta^j C) = A^d - C^d$; applying it in the commutative ring $S$ with $A = Y_{h-1}$, $C = -2y_h$ yields $$\prod_{j=0}^{d-1}\bigl(Y_{h-1} + 2\zeta^j y_h\bigr) = Y_{h-1}^{\,d} - (-2y_h)^d = Y_{h-1}^{\,d} + \theta\, y_h^{\,d}. \tag{8.a}$$ All mixed products of $Y_{h-1}$ and $y_h$ have cancelled — that is what the fan of $d$ rotated columns buys (Fig. 16). Multiplying the column forms of all $r-d$ columns of $U$: $$\prod_{j=1}^{r-d}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{r}U_{ij}z_i\Bigr) = \Bigl(\prod_{h=1}^{b} y_h^{\,t-d}\Bigr)\prod_{h=2}^{b}\bigl(Y_{h-1}^{\,d} + \theta\,y_h^{\,d}\bigr). \tag{6.5}$$

Step 4: expand the filter product by induction on blocks. For $1 \le h \le b$ set $$C_h = \Bigl(\prod_{g=1}^{h} y_g^{\,t-d}\Bigr)\prod_{g=2}^{h}\bigl(Y_{g-1}^{\,d} + \theta\,y_g^{\,d}\bigr),$$ so (6.5) says the full product equals $C_b$. We claim by induction that $$C_h = \sum_{i=1}^{h} \lambda^{(h)}_i\, y_i^{\,t-d} \prod_{\substack{g \in [h] \\ g \ne i}} y_g^{\,t},$$ i.e. exactly one block is left unsaturated, with weights $\lambda^{(h)}_i$. For $h=1$: $C_1 = y_1^{t-d}$ with $\lambda^{(1)}_1 = 1$. Assume the claim for $h$; by definition $C_{h+1} = C_h\, y_{h+1}^{\,t-d}\,(Y_h^{\,d} + \theta\, y_{h+1}^{\,d})$. Two contributions:

  • The $\theta y_{h+1}^{\,d}$ term combines with $y_{h+1}^{t-d}$ to give $y_{h+1}^{t}$ — the new block saturates — and multiplies every old coefficient by $\theta$.
  • In the $Y_h^{\,d}$ term, look at the $i$-th summand of $C_h$: every old block except $R_i$ is already saturated there, so any monomial of $Y_h^{\,d} = (y_1 + \cdots + y_h)^d$ containing some $y_g$ with $g \ne i$ dies against $y_g^{\,t}$ (by (6.4), $y_g^{t+1} = 0$). The only survivor is the pure power $y_i^{\,d}$ (multinomial coefficient $1$), which saturates block $i$: $y_i^{t-d}\,y_i^{\,d} = y_i^{\,t}$. The new block keeps its factor $y_{h+1}^{\,t-d}$ — it is now the unsaturated one.

Collecting: $$\lambda^{(h+1)}_i = \theta\,\lambda^{(h)}_i \ (1 \le i \le h), \qquad \lambda^{(h+1)}_{h+1} = \sum_{i=1}^{h}\lambda^{(h)}_i.$$ With $S_h = \sum_i \lambda^{(h)}_i$: $S_1 = 1$ and $S_{h+1} = \theta S_h + S_h = (1+\theta)S_h$, so $S_h = (1+\theta)^{h-1}$. Unwinding at $h = b$: $$\lambda_1 = \theta^{\,b-1}, \qquad \lambda_h = \theta^{\,b-h}(1+\theta)^{h-2} \quad (2 \le h \le b).$$ All are nonzero: $\theta = \pm 2^d \ne 0$, and $|\theta| = 2^d \gt 1$ forces $1 + \theta \ne 0$. The induction and (6.5) give $$\prod_{j=1}^{r-d}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{r}U_{ij}z_i\Bigr) = \sum_{h=1}^{b} \lambda_h\, y_h^{\,t-d} \prod_{g \ne h} y_g^{\,t}. \tag{6.6}$$

Step 5: read off the minors and conclude. Let $I \subseteq [r]$, $|I| = d$. By (6.3), the coefficient of $z_{I^c}$ on the left of (6.6) is $\operatorname{per}(U_{I^c,[r-d]})$. On the right, the $h$-th summand saturates every block except $R_h$ — it contains $z_{R_g}$ in full for all $g \ne h$ — so it can contribute to $z_{I^c}$ (whose complement is $I$) only if $I \subseteq R_h$. In that case (6.4) gives the coefficient $(t-d)!$ from $y_h^{\,t-d}$ (the subset $K = R_h \setminus I$) and $t!$ from each of the other $b-1$ blocks: $$\operatorname{per}(U_{I^c,[r-d]}) = \begin{cases} \lambda_h\,(t-d)!\,(t!)^{b-1}, & I \subseteq R_h,\\[2pt] 0, & I \text{ meets more than one block.} \end{cases}$$ Substituting into (6.2) and grouping the surviving $I$ by their block: $$\operatorname{per}(B(X)) = (s-d)!\,(t-d)!\,(t!)^{b-1} \sum_{h=1}^{b} \lambda_h \sum_{\substack{I \subseteq R_h,\ J \subseteq [s]\\ |I|=|J|=d}}\operatorname{per}(X_{I,J}).$$ The inner sum is $M_{t,s,d}(X_{R_h,[s]})$ by (5.1), and the factorial prefactor $c$ is a nonzero rational number. This proves (6.1). ∎

Toy checks (run in §15). (i) Taking $b = 2$, $t = s = d = 2$ gives $\theta = -4$, $\lambda_1 = \theta = -4$, $\lambda_2 = 1$, $c = 0!\,0!\,2! = 2$, recovering exactly the $4\times4$ identity of §8.1: $c\lambda_1 = -8$ and $c\lambda_2 = 2$. (ii) The smallest instance with a genuine root-of-unity filter, $b=2$, $t=d=3$, $s=4$, has $\theta = (-1)^4 2^3 = 8$, $\lambda = (8, 1)$, $c = 1!\cdot 0!\cdot (3!)^1 = 6$, and §15 verifies (6.1) there by exact arithmetic in $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta]$, $\zeta^2 + \zeta + 1 = 0$, including that every mixed minor of $U$ vanishes exactly.
