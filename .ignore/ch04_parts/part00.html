One factor, infinitely many groups

A rigorous, self-contained walkthrough of Chapter 4 of Ten Advances in Mathematics and Theoretical Computer Science (pp. 94–111) — the counterexample to Connes's rigidity conjecture — written for a reader with a PhD in some quantitative field but not in operator algebras. Every symbol is defined before use; everything provable at graduate-core level is proved (the group-factor ICC criterion, Fourier duality, the entire binary-carry construction, all orbit and spectral estimates); the five deep imports (Connes–Jones, Ershov–Jaikin-Zapirain, Suslin, the spectral theorem, and two property-(T) inheritance facts) are stated precisely and flagged; and the construction's identities and counts are re-verified by machine in §10.

0. Motivation — can you hear the shape of a group?

Kac's famous question about drums — can two different shapes sound identical? — has a noncommutative sibling. To every countable group $G$ one can attach an analytic object $L(G)$, its group von Neumann algebra: roughly, the ring of all “infinite linear combinations” of group elements that act boundedly on the Hilbert space $\ell^2(G)$. This object is the group's harmonic shadow. It forgets a lot: by a celebrated theorem of Connes, every amenable infinite group with infinite conjugacy classes — $\mathbb Z$'s lamplighter cousins, solvable groups, and countless others — casts the same shadow, a single algebra called the hyperfinite II$_1$ factor. The shadow of an amenable group remembers essentially nothing.

Rigidity theory is the study of the opposite regime. Kazhdan's property (T) — the representation-theoretic rigidity we met in Chapter 3's explainer — was expected to prevent the collapse: a property-(T) group is so stiff that its shadow ought to determine it completely. Connes conjectured exactly this in the early 1980s:

Conjecture 1.1 (Connes's rigidity conjecture). Let $G$ and $H$ be countable ICC groups with property (T) (ICC: every nonidentity conjugacy class is infinite). If $L(G) \cong L(H)$, then $G \cong H$.

The conjecture stood for four decades, supported by spectacular partial evidence: Popa's deformation/rigidity theory recovered groups and their actions from crossed-product algebras; Ioana proved that Bernoulli actions of ICC property-(T) groups are $W^*$-superrigid; and in 2023 Chifan, Ioana, Osin, and Sun produced the first property-(T) groups that genuinely are determined by their von Neumann algebras. Popa proved in his 2006 ICM address that the map $G \mapsto L(G)$ is at most countable-to-one on ICC property-(T) groups, and asked whether its fibers must be finite. Chapter 4 demolishes the conjecture in the strongest possible way:

Theorem 1.2 (proved in §9). There exist finitely generated ICC property-(T) groups $\Lambda, \Gamma_0, \Gamma_1, \Gamma_2, \dots$, pairwise nonisomorphic, with $$L(\Gamma_n) \;\cong\; L(\Lambda) \qquad (n \ge 0).$$ Moreover each $\Gamma_n$ contains a copy of $\Gamma_0$ of index $2^{4n}$, so the $\Gamma_n$ are mutually commensurable. In particular the fiber of the group-factor map at $L(\Lambda)$ is countably infinite: Popa's countability bound is sharp, and even the finite-to-one weakening of Connes's conjecture fails.

What is remarkable is how cheap the counterexample turns out to be. No quantum machinery, no exotic cohomology — the whole thing rests on a trick you can explain on four points. Here it is. Take the set $\{0,1\}^2$ with the uniform measure. Coordinatewise addition mod $2$ makes it the Klein four-group $\mathbb Z/2 \times \mathbb Z/2$. But the twisted law $$(x, y) \diamond (x', y') = (x + x',\; y + y' + xx')$$ makes it $\mathbb Z/4$ — the extra term $xx'$ is precisely the carry bit of binary addition ($x$ is the ones digit, $y$ the twos digit). Two genuinely different groups; one and the same measure space (§4). The proof strategy is now a pipeline of three moves:

  • Move one (Fourier). For a countable abelian group $A$ acted on by a group $K$, the von Neumann algebra $L(A \rtimes K)$ can be computed by Fourier transform: it equals a crossed product $L^\infty(\widehat A) \rtimes K$ built from the compact dual $\widehat A$ — but only from its measure space and $K$-action, never from its compact group law (§3). The shadow is measure-theoretic; the group law is invisible to it.
  • Move two (carry). Globalize the four-point trick $K$-equivariantly: put infinitely many different compact abelian group structures $C_0, C_1, C_2, \dots$ on one fixed measured $K$-space, by inserting the binary carry at coordinate position $n$ (§6, §8). Their Pontryagin duals $E_n$ are genuinely different discrete groups; the semidirect products $\Gamma_n = E_n \rtimes K$ all have the factor of $\Lambda = D \rtimes K$, where $D$ is the untwisted model. An order-four element (the carry made algebra) distinguishes $\Gamma_n$ from $\Lambda$, and an intrinsic counting invariant of size $2^{4n}$ recovers $n$.
  • Move three (rigidity bookkeeping). None of this would touch Connes's conjecture unless the groups were ICC with property (T). The acting group $K$ is a congruence subgroup of $\mathrm{SL}_4(\mathbb Z[t])$ — property (T) by Ershov–Jaikin-Zapirain (the same import as Chapter 3) plus Suslin's theorem; ICC and torsion-free by direct matrix arguments (§5). The semidirect product $\Lambda$ is ICC by an infinite-orbit computation, and has property (T) by a beautiful hands-on spectral estimate whose engine is a quarter-support bound for quadratic Boolean polynomials — and which works precisely because the matrices are $4 \times 4$: rank $4$ is the smallest rank for which the numbers come out positive (§7). Everything then transfers to every $\Gamma_n$ through the common factor via the Connes–Jones theorem (§2).

The plan: §1 builds group von Neumann algebras from nothing and proves the Murray–von Neumann ICC criterion. §2 recalls property (T) (Chapter 3's primer covers the basics; here we add relative property (T) and the Connes–Jones bridge). §3 proves the Fourier mechanism, §4 the four-point toy. §5–§7 build $K$, the carry group, and the rigidity proofs; §8 produces the infinite family and its distinguishing invariant; §9 assembles Theorem 1.2 and maps the landscape; §10 re-verifies the constructions by machine; §11 is the honest scorecard.
