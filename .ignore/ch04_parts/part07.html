9.1 The rigidity landscape after Theorem 1.2

Settled. Connes's Conjecture 1.1 is false (already by the pair $\Lambda, \Gamma_0$ — Corollary 5.10). Stronger: even the finite-to-one weakening fails, since $L(\Lambda)$ has countably infinitely many pairwise nonisomorphic ICC property-(T) realizations — so Popa's countable-to-one theorem [Pop07] is sharp, answering his finite-to-one question [Pop13] in the negative. The examples are as tame as could be: mutually commensurable, explicitly presented, and distinguished by nothing subtler than counting torsion orbits.

Sharpened context (Fig. 22). Infinite fibers of $G \mapsto L(G)$ were known outside the property-(T) world — Ioana–Popa–Vaes produced infinitely many groups sharing the factor of $H_0 \wr \mathrm{PSL}_n(\mathbb Z)$ [IPV13] — but wreath products are never property (T); the new feature here is an infinite fiber inside the ICC property-(T) class, the exact class Popa's countability bound governs. In the opposite direction, rigidity is real: some ICC property-(T) groups are $W^*$-superrigid (Chifan–Ioana–Osin–Sun [CIOS23]), and Ioana's theorem [Ioa11] recovers Bernoulli actions of ICC (T) groups from their crossed products. So the corrected picture: property (T) is compatible with maximal rigidity and with countably infinite ambiguity — it guarantees neither. What decides which, group by group, is open.

Open, highlighted by the result. Which II$_1$ factors have infinite group-fibers; whether the specific factor $L(\Lambda)$ has uncountably many non-(T) realizations (the countability bound uses (T)); effective invariants distinguishing group factors in general; and the amenable-vs-rigid frontier: a structural characterization of the $W^*$-superrigid property-(T) groups. (Historical note from the chapter's acknowledgments: an independent, concurrent counterexample to Connes's conjecture was found by Shuoxing Zhou — developed in part with AI assistance — a sign of how quickly this forty-year-old wall crumbled once the right crack appeared.)

Honest limitations of this exposition. First, the operator-algebra layer is deliberately shallow: we define $L(G)$ and crossed products concretely and prove exactly what the argument needs (Lemma A, Props. B, F, G), but the theory of II$_1$ factors — including the meaning of Connes–Jones property (T) for factors — stays behind Import 1. Second, the machine checks of §10 validate the finitary skeleton (identities, counts, group axioms on truncations, sampled equivariance); they do not, and cannot, check the analytic statements (spectral theorem, Haar uniqueness, the imports). Third, the proof of Theorem 1.2 as presented follows the chapter's architecture faithfully, including its division of labor between the primes $2$ and $3$; no attempt is made to survey alternative routes. Within those boundaries, every step from the definitions to Theorem 1.2 is on the page.
