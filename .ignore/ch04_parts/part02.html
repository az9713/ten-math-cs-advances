3. Fourier duality: what the factor remembers

The engine of the counterexample is a precise statement of what $L(G)$ can see when $G = A \rtimes K$ is a semidirect product with abelian normal part. The answer: it sees the dual probability space of $A$ and the $K$-action on it — and nothing else.

3.1 Pontryagin duality for discrete abelian groups

Write $\mathbb T = \{z \in \mathbb C : |z| = 1\}$, a group under multiplication. For a countable discrete abelian group $A$, the Pontryagin dual is $$\widehat A \;=\; \mathrm{Hom}(A, \mathbb T) \;=\; \{\text{characters } \chi : A \to \mathbb T,\ \chi(a+b) = \chi(a)\chi(b)\},$$ topologized by pointwise convergence. As a closed subset of the compact product $\mathbb T^A$, the dual $\widehat A$ is a compact abelian group (pointwise multiplication); it is metrizable since $A$ is countable, and it carries a unique translation-invariant Borel probability measure $m_{\widehat A}$, its Haar measure (for the concrete duals below we exhibit it directly — no existence theorem needed). The trivial character is $\mathbf 1(a) \equiv 1$. Three examples calibrate the notion (Fig. 5): $\widehat{\mathbb Z} \cong \mathbb T$ (a character of $\mathbb Z$ is determined by $\chi(1)$); the dual of a finite abelian group is isomorphic to itself; and — the case that runs this whole chapter — the dual of a countable $\mathbb F_2$-vector space $A = \bigoplus_{i \in I} \mathbb F_2$ is the compact product $$\widehat A \;\cong\; \prod_{i \in I} \mathbb F_2, \qquad \chi_x(a) = (-1)^{\sum_i x_i a_i},$$ with Haar measure = the fair-coin product measure: each dual coordinate is an independent unbiased bit.

Two small facts make the duality usable, and both are provable from scratch:

Lemma D (characters separate points). Let $A$ be a discrete abelian group and $a_0 \neq 0$. Then some $\chi \in \widehat A$ has $\chi(a_0) \neq 1$.

First define $\chi$ on the cyclic subgroup $\langle a_0\rangle$: if $a_0$ has finite order $n \ge 2$, send $a_0 \mapsto e^{2\pi i/n}$; if infinite order, send $a_0 \mapsto -1$. Now extend to all of $A$ one element at a time, using that $\mathbb T$ is divisible (every $z$ has an $n$-th root): given $\chi$ on a subgroup $H$ and $b \notin H$, let $n = \min\{n \ge 1 : nb \in H\}$ if it exists, pick $\zeta$ with $\zeta^n = \chi(nb)$, and set $\chi(h + kb) = \chi(h)\zeta^k$ — well-defined because any two representations of the same element differ by a multiple of $(nb, -n)$, on which the two factors cancel; if no multiple of $b$ lies in $H$, choose $\zeta = 1$. Zorn's lemma (or a straight enumeration of countable $A$) completes the extension to $A$. ∎

Lemma E (Plancherel for countable discrete $A$). The evaluation functions $\mathrm{ev}_a : \widehat A \to \mathbb C$, $\mathrm{ev}_a(\chi) = \chi(a)$, form an orthonormal basis of $L^2(\widehat A, m_{\widehat A})$. Consequently $$U : \ell^2(A) \longrightarrow L^2(\widehat A, m_{\widehat A}), \qquad U\delta_a = \mathrm{ev}_a$$ is a unitary, and it converts the translation operator $\lambda_A(a)$ into multiplication by the function $\mathrm{ev}_a$.

Orthonormality. $\mathrm{ev}_a\overline{\mathrm{ev}_b} = \mathrm{ev}_{a-b}$ pointwise, so it suffices to show $\int \mathrm{ev}_c\, dm = 0$ for $c \neq 0$ (and $= 1$ for $c = 0$, clear). By Lemma D choose $\chi_0$ with $\chi_0(c) \neq 1$. Translation invariance of Haar measure under the shift $\chi \mapsto \chi_0\chi$ gives $$\int \chi(c)\, dm(\chi) = \int (\chi_0\chi)(c)\, dm(\chi) = \chi_0(c)\int \chi(c)\, dm(\chi),$$ and $(1 - \chi_0(c))\int \mathrm{ev}_c\,dm = 0$ forces the integral to vanish.

Completeness. The linear span of the $\mathrm{ev}_a$ is a unital $*$-subalgebra of $C(\widehat A)$ (products and conjugates are again evaluations) which separates the points of $\widehat A$ (two characters differing somewhere differ on some $\mathrm{ev}_a$ — tautologically). By the Stone–Weierstrass theorem (standard analysis, used as a black box) the span is uniformly dense in $C(\widehat A)$, hence dense in $L^2$. An orthonormal family with dense span is a basis.

The conversion. $U\lambda_A(a)\delta_b = U\delta_{a+b} = \mathrm{ev}_{a+b} = \mathrm{ev}_a \cdot \mathrm{ev}_b = \mathrm{ev}_a \cdot U\delta_b$, and both sides are bounded, so $U\lambda_A(a)U^* = M_{\mathrm{ev}_a}$, multiplication by $\mathrm{ev}_a$. ∎

3.2 Crossed products, and the identity $L(A \rtimes K) \cong L^\infty(\widehat A) \rtimes K$

Now let a countable group $K$ act on $A$ by automorphisms $a \mapsto k \cdot a$. The semidirect product $A \rtimes K$ is the set $A \times K$ with $(a, k)(b, h) = (a + k\cdot b,\, kh)$. The action dualizes to $\widehat A$ and to functions on it: $$(k \cdot \chi)(a) = \chi(k^{-1}\cdot a), \qquad \alpha_k(f)(\chi) = f(k^{-1}\cdot\chi).$$ Each $k$ acts on $\widehat A$ by a homeomorphic group automorphism preserving $m_{\widehat A}$ (the pushforward of Haar is translation-invariant, hence equals Haar by uniqueness). The crossed product $L^\infty(\widehat A, m) \rtimes K$ is the von Neumann algebra on $L^2(\widehat A) \otimes \ell^2(K)$ generated by the multiplication operators $M_f \otimes 1$ ($f \in L^\infty$) and the unitaries $$v_k \;=\; u_k \otimes \lambda_K(k), \qquad (u_k \xi)(\chi) = \xi(k^{-1}\cdot\chi),$$ which satisfy the defining relations $v_k (M_f\otimes 1) v_k^* = M_{\alpha_k(f)}\otimes 1$ and $v_kv_h = v_{kh}$.

Proposition F (the chapter's equation (2.1)). There is a trace-preserving isomorphism $$L(A \rtimes K) \;\cong\; L^\infty(\widehat A, m_{\widehat A}) \rtimes K.$$

Identify $\ell^2(A \rtimes K) \cong \ell^2(A)\otimes\ell^2(K)$ by $\delta_{(a,k)} \mapsto \delta_a \otimes \delta_k$. Compute the group unitaries in these coordinates: $$\lambda(a, 1)\,\delta_{(b,h)} = \delta_{(a+b,\,h)}, \qquad \lambda(0, k)\,\delta_{(b,h)} = \delta_{(k\cdot b,\, kh)},$$ so $\lambda(a,1) = \lambda_A(a)\otimes 1$ and $\lambda(0,k) = \sigma_k \otimes \lambda_K(k)$, where $\sigma_k\delta_b = \delta_{k\cdot b}$ permutes the $A$-basis. Since $(a,k) = (a,1)(0,k)$, these operators generate $\lambda(A\rtimes K)$, hence (taking double commutants) all of $L(A\rtimes K)$.

Now conjugate by the Fourier unitary $U\otimes 1$ of Lemma E. First, $U\lambda_A(a)U^* = M_{\mathrm{ev}_a}$. Second, $U\sigma_k U^* = u_k$: indeed $U\sigma_k\delta_b = \mathrm{ev}_{k\cdot b}$ and $(u_k\,\mathrm{ev}_b)(\chi) = \mathrm{ev}_b(k^{-1}\chi) = \chi(k\cdot b) = \mathrm{ev}_{k\cdot b}(\chi)$ — wait, one must check $(k^{-1}\cdot\chi)(b) = \chi(k \cdot b)$: by definition $(k^{-1}\cdot\chi)(b) = \chi((k^{-1})^{-1}\cdot b) = \chi(k\cdot b)$. Correct. So conjugation carries the generators of $L(A\rtimes K)$ exactly onto the generators $M_{\mathrm{ev}_a}\otimes 1$ and $v_k$ of the crossed product; the functions $\mathrm{ev}_a$ generate $L^\infty(\widehat A)$ as a von Neumann algebra (their span is weak-$*$ dense, by Lemma E and density of $C(\widehat A)$ in $L^\infty$ in the relevant topology), so the two von Neumann algebras coincide after conjugation. The canonical traces match: $\tau(\,\cdot\,) = \langle \,\cdot\,(\mathbf 1_{\widehat A}\otimes\delta_1), \mathbf 1_{\widehat A}\otimes\delta_1\rangle$ corresponds to $\langle\,\cdot\,\delta_{(0,1)},\delta_{(0,1)}\rangle$ since $U\delta_0 = \mathrm{ev}_0 = \mathbf 1_{\widehat A}$. ∎

The right-hand side of Proposition F is built from three pieces of data: the probability space $(\widehat A, m_{\widehat A})$, the measure-preserving $K$-action on it, and nothing else. In particular the compact group law on $\widehat A$ has disappeared. That observation, made precise, is the mechanism of the whole chapter:

Proposition G (transport principle). Let $A, A'$ be countable discrete abelian $K$-modules, and let $\theta : \widehat A \to \widehat{A'}$ be a bijection modulo null sets which is measurable with measurable inverse, measure-preserving ($\theta_*m_{\widehat A} = m_{\widehat{A'}}$), and $K$-equivariant ($\theta(k\cdot\chi) = k\cdot\theta(\chi)$ a.e.). Then $$L(A \rtimes K) \;\cong\; L(A' \rtimes K).$$ No compatibility whatsoever between the group laws of $\widehat A$ and $\widehat{A'}$ is required.

Composition $W : L^2(\widehat{A}) \to L^2(\widehat{A'})$, $W\xi = \xi\circ\theta^{-1}$, is unitary (measure preservation) and satisfies $W M_f W^* = M_{f\circ\theta^{-1}}$ and $W u_k W^* = u'_k$ (equivariance: $(W u_k \xi)(\chi') = \xi(k^{-1}\cdot\theta^{-1}\chi') = \xi(\theta^{-1}(k^{-1}\cdot\chi'))$). Hence $W\otimes 1$ conjugates the generators of $L^\infty(\widehat A)\rtimes K$ onto those of $L^\infty(\widehat{A'})\rtimes K$. Combine with Proposition F on both sides. ∎

4. The four-point toy: one measure, two group laws

Everything hinges on the possibility of genuinely different compact group laws with the same measure and the same symmetries. The minimal example has four points, and deserves to be savored (Fig. 7). On the set $\mathbb F_2^2 = \{(x,y) : x, y \in \{0,1\}\}$ with the uniform probability measure, consider two operations: $$(x,y) + (x',y') = (x + x',\, y + y'), \qquad (x,y) \diamond (x',y') = (x + x',\, y + y' + xx'),$$ all coordinates computed mod $2$. The first is the Klein four-group $\mathbb Z/2 \times \mathbb Z/2$: every element has order $\le 2$. For the second, write $\tilde s \in \{0,1\} \subset \mathbb Z$ for the standard representative of $s \in \mathbb F_2$; then $$(x, y) \longmapsto \tilde x + 2\tilde y \pmod 4$$ is an isomorphism from $(\mathbb F_2^2, \diamond)$ to $\mathbb Z/4$ (machine-checked over all $16$ pairs in §10; conceptually it is the binary-carry identity $$\tilde s + \tilde{s'} \;=\; \widetilde{s + s'} + 2\,\tilde s\,\tilde{s'} \pmod 4,$$ “ones digits add, and their carry $ss'$ feeds the twos digit”). The element $(1,0)$ has $\diamond$-order $4$. Two facts stare at each other:

  • $(\mathbb F_2^2, +)$ and $(\mathbb F_2^2, \diamond)$ are not isomorphic groups (exponent $2$ versus an element of order $4$);
  • they are identical as measured spaces — literally the same four points with the same uniform measure, and for either law the measure is the Haar measure.

By the transport principle (with trivial $K$), the “group algebras” built measure-theoretically from the two laws coincide — for four points this is no surprise and no counterexample (finite groups are nowhere near ICC, and both algebras are just $\mathbb C^4$). The content of the chapter is engineering: embed this toy in a setting where (i) the ambient measured space carries an action of a property-(T) group $K$ commuting with both group laws, (ii) the resulting semidirect products are ICC with property (T), and (iii) the difference between the laws survives in the discrete duals as torsion — the order-$4$ carry element. Then Fourier duality (Propositions F and G) turns “same measured space” into “same factor”, while Pontryagin duality preserves “different groups”. That engineering occupies the rest of the document.
