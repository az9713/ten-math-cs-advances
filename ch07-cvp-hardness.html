0. Motivation — the nearest point in a crystal

Take an infinite, perfectly regular grid of points in $n$-dimensional space — think of the atoms of an ideal crystal, extended forever. Now mark an arbitrary location $t$ in that space, not necessarily a grid point. Question: which grid point is nearest to $t$, and how far away is it? In the plane you would answer instantly with a glance (Fig. 1). The astonishing content of this chapter is that in high dimension, even a wildly approximate version of that glance — “is the nearest point within distance $r$, or is every point at least $n^{1/400} \cdot r$ away?” — is as hard as any problem whose answers can be checked efficiently. Hard in the precise, universal sense: NP-hard.

Why should anyone care about approximate nearest lattice points? Because a remarkable amount of modern computation leans on them. The Lenstra–Lenstra–Lovász basis-reduction algorithm (1982) — which finds somewhat close lattice points in polynomial time — powered polynomial factorization, integer programming in fixed dimension, and famous cryptanalyses. In the other direction, the hardness of lattice problems is the security foundation of post-quantum cryptography: the encryption and signature schemes standardized by NIST in 2024 (the FIPS 203 and 204 module-lattice standards) rest on the presumed intractability of structured lattice problems. Those cryptographic constructions use average-case assumptions rather than worst-case NP-hardness, but the two questions bracket the same landscape: which approximation regimes for lattice problems are genuinely intractable? This chapter moves the unconditional frontier for the central such problem.

Here is the landscape before and after (Fig. 2). Exact CVP was proved NP-hard by van Emde Boas in 1981. Approximating within any fixed constant factor was proved NP-hard by Arora, Babai, Stern, and Sweedyk in 1997. Dinur, Kindler, Raz, and Safra (2003) reached factors of the shape $n^{a/\log\log n}$ — growing faster than any power of $\log n$, yet with an exponent $a/\log\log n$ that tends to zero, so their result establishes hardness for no fixed power $n^c$ whatsoever. On the other side, Aharonov and Regev (2005) showed that at factor $C\sqrt{n}$ the problem sits in $\mathsf{NP} \cap \mathsf{coNP}$, so NP-hardness there would collapse the polynomial hierarchy's first level ($\mathsf{NP} = \mathsf{coNP}$) — a barrier. Between “no fixed power” and the $\sqrt{n}$ barrier lay a wide-open regime. The chapter's main theorem plants a flag inside it: hardness at the fixed power $n^{1/400}$, by a reduction that is deterministic and completely elementary.

The second surprise is how the theorem is proved. Since the 1990s, strong hardness-of-approximation results have almost universally passed through the PCP theorem — the deep machinery of probabilistically checkable proofs — or through conjectural strengthenings of it such as the Projection Games Conjecture, which was previously known to imply fixed-polynomial CVP hardness (Moshkovitz 2015, Mukhopadhyay 2022). This chapter uses none of that. The reduction goes directly from 3SAT, the original NP-complete problem, using mathematics a well-trained algebra student can verify line by line: polynomial interpolation over a finite field of characteristic two, power sums and Hankel matrices, Newton's identities, and valuations on rational function fields. For a mathematician reader this is a gift twice over — the whole proof is actually checkable, and the soundness argument turns out to be your machinery (separable polynomials, splitting fields, ramified valuations) deployed on a complexity-theoretic battlefield.

The paper we walk through proves, precisely:

Theorem 1 (main theorem; proved in §14). There is a deterministic polynomial-time mapping that assigns to each 3SAT formula $\varphi$ a nonsingular matrix $B \in \mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}$, a target $t \in \mathbb{Z}^n$, and a radius $r \in \mathbb{Q}_{>0}$ such that $$\varphi \text{ satisfiable} \;\Longrightarrow\; \mathrm{dist}_2(t, L(B)) \le r, \qquad \varphi \text{ unsatisfiable} \;\Longrightarrow\; \mathrm{dist}_2(t, L(B)) > n^{1/400}\, r.$$ Consequently $\mathrm{GapCVP}^{(2)}_{n^{1/400}}$ is NP-hard under deterministic polynomial-time many-one reductions. The same construction yields NP-hardness of approximating binary nearest codeword and syndrome decoding within $n^{1/200}$, and of closest vector in every fixed rational $\ell_p$ norm ($p \ge 1$) within $n^{1/(200p)}$.

1. Lattices, targets, and the gap version of “closest”

Everything begins with a single algebraic object.

Definition (lattice of a basis). Let $B \in \mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}$ be a nonsingular integer matrix (its columns are linearly independent over $\mathbb{Q}$, equivalently $\det B \neq 0$). The lattice generated by $B$ is the set of all integer combinations of its columns: $$L(B) \;=\; B\,\mathbb{Z}^n \;=\; \{\,Bz : z \in \mathbb{Z}^n\,\}.$$ The number $n$ is the rank (or dimension) of the lattice. The matrix $B$ is called a basis of the lattice.

A lattice is an additive subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}^n$: sums and negatives of lattice points are lattice points, because $Bz_1 + Bz_2 = B(z_1+z_2)$. Concretely it is a “crystal”: the grid $\mathbb{Z}^n$ pushed through the linear map $B$. One geometric habit to install immediately: the basis is not canonical. Different matrices generate the same point set — precisely, $L(B) = L(B')$ if and only if $B' = BU$ for some integer matrix $U$ with $\det U = \pm 1$ (such $U$ are called unimodular; they are exactly the integer matrices with integer inverses, since $U^{-1} = \mathrm{adj}(U)/\det U$). Fig. 4 shows one planar lattice wearing two very different bases. Algorithms are handed a basis, not the point set, and a skewed basis hides the geometry — that tension is the whole subject of lattice-basis reduction, and one reason lattice problems are hard.

Next, distance. For a vector $x = (x_1,\dots,x_n) \in \mathbb{R}^n$ write $\lVert x \rVert_2 = (\sum_j x_j^2)^{1/2}$ for the Euclidean norm. The Euclidean distance from a target to the lattice is $$\mathrm{dist}_2\big(t, L(B)\big) \;=\; \min_{z \in \mathbb{Z}^n} \big\lVert t - Bz \big\rVert_2 , \qquad t \in \mathbb{Q}^n.$$ Writing $\min$ rather than $\inf$ needs a one-line justification — a priori an infimum over infinitely many points might not be attained.

Proposition A (the minimum is attained, over finitely many candidates). Let $B \in \mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}$ be nonsingular and $t \in \mathbb{Q}^n$. Then for any $\rho \ge \mathrm{dist}$ to one fixed lattice point, the set $\{\,z \in \mathbb{Z}^n : \lVert t - Bz\rVert_2 \le \rho\,\}$ is finite, and $\mathrm{dist}_2(t,L(B))$ is attained by some lattice point.
Fix the candidate $z_0 = 0$ and let $\rho = \lVert t \rVert_2$. If $\lVert t - Bz \rVert_2 \le \rho$ then $\lVert Bz \rVert_2 \le 2\rho$ by the triangle inequality. Since $B$ is nonsingular it has a real inverse, and every linear map on $\mathbb{R}^n$ is Lipschitz: $\lVert z \rVert_2 = \lVert B^{-1}Bz \rVert_2 \le \lVert B^{-1}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}} \lVert Bz \rVert_2 \le 2\rho\,\lVert B^{-1}\rVert_{\mathrm{op}}$, where $\lVert \cdot \rVert_{\mathrm{op}}$ is the operator norm (finite, as the supremum of a continuous function on the unit sphere, which is compact). So all competing $z$ lie in a bounded region of $\mathbb{Z}^n$, of which there are finitely many points; a minimum over a finite nonempty set is attained. ∎

The closest vector problem (CVP) is the computational task: given $B$ and $t$, compute $\mathrm{dist}_2(t, L(B))$ (or the minimizing point). By Proposition A one can always solve it by finite search — but the search region contains on the order of $\rho^n$ candidates, exponential in the rank. The real question is what is achievable in time polynomial in the bit length of the input, and the modern formulation asks it approximately, with both thresholds supplied in the instance.

Definition ($\mathrm{GapCVP}$, the promise problem). Fix an approximation factor $\gamma(n) \ge 1$ (a function of the rank). An instance of $\mathrm{GapCVP}^{(2)}_{\gamma}$ consists of a nonsingular lattice basis $B \in \mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}$, a target $t \in \mathbb{Q}^n$, and a rational radius $r \in \mathbb{Q}_{>0}$, all encoded in binary. The task is to distinguish: $$\textsf{YES}: \;\; \mathrm{dist}_2(t, L(B)) \le r, \qquad\qquad \textsf{NO}: \;\; \mathrm{dist}_2(t, L(B)) > \gamma(n)\, r.$$ An algorithm must answer correctly whenever one of the two conditions holds; if $r < \mathrm{dist}_2(t,L(B)) \le \gamma(n) r$, either answer is accepted. The superscript $(2)$ records the Euclidean norm ($\ell_p$ variants appear in §15).

Why formulate approximation this way? Because a promise problem with a gap is exactly what an approximation algorithm would solve, so hardness of the former is hardness of the latter — we make that equivalence precise in §2 (Fig. 8). Note also a quiet convention used throughout: $n$, the parameter in the factor $n^{1/400}$, is the rank of the lattice actually output by the reduction, so the hardness statement is honest about dimension — nothing is hidden by padding.

Orientation for readers of Chapter 8's explainer. That chapter develops the volume-based geometry of numbers: Blichfeldt's and Minkowski's theorems, determinants as covolumes. None of that machinery is needed here — this chapter treats lattices purely as algebraic objects (integer images and mod-2 congruences), and is fully self-contained. What does carry over is the object itself and the habit of thinking of $\det B$ as an invariant of $L(B)$: unimodular basis changes leave $\lvert\det B\rvert$ untouched, a fact we will meet again in Lemma 6.

2. The complexity frame: 3SAT, reductions, and hardness of approximation

This section builds, from zero, the complexity-theoretic scaffolding a mathematician needs to read Theorem 1 as a theorem. Readers fluent in NP-hardness can skim to the promise-problem conventions at the end.

2.1 Decision problems, $\mathsf{P}$, and $\mathsf{NP}$

A decision problem is a set $\Pi$ of finite binary strings, called its YES-instances; the computational task is, given a string $x \in \{0,1\}^*$, to decide whether $x \in \Pi$. Any mathematical object we care about — a formula, a matrix of integers, a rational vector — is presented as a binary string by fixing a reasonable encoding, and $\lvert x\rvert$ denotes the string's length in bits. An algorithm runs in polynomial time if there is a constant $k$ such that on every input $x$ it halts within $O(\lvert x\rvert^k)$ elementary steps. The class $\mathsf{P}$ consists of the decision problems solvable in polynomial time.

The class $\mathsf{NP}$ consists of the problems whose YES-answers have short, checkable certificates: $\Pi \in \mathsf{NP}$ if there is a polynomial-time algorithm $V(x,w)$ (the verifier) and a constant $k$ such that $$x \in \Pi \iff \exists\, w \in \{0,1\}^{\le \lvert x \rvert^k}:\; V(x, w) \text{ accepts}.$$ The string $w$ is the certificate — for “this formula is satisfiable”, a satisfying assignment; for “this lattice has a point within distance $r$ of $t$”, the integer coordinate vector $z$ of such a point (checkable by computing $\lVert t - Bz\rVert_2^2$ exactly in rational arithmetic; a polynomial bit-size bound for a witness $z$ follows from the argument in Proposition A). Whether $\mathsf{P} = \mathsf{NP}$ — whether the existence of easily checkable certificates implies they are easy to find — is the central open problem of the field; every hardness statement below is calibrated against it.

2.2 The reference problem: 3SAT

Fix Boolean variables $v_1, \dots, v_m$, each taking values in $\{0,1\}$ (false/true). A literal is a variable $v_i$ or its negation $\lnot v_i$. A clause is a disjunction (OR) of at most three literals, e.g. $(v_1 \lor v_2 \lor \lnot v_3)$: it is satisfied by an assignment iff at least one of its literals evaluates to true. A 3SAT formula $\varphi$ is a conjunction (AND) of clauses $C_1, \dots, C_\ell$; an assignment $\sigma \in \{0,1\}^m$ satisfies $\varphi$ if it satisfies every clause. The decision problem 3SAT asks: does a satisfying assignment exist? Fig. 6 dissects the two-clause toy formula that will follow us through the whole chapter and into the machine-checked §17.

3SAT is obviously in $\mathsf{NP}$ ($\sigma$ is the certificate). What makes it the reference problem is the Cook–Levin theorem, the one classical result of this section we import rather than reprove (its proof — simulating an arbitrary polynomial-time verifier by a Boolean formula — is long but appears in every complexity textbook):

Import 1 (Cook 1971, Levin 1973; with the standard reduction to three literals). 3SAT is $\mathsf{NP}$-complete: it lies in $\mathsf{NP}$, and for every problem $\Pi \in \mathsf{NP}$ there is a deterministic polynomial-time computable map $g$ with $x \in \Pi \iff g(x) \in \text{3SAT}$.

2.3 Reductions and hardness

Definition (many-one reduction; hardness). A deterministic polynomial-time many-one reduction from problem $\Pi_1$ to problem $\Pi_2$ is a function $f$, computable in deterministic polynomial time, such that for every instance $x$: $$x \in \Pi_1 \;\Longrightarrow\; f(x) \in \Pi_2, \qquad x \notin \Pi_1 \;\Longrightarrow\; f(x) \notin \Pi_2.$$ A problem $\Pi_2$ is NP-hard (under such reductions) if every problem in $\mathsf{NP}$ reduces to it.

The force of the definition is contrapositive: a polynomial-time algorithm for $\Pi_2$ would yield one for $\Pi_1$ (compute $f$, then decide). Two standard remarks, both one-liners we will use silently: reductions compose (run one map after the other; a polynomial of a polynomial is a polynomial), and therefore, by Import 1, to prove a problem NP-hard it suffices to reduce 3SAT to it. That is exactly the shape of Theorem 1, and Fig. 7 draws it: satisfiable formulas must land on YES-instances, unsatisfiable ones on NO-instances, and the map itself must be cheap to compute.

For promise problems such as $\mathrm{GapCVP}$ the same definition applies verbatim, with “$f(x) \in \Pi_2$” and “$f(x) \notin \Pi_2$” replaced by “$f(x)$ is a YES-instance” and “$f(x)$ is a NO-instance”. The promise makes the target easier to solve and hence harder to prove hard: the reduction must never emit an instance in the excluded annulus, on pain of proving nothing.

2.4 Gap hardness is approximation hardness

Suppose an algorithm $\mathcal{A}$, given $(B,t)$, always outputs a number $\hat d$ with $\mathrm{dist}_2(t,L) \le \hat d \le \gamma(n)\cdot \mathrm{dist}_2(t,L)$ — a $\gamma$-approximation of the distance. (Overestimating by a bounded factor is the natural notion: computing $\hat d$ from any candidate lattice point gives an overestimate for free, and the guarantee bounds how far above truth it can sit.)

Proposition B (approximation solves the gap problem). If a $\gamma$-approximation algorithm $\mathcal{A}$ for CVP runs in polynomial time, then $\mathrm{GapCVP}^{(2)}_{\gamma} \in \mathsf{P}$: answer YES iff $\hat d \le \gamma(n)\, r$.
On a YES-instance, $\mathrm{dist}_2 \le r$, so $\hat d \le \gamma(n)\,\mathrm{dist}_2 \le \gamma(n) r$ and the rule answers YES. On a NO-instance, $\hat d \ge \mathrm{dist}_2 > \gamma(n) r$, and the rule answers NO. On instances violating the promise any answer is permitted, so the rule is correct wherever correctness is demanded (Fig. 8). ∎

Combining Proposition B with Theorem 1: a polynomial-time $n^{1/400}$-approximation of Euclidean CVP would put an NP-hard problem in $\mathsf{P}$ and hence prove $\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{NP}$. That is the operational meaning of “NP-hard to approximate”.

2.5 Why the theorem's shape is remarkable

Three contextual facts calibrate the achievement; the first two are proved elsewhere in the literature and quoted here only as context, not used in any proof.

  • A ceiling. Aharonov and Regev (2005) proved $\mathrm{GapCVP}^{(2)}_{C\sqrt n} \in \mathsf{NP}\cap\mathsf{coNP}$ for an absolute constant $C$: at factor $\sqrt n$, both YES and NO answers have efficiently checkable certificates. An NP-hardness proof at that factor would give $\mathsf{NP} = \mathsf{coNP}$, which is widely disbelieved. So fixed-polynomial hardness must live at exponents $c < 1/2$, and the open question after this chapter is the supremum of achievable $c \in [1/400, 1/2)$.
  • The prior record. The strongest unconditional factor before this work, $n^{a/\log\log n}$ (Dinur–Kindler–Raz–Safra 2003), grows faster than any $\mathrm{polylog}(n)$ yet slower than every fixed $n^c$ — its exponent decays to zero. Fixed-polynomial factors were known only conditionally, assuming the Projection Games Conjecture (Moshkovitz 2015; Mukhopadhyay 2022).
  • No PCP. Since Arora–Safra, hardness-of-approximation proofs standardly route through the PCP theorem — a deep transformation of proofs into locally checkable form. The present reduction never invokes it: the “gap amplification” is done by hand, with Reed–Solomon codes supplying redundancy and valuation theory supplying rigidity. For the reader this has a concrete payoff: the entire proof is contained in this document, modulo two precisely-stated imports.
Related problems, for orientation. The same machinery will prove hardness for two classical coding problems (defined and treated in §4 and §15): nearest codeword and syndrome decoding, the binary-Hamming analogues of CVP, become NP-hard to approximate within $n^{1/200}$. Their exact versions were proved intractable by Berlekamp–McEliece–van Tilborg (1978); constant-factor PCP-free hardness is recent (Bhattiprolu–Guruswami–Ren 2025); the fixed-power factor here is new. And for the shortest vector problem — CVP's homogeneous sibling — Bennett and Peikert (2023) combined Reed–Solomon codes with the same lattice lift in a randomized reduction; derandomizing that route is one motivation the paper cites.

3. Theorem 1 and the plan of the proof

We can now state the main theorem in its final form. (The proof is assembled in §14; everything in between builds its two halves.)

Theorem 1 (main theorem). There is a deterministic polynomial-time mapping that assigns to each 3SAT formula $\varphi$ a nonsingular matrix $B \in \mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}$, a target $t \in \mathbb{Z}^n$, and a radius $r \in \mathbb{Q}_{>0}$ such that $$\varphi \text{ satisfiable} \;\Longrightarrow\; \mathrm{dist}_2(t, L(B)) \le r, \qquad\qquad \varphi \text{ unsatisfiable} \;\Longrightarrow\; \mathrm{dist}_2(t, L(B)) > n^{1/400}\, r.$$ Consequently, $\mathrm{GapCVP}^{(2)}_{n^{1/400}}$ is NP-hard under deterministic polynomial-time many-one reductions.

The reduction factors through an intermediate combinatorial problem: $$\text{3SAT} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{binary nearest codeword} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{Euclidean CVP}.$$ The second arrow is a classical, dimension-preserving construction (the “parity-lift” lattice), which converts a Hamming-weight gap of factor $\Gamma$ into a Euclidean-distance gap of factor $\sqrt{\Gamma}$ — squaring appears because Euclidean distance squared counts $\pm 1$ coordinates. We prove it first, in §4, as Lemma 7. The first arrow is the paper's contribution: a deterministic encoding of 3SAT into a binary affine system $Hx = b$ over $\mathbb{F}_2$ (the field with two elements, defined properly in §4) whose minimum-weight solutions are rigid enough to encode satisfiability, with a weight gap of factor $4M^{1/200}$ in dimension $M$. Chaining the arrows: $\sqrt{4M^{1/200}}\ \ge M^{1/400} = n^{1/400}$, since the lattice rank is exactly $n = M$.

Where does the strange exponent come from? Not from one clever trick but from a cascade of budgets, each chosen to dominate the next with room to spare. Reading Fig. 9 top to bottom: the encoding needs a moment budget $T = N^{30}$ (enough power sums to run a valuation argument whose window reaches $\approx 5N^{21}$, §12); the soundness analysis caps fiber sizes at $K = N^4$ and needs $|P| \approx q$ to exceed error terms of size $\approx 2dK^2T \approx 2N^{39}$ (§9); and the Markov-style discard of oversized fibers needs the total solution weight $4M^{1/200}R$ divided by $K$ to be a tiny fraction of $q$ (§11). Setting $q \approx N^{200}$ satisfies everything simultaneously with slack; then $M \approx q^2$ forces the binary gap exponent $1/200$, and the square root of Lemma 7 halves it to $1/400$. The exponents $4, 30, 200, 400$ are engineering choices, not magic constants — and in §17 every one of the resulting inequalities is re-verified by exact integer arithmetic at $N = 100$.

The construction track (§4–§7) is finite linear algebra: codes, lattices, tables, constraints, and the easy completeness direction. The algebra track (§8–§10) is field theory: power sums, Hankel determinants, and valuations, developed with no reference to 3SAT at all. Soundness (§11–§13) is where the second track dismantles the first: it takes an arbitrary low-weight solution of the constraints and squeezes a satisfying assignment out of it. 4. The last mile first: binary codes and the parity-lift lattice (Lemmas 6–7)

We prove the pipeline's final arrow before building anything else. Once it is in hand, the rest of the chapter never has to mention Euclidean geometry again: everything reduces to weights of binary vectors.

4.1 Binary linear codes and two decoding problems

Write $\mathbb{F}_2 = \{0,1\}$ for the field with two elements, with arithmetic modulo $2$; note $-1 = 1$, so subtraction and addition coincide. The vector space $\mathbb{F}_2^M$ consists of length-$M$ binary words with coordinatewise XOR as addition. The Hamming weight $\mathrm{wt}(x)$ of a word is its number of nonzero coordinates, and the Hamming distance between two words is $\mathrm{wt}$ of their difference.

Definition (binary linear code; two problems). A binary linear code is a linear subspace $\mathcal{C} \le \mathbb{F}_2^M$; its elements are codewords. A generator matrix has rows spanning $\mathcal{C}$; a parity-check matrix is any $H \in \mathbb{F}_2^{r\times M}$ with $\mathcal{C} = \ker_{\mathbb{F}_2} H$ (rows need not be independent). Given a code and a received word $u \in \mathbb{F}_2^M$, the nearest-codeword problem asks for $$d_H(u, \mathcal{C}) = \min_{c \in \mathcal{C}} \mathrm{wt}(u - c).$$ Given a parity-check matrix $H$, a syndrome $b \in \mathbb{F}_2^{r}$, and the consistent affine system $Hx = b$, the syndrome-decoding (minimum-weight affine solution) problem asks for $$W(H, b) = \min\{\, \mathrm{wt}(x) : x \in \mathbb{F}_2^M,\ Hx = b \,\}.$$ Promise versions carry a radius $R > 0$ and factor $\gamma$: YES if the minimum is $\le R$, NO if it is $> \gamma R$.

The two problems are the same problem in different clothes. If $Hu = b$, then the solution set of $Hx = b$ is the affine coset $u + \mathcal{C}$, so $W(H,b) = \min_{c\in\mathcal{C}} \mathrm{wt}(u - c) = d_H(u,\mathcal{C})$ (using $-=+$ in characteristic two); conversely, given $(\mathcal{C}, u)$, set $b = Hu$. Gaussian elimination over $\mathbb{F}_2$ converts between generator and parity-check descriptions, tests whether $Hx=b$ is consistent (i.e. $\operatorname{rank} H = \operatorname{rank} [H\,|\,b]$), and computes a particular solution — all in polynomial time, by the row-reduction algorithm of ordinary linear algebra, which is valid verbatim over any field. Fig. 10 shows the smallest interesting example, which doubles as the paper's worked example.

4.2 The parity-lift lattice

Now lift the mod-2 picture to the integers. Let $$\rho : \mathbb{Z}^M \longrightarrow \mathbb{F}_2^M, \qquad \rho(z) = z \bmod 2$$ be coordinatewise reduction. Given a parity-check matrix $H$, define the parity-lift lattice $$\Lambda_H \;=\; \rho^{-1}(\mathcal{C}) \;=\; \{\, z \in \mathbb{Z}^M : H(z \bmod 2) = 0 \,\}, \qquad \mathcal{C} = \ker_{\mathbb{F}_2} H .$$ Because $\mathcal{C}$ is a subgroup of $\mathbb{F}_2^M$ and $\rho$ is a group homomorphism, $\Lambda_H$ is a subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}^M$; it contains $2\mathbb{Z}^M$ (which $\rho$ kills), so it spans $\mathbb{R}^M$ and is a full-rank lattice. Writing $e_c \in \{0,1\}^M$ for the obvious integer lift of a codeword $c$, the lattice decomposes into cosets of $2\mathbb{Z}^M$: $$\Lambda_H \;=\; \bigcup_{c \in \mathcal{C}} \big( e_c + 2\mathbb{Z}^M \big), \qquad 2\mathbb{Z}^M \subseteq \Lambda_H \subseteq \mathbb{Z}^M .$$ This inverse-image construction is classical (it appears in Leech and Sloane's 1971 sphere-packing constructions, and Bennett–Peikert's randomized SVP reduction combines the same lift with Reed–Solomon codes). Fig. 11 shows it in the plane.

A CVP instance needs a basis, not a membership test. One is produced by putting the code in systematic form: Gaussian elimination plus a permutation of the $M$ coordinates writes $$\mathcal{C} = \{\, (f,\ P_{\mathrm{sys}} f) : f \in \mathbb{F}_2^{k_0} \,\}, \qquad k_0 = \dim_{\mathbb{F}_2}\mathcal{C} = M - \operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb{F}_2} H,$$ where the first $k_0$ coordinates are free (“information”) and $P_{\mathrm{sys}} \in \mathbb{F}_2^{(M-k_0)\times k_0}$ determines the remaining (“parity”) coordinates. Write $\widetilde{P}_{\mathrm{sys}} \in \{0,1\}^{(M-k_0)\times k_0}$ for its entrywise integer lift.

Lemma 6 (explicit parity-lift lattice basis). In systematic coordinate order, $\Lambda_H$ has the integer basis $$B_H \;=\; \begin{pmatrix} I_{k_0} & 0 \\ \widetilde{P}_{\mathrm{sys}} & 2 I_{M-k_0} \end{pmatrix} , \tag{1}$$ i.e. $\Lambda_H = B_H \mathbb{Z}^M$, and $\lvert \det B_H \rvert = 2^{M-k_0} = 2^{\operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb{F}_2} H}$. The basis and the coordinate permutation are computable in polynomial time from $H$.
Work in systematic coordinates and split an integer vector as $z = (f, g)$ with $f \in \mathbb{Z}^{k_0}$, $g \in \mathbb{Z}^{M-k_0}$. Membership unwinds step by step: $$z \in \Lambda_H \iff (f, g) \bmod 2 \in \mathcal{C} \iff g \equiv \widetilde{P}_{\mathrm{sys}} f \pmod 2 \iff g = \widetilde{P}_{\mathrm{sys}} f + 2w \ \text{ for some } w \in \mathbb{Z}^{M-k_0} \iff z = B_H \begin{pmatrix} f \\ w \end{pmatrix}.$$ (The second equivalence is the systematic description of $\mathcal{C}$ applied to $\rho(z)$; the third rewrites a congruence as an equation.) Hence $\Lambda_H = B_H\mathbb{Z}^M$. The matrix is lower block-triangular, so its determinant is the product of the diagonal entries, $1^{k_0} \cdot 2^{M-k_0}$. Gaussian elimination computes $P_{\mathrm{sys}}$ and the permutation in polynomial time; applying the inverse permutation to the rows of $B_H$ restores the original coordinate order. ∎
Worked example. $H = [1\ 1\ 1]$, $b = 1$. Then $\mathcal{C} = \{000, 011, 101, 110\}$ and $\Lambda_H = \{z \in \mathbb{Z}^3 : z_1 + z_2 + z_3 \equiv 0 \bmod 2\}$. Here $k_0 = 2$, $P_{\mathrm{sys}} = [1\ 1]$ (the third coordinate is the sum of the first two), and $$B_H = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \lvert\det B_H\rvert = 2 .$$ With target $u = (1,0,0)$: the binary solutions of $x_1+x_2+x_3 = 1$ are the odd-weight words, of minimum weight $1$, and correspondingly $\mathrm{dist}_2(u, \Lambda_H) = 1$ (Fig. 13). This instance is verified by brute force in §17.

4.3 The distance identity

The point of the construction is an exact identity between lattice distances and binary weights — not an inequality with losses.

Lemma 7 (nearest codeword to CVP, exactly, in every norm). Let $H \in \mathbb{F}_2^{r\times M}$, $b \in \mathbb{F}_2^r$, and suppose $Hx = b$ is consistent. In deterministic polynomial time one can construct the nonsingular $B_H \in \mathbb{Z}^{M\times M}$ of Lemma 6 and a target $u \in \{0,1\}^M$ such that, for every real $p \ge 1$, $$\min_{\lambda \in L(B_H)} \lVert u - \lambda \rVert_p^p \;=\; W(H, b),$$ and in particular $\mathrm{dist}_2(u, L(B_H))^2 = W(H,b)$. The lattice rank is exactly $M$.
Compute (Gaussian elimination) a particular binary solution $u$ of $Hu = b$, the systematic form, and $B_H$; restore the original coordinate order. Then $L(B_H) = \Lambda_H$ by Lemma 6.

Lower bound. Let $\lambda \in \Lambda_H$ and set $x = (u - \lambda) \bmod 2$. Since $H(\lambda \bmod 2) = 0$, linearity gives $Hx = Hu = b$, so $x$ is a binary solution and $\mathrm{wt}(x) \ge W(H,b)$. Each nonzero coordinate of $x$ arises from an odd integer coordinate of $u - \lambda$, whose absolute value is therefore at least $1$; all coordinates contribute nonnegatively (Fig. 12). Hence $$\lVert u - \lambda \rVert_p^p = \sum_{j=1}^{M} \lvert u_j - \lambda_j \rvert^p \;\ge\; \#\{j : u_j - \lambda_j \text{ odd}\} = \mathrm{wt}(x) \;\ge\; W(H,b).$$ Upper bound. Let $x$ be any binary solution of $Hx = b$ and $e_x \in \{0,1\}^M$ its integer lift. Then $(u - e_x) \bmod 2$ is a codeword's residue: $H((u-e_x)\bmod 2) = b - b = 0$, so $\lambda = u - e_x \in \Lambda_H$, and $\lVert u - \lambda \rVert_p^p = \lVert e_x \rVert_p^p = \mathrm{wt}(x)$ because every entry of $e_x$ is $0$ or $1$. Minimizing over binary solutions $x$ makes the two bounds meet. Setting $p = 2$ gives the squared-distance identity, and $B_H$ is a nonsingular $M \times M$ matrix, so the rank is $M$. ∎

Consequently a binary weight gap converts into a distance gap with a square root: if either $W(H,b) \le R$ or $W(H,b) > \Gamma R$, then correspondingly either $$\min_{\lambda} \lVert u - \lambda \rVert_p \le R^{1/p} \qquad \text{or} \qquad \min_{\lambda} \lVert u - \lambda \rVert_p > \Gamma^{1/p} R^{1/p},$$ an $\ell_p$ gap of factor $\Gamma^{1/p}$ — for the Euclidean norm, $\sqrt{\Gamma}$. Two cautions the paper records, which will matter in §14: the thresholds $R^{1/p}$ need not be rational (the final instance uses the integer radius $\lceil \sqrt R\, \rceil$ and pays a factor $\le 4$), and the construction needs a particular solution $u$, hence a consistent system — the inconsistent case will be handled by a separate trivial branch.

5. Characteristic-two fields and Reed–Solomon codes

The encoding of §6 lives inside a large finite field of characteristic two. This section constructs those fields, records the two polynomial facts the whole chapter leans on (the root bound and Lagrange interpolation), and defines Reed–Solomon codes together with the parity checks that will later become rows of the binary matrix $H$.

5.1 Building $\mathbb{F}_{2^e}$

Let $\mathbb{F}_2[x]$ be the ring of polynomials with coefficients in $\mathbb{F}_2$. A polynomial $\pi$ of degree $e \ge 1$ is irreducible if it is not a product of two polynomials of smaller degree. Since $\mathbb{F}_2[x]$ has division with remainder, the ideal $(\pi)$ of multiples of an irreducible $\pi$ is maximal (any polynomial outside it has $\gcd 1$ with $\pi$, so Bézout gives an inverse mod $\pi$), and therefore the quotient $$K = \mathbb{F}_2[x]/(\pi)$$ is a field with exactly $2^e$ elements: the residues of the $2^e$ polynomials of degree $< e$. Addition is coefficientwise XOR; multiplication is polynomial multiplication followed by reduction mod $\pi$. Every such $K$ has characteristic two: $1 + 1 = 0$, so every element is its own negative — a fact we will exploit constantly ($-$ and $+$ agree, and squaring is additive: $(a+b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2 = a^2 + b^2$). Fig. 14 lays out the sixteen elements of $\mathbb{F}_{16} = \mathbb{F}_2[x]/(x^4 + x + 1)$, the toy field used in all worked examples and in the machine validation.

Does an irreducible $\pi$ of every degree $e$ exist — and can we find one deterministically? Yes on both counts.

Proposition C (existence and deterministic construction of $\mathbb{F}_{2^e}$). For every $e \ge 1$ there is an irreducible $\pi \in \mathbb{F}_2[x]$ of degree $e$; consequently a field with $2^e$ elements exists. Moreover, when $2^e$ is bounded by a polynomial in the relevant input parameter (here $q < 2N^{200}$), such a $\pi$ can be found by exhaustive search in deterministic polynomial time.
We use three steps, each standard graduate algebra, proved here for self-containment.

Step 1: some finite extension contains $2^e$ distinct roots of $f(x) = x^{2^e} - x$. Given any nonconstant polynomial over any field, the quotient construction above (by any irreducible factor) produces an extension field in which it acquires a root; splitting off that root and iterating produces a finite extension $E \supseteq \mathbb{F}_2$ in which $f$ factors into linear factors. The formal derivative of $f$ is $f' = 2^e x^{2^e - 1} - 1 = 1$ in characteristic two (the first term has even coefficient), so $\gcd(f, f') = 1$ and $f$ has no repeated roots: a repeated root $\alpha$ would make $(x-\alpha)^2 \mid f$, forcing $(x - \alpha) \mid f'$. Hence $f$ has exactly $2^e$ distinct roots in $E$.

Step 2: the roots form a field $F$ with $\lvert F \rvert = 2^e$. The map $\phi(a) = a^2$ satisfies $\phi(a+b) = \phi(a) + \phi(b)$ (char 2) and $\phi(ab) = \phi(a)\phi(b)$; so its $e$-fold iterate $a \mapsto a^{2^e}$ is a ring homomorphism, and the set of $a$ with $a^{2^e} = a$ is closed under addition, multiplication, and (for $a \ne 0$, multiplying the equation by $a^{-2^e}$) inversion. It contains $0$ and $1$. So the root set is a subfield of $E$ of size $2^e$.

Step 3: $F^\times$ is cyclic, and a generator's minimal polynomial has degree $e$. Let $F^\times$ be the multiplicative group, of order $s = 2^e - 1$, and let $x_0 \in F^\times$ have maximal multiplicative order $\mathrm{ord}(x_0) = h$. For any $y \in F^\times$, $\mathrm{ord}(y)$ divides $h$: otherwise some prime power $\pi^k \,\|\, \mathrm{ord}(y)$ exceeds the power of $\pi$ in $h$, and the element $x_0^{\pi^{a}} y^{\,\mathrm{ord}(y)/\pi^{k}}$ (with $\pi^a \,\|\, h$) has order $\mathrm{lcm} > h$ — using that for commuting elements of coprime orders, orders multiply. Hence every element of $F^\times$ satisfies $x^h = 1$; the polynomial $x^h - 1$ has at most $h$ roots in a field (Proposition D below), so $s \le h$, i.e. $x_0$ generates. Now let $\theta$ generate $F^\times$ and let $\pi_\theta$ be its minimal polynomial over $\mathbb{F}_2$ (the lowest-degree monic polynomial vanishing at $\theta$; it is irreducible, since a factorization would make a smaller-degree factor vanish at $\theta$). The subfield $\mathbb{F}_2(\theta) \subseteq F$ contains all powers of $\theta$ and $0$, hence equals $F$; and $\mathbb{F}_2(\theta) \cong \mathbb{F}_2[x]/(\pi_\theta)$ has $2^{\deg \pi_\theta}$ elements, forcing $\deg \pi_\theta = e$.

Deterministic search. Enumerate the $2^e$ monic binary polynomials of degree $e$; test each for irreducibility by trial division by all $\approx 2^{e/2+1}$ polynomials of degree $\le e/2$. Total cost $2^{O(e)} = N^{O(1)}$ bit operations when $2^e \le 2N^{200}$ — brute force is genuinely fast enough here, because $e = O(\log N)$. (Standard polynomial-time irreducibility tests do better; they are not needed.) ∎

5.2 The two polynomial workhorses

Proposition D (root bound). Over any field $K$, a nonzero polynomial $f \in K[X]$ of degree $\le D$ has at most $D$ roots in $K$. Consequently two polynomials of degree $\le D$ agreeing at $D+1$ points are equal.
Induction on $D$. If $f(\alpha) = 0$, divide with remainder: $f = (X-\alpha) g + r$ with $r$ constant; evaluating at $\alpha$ gives $r = 0$, so $f = (X - \alpha)g$ with $\deg g = \deg f - 1$ (the factor theorem). Any other root $\beta \neq \alpha$ satisfies $(\beta - \alpha) g(\beta) = 0$, and a field has no zero divisors, so $g(\beta) = 0$; by induction $g$ has at most $D - 1$ roots. The consequence follows by applying the bound to the difference. ∎
Proposition E (Lagrange interpolation). Let $a_1, \dots, a_m \in K$ be distinct and $\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_m \in K$. There is exactly one polynomial $Q \in K[X]$ with $\deg Q \le m-1$ and $Q(a_i) = \sigma_i$ for all $i$, namely $$Q(X) = \sum_{i=1}^m \sigma_i \prod_{\substack{1 \le h \le m \\ h \ne i}} \frac{X - a_h}{a_i - a_h}.$$
Each product is a polynomial of degree $m-1$ (denominators are nonzero scalars since the $a_i$ are distinct) that equals $1$ at $a_i$ and $0$ at every other $a_h$; the displayed sum therefore has degree $\le m-1$ and the required values. Uniqueness is Proposition D applied to the difference of two candidates, which vanishes at the $m > m-1$ points $a_i$. ∎

5.3 Reed–Solomon codes and their parity checks

Definition (Reed–Solomon evaluation code). Let $K = \mathbb{F}_q$, let $P \subseteq K$ be a set of evaluation points, and let $0 \le D < \lvert P \rvert$. The Reed–Solomon code of degree $D$ on $P$ is the set of all evaluation vectors of low-degree polynomials: $$\mathrm{RS}(P, D) \;=\; \Big\{\, \big(f(p)\big)_{p \in P} \;:\; f \in K[X],\ \deg f \le D \,\Big\} \;\le\; K^{P}.$$

By Proposition D, distinct polynomials of degree $\le D$ give distinct codewords (they cannot agree at $\lvert P\rvert > D$ points), so each codeword has a unique interpolating polynomial; the code is a $K$-linear subspace of dimension $D + 1$ (a basis of polynomials $1, X, \dots, X^D$ gives a basis of codewords). Membership is a linear condition: form the $(D{+}1) \times \lvert P \rvert$ evaluation matrix of the monomials, compute a basis of its null space by Gaussian elimination over $K$ — each null vector $(\lambda_p)_{p}$ gives a parity check $\sum_{p} \lambda_p\, y_p = 0$ satisfied exactly by... more precisely, a vector $y \in K^P$ is a codeword iff it is orthogonal to all $\lvert P \rvert - D - 1$ basis null vectors, because the code is precisely the row space of the evaluation matrix and orthogonality to a subspace's complement characterizes membership (rank–nullity over the field $K$). Fig. 15 shows what a Reed–Solomon codeword actually looks like — and what it does not look like.

One last translation step makes everything binary. Fix once and for all an $\mathbb{F}_2$-basis of $K$ (for the quotient construction, the residues $1, x, \dots, x^{e-1}$). Every element of $K$ is then a length-$e$ bit vector, and every $K$-linear equation $\sum_j \lambda_j y_j = \mu$ in unknowns $y_j$ becomes, upon expanding each product $\lambda_j y_j$ bitwise, a system of exactly $e$ binary-linear equations in the bits of the $y_j$. In our use case the unknowns will themselves be single bits $x_{\tau,p,w} \in \{0,1\}$ multiplied by known field constants, so each field-valued parity check contributes $e$ rows to the binary matrix $H$ — this is where the system (3) of §7 gets its rows.

6. Encoding an assignment: one polynomial, many tables

Now the construction proper. Fix a 3SAT formula $\varphi$ of encoding length $s$. First, harmless preprocessing: delete every tautological clause (one containing both $v_i$ and $\lnot v_i$ — satisfied by all assignments) and repeated literals within a clause. If an empty clause remains, $\varphi$ is trivially unsatisfiable; if no clauses remain, trivially satisfiable — both cases will be handled by fixed one-dimensional instances in §14. Otherwise delete variables that occur in no clause, and write $$v_1, \dots, v_m \quad\text{and}\quad C_1, \dots, C_\ell \qquad (m, \ell \ge 1)$$ for the surviving variables and clauses, $[m] = \{1, \dots, m\}$ for the variable indices.

6.1 Assignments as interpolation polynomials

Let $K = \mathbb{F}_q$ be a characteristic-two field (built as in §5) large enough to contain $m$ distinct elements $$a_1, \dots, a_m \in K,$$ called the anchors — one per Boolean variable. Identify the Boolean values $0, 1$ with the corresponding field elements. By Lagrange interpolation (Proposition E), each assignment $\sigma = (\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_m) \in \{0,1\}^m$ determines a unique assignment polynomial $$Q_\sigma \in K[X], \qquad \deg Q_\sigma \le m - 1, \qquad Q_\sigma(a_i) = \sigma_i \quad (1 \le i \le m),$$ and conversely any polynomial of degree $\le m-1$ taking $\{0,1\}$-values at all anchors reads out an assignment. The polynomial is the assignment, wearing algebraic clothes (Fig. 16). We write $d = m$ for a convenient degree bound.

Evaluate everywhere except the anchors: let $$P = K \setminus \{a_1, \dots, a_m\}, \qquad \lvert P \rvert = q - m.$$ The vector $(Q_\sigma(p))_{p\in P} \in \mathrm{RS}(P, m-1)$ is a Reed–Solomon encoding of the assignment. Excluding anchors guarantees $p - a_i \ne 0$ for all $p \in P$ and all $i$, so these differences may safely appear in denominators — the shifted moments of §7 depend on it.

The encoding must be binary, so we cannot store field elements directly. Instead, store indicator bits: for each evaluation point, one bit per candidate field value, exactly one of them set. This one-hot array is the global evaluation table: bits $x_{0,p,w}$ for $p \in P$, $w \in K$, with intended meaning $$x_{0, p, w} = 1 \iff w = Q_\sigma(p).$$

6.2 Clause tables

Satisfiability is a property of the assignment's restrictions to clauses, so the encoding carries clause-local copies. For a clause $C$ let $$I_C \subseteq [m]$$ be the indices of its variables ($\lvert I_C\rvert \le 3$), and let $$B_C = \{\, \beta \in \{0,1\}^{I_C} : \beta \text{ satisfies } C \,\}$$ be its satisfying local assignments — all ways to set just the clause's variables so the clause holds. A clause with three distinct literals excludes exactly one of the $8$ local patterns, so $\lvert B_C \rvert \le 8$ (our toy clauses have $\lvert B_C\rvert = 7$). If a global assignment $\sigma$ satisfies $C$, its restriction $\sigma|_{I_C}$ is one particular element of $B_C$.

Definition (table types). The type index set is $$\Theta = \{0\} \cup \{\, (C, \beta) : C \text{ a clause},\ \beta \in B_C \,\},$$ so $\lvert\Theta\rvert = 1 + \sum_C \lvert B_C\rvert \le 1 + 8\ell$. Type $0$ is the global table; a type $(C,\beta)$ is a clause subtype: a full evaluation table reserved for the possibility that the eventual assignment restricts to $\beta$ on clause $C$. For every $\tau \in \Theta$, $p \in P$, $w \in K$ there is one binary unknown $x_{\tau,p,w} \in \mathbb{F}_2$; for fixed $(\tau, p)$, the bit vector $(x_{\tau,p,w})_{w \in K}$ is the fiber at $p$, and the support of that fiber is $$S_\tau(p) = \{\, w \in K : x_{\tau,p,w} = 1 \,\}.$$

The intended assignment of all these bits, given a satisfying $\sigma$: declare type $0$ and, for each clause $C$, the single subtype $(C, \sigma|_{I_C})$ active; all other subtypes inactive. Each active table carries the same one-hot data, $$x_{\tau, p, w} = 1 \iff w = Q_\sigma(p) \qquad (\tau \text{ active}),$$ and inactive tables are identically zero. So for the intended solution, every fiber of an active table has support $\{Q_\sigma(p)\}$, a single dot tracing the graph of $Q_\sigma$, and inactive fibers are empty (Figs. 17, 18). A general binary vector $x$, of course, respects none of this — fibers may hold any number of dots — and the entire soundness argument (§11–§13) is about how the constraints of the next section tame arbitrary low-weight solutions anyway.

Why clause subtypes rather than one table per clause? Because the soundness argument will need each table to commit to a specific local assignment $\beta$: the shifted-moment constraints of §7 are built from the pair $(C,\beta)$, and in §12 a valuation argument will read the bit $\beta_i$ back off the algebraic roots associated with that table. The subtype structure is the reduction's way of making the eventual assignment legible, coordinate by coordinate, to algebra.

Toy bookkeeping. $q = 16$, $m = 3$, $\ell = 2$: anchors $\{1, g, g^2\}$, $\lvert P\rvert = 13$, $\lvert\Theta\rvert = 1 + 7 + 7 = 15$, so the number of binary unknowns is $M = \lvert\Theta\rvert\,\lvert P\rvert\, q = 15 \cdot 13 \cdot 16 = 3120$. All numbers verified in §17.

7. The moment constraints, completeness (Lemma 8), and the parameters

The tables of §6 are so far unconstrained bits. This section writes down the four families of binary-linear conditions that tie them to a single low-degree polynomial, proves the easy direction of the reduction, and fixes all numerical parameters.

7.1 Ordinary and shifted moments

Fix a moment budget $T \ge 0$ with $dT < \lvert P \rvert$ (recall $d = m$). For a table $\tau$, a point $p \in P$, and $0 \le j \le T$, define the ordinary moment $$\mu_{\tau, j}(p) \;=\; \sum_{w \in K} x_{\tau,p,w}\, w^j \;=\; \sum_{w \in S_\tau(p)} w^j ,$$ the $j$-th power sum of the fiber's support, with the convention $w^0 = 1$ for every $w$ including $w = 0$ (so $\mu_{\tau,0}(p)$ is the support's size reduced mod 2 — characteristic two sees only the parity). For a clause subtype $\tau = (C,\beta)$ and a variable index $i \in I_C$, define the shifted moment $$\eta_{(C,\beta), i, j}(p) \;=\; \sum_{w \in K} x_{(C,\beta),p,w} \left( \frac{w - \beta_i}{p - a_i} \right)^{\!j},$$ whose denominator is nonzero because $p \in P$ avoids the anchors.

Why these quantities? Check them against the intended solution. If the fiber support is exactly $\{Q_\sigma(p)\}$, then $\mu_{\tau,j}(p) = Q_\sigma(p)^j$: as $p$ varies, these are the evaluations of the polynomial $Q_\sigma(X)^j$ of degree $\le (m-1) j \le dj$ — a Reed–Solomon codeword of degree $dj$. For the shifted moments, suppose the table's committed bit matches the assignment: $\beta_i = \sigma_i$. Then $Q_\sigma(a_i) = \beta_i$, so the factor theorem (inside Proposition D's proof) makes the difference quotient a genuine polynomial: $$\frac{Q_\sigma(X) - \beta_i}{X - a_i} \in K[X], \qquad \text{degree} \le m - 2 \ \ (m \ge 2; \text{ zero if } m = 1),$$ and the active shifted moments are evaluations of its $j$-th power — a codeword of degree $\le (d-1)j$. The degree drops. If instead $\beta_i \ne \sigma_i$, the quotient is not a polynomial at all: it has a pole at the anchor. Fig. 20 shows the real-line analogy: shifted moments are secant slopes through the anchor point $(a_i, \beta_i)$, and the slope function is polynomial exactly when the curve really passes through that point. This dichotomy — degree drop versus hidden pole — is the seed of the entire soundness argument: §12 will detect the pole with valuations even after the fibers have been scrambled into unordered sets.

7.2 The four constraint families

Impose the following conditions on the bits $x = (x_{\tau,p,w})$; Fig. 19 locates each on the tables.

The constraint system.
  • (C1) For every $p \in P$: $\;\displaystyle\sum_{w\in K} x_{0,p,w} = 1$ in $\mathbb{F}_2$ — every global fiber has odd support size (in particular, nonempty).
  • (C2) For every clause $C$, $p \in P$, $w \in K$: $\;\displaystyle x_{0,p,w} = \sum_{\beta \in B_C} x_{(C,\beta),p,w}$ in $\mathbb{F}_2$ — fiber by fiber, the clause subtypes reproduce the global table mod 2.
  • (C3) For every $\tau \in \Theta$ and $0 \le j \le T$: $\;\big(\mu_{\tau,j}(p)\big)_{p\in P} \in \mathrm{RS}(P,\, dj)$.
  • (C4) For every $\tau = (C,\beta)$, $i \in I_C$, $0 \le j \le T$: $\;\big(\eta_{\tau,i,j}(p)\big)_{p\in P} \in \mathrm{RS}(P,\, (d-1)j)$.

Condition (C4), together with $(d-1)j \le dT < \lvert P\rvert$ and Reed–Solomon uniqueness (Proposition D), determines a unique interpolating polynomial for each shifted-moment vector: $$\eta_{\tau,i,j}(X) \in K[X], \qquad \deg \eta_{\tau,i,j} \le (d-1)j, \qquad \eta_{\tau,i,j}(p) = \sum_{w\in K} x_{\tau,p,w}\Big(\tfrac{w-\beta_i}{p-a_i}\Big)^{j} \quad (p \in P). \tag{2}$$ Likewise (C3) determines unique moment polynomials $\mu_{\tau,j}(X)$ of degree $\le dj$. We use the same symbols for the vectors and their interpolants; context disambiguates.

The crucial structural observation: all four families are linear over $\mathbb{F}_2$ in the unknowns. The moments look nonlinear — they contain powers $w^j$ — but $w$ is not an unknown: for each fixed triple $(\tau,p,w)$ the quantity $w^j$ (or $((w-\beta_i)/(p-a_i))^j$) is a known field constant multiplying the single unknown bit $x_{\tau,p,w}$. By §5, membership in a Reed–Solomon code is a set of $K$-linear parity checks, and each expands into $e$ binary equations in a fixed $\mathbb{F}_2$-basis of $K$. Conditions (C1) and (C2) are already binary. Together the four families form one explicit binary affine system $$H x = b \qquad \text{over } \mathbb{F}_2, \tag{3}$$ with $b$ collecting the constant sides (only (C1) contributes nonzero constants). This is the system whose minimum-weight solutions the rest of the chapter studies.

7.3 Completeness

Lemma 8 (satisfying assignments give low-weight solutions). If $\varphi$ is satisfiable, system (3) has a binary solution of Hamming weight exactly $$R = (\ell + 1)\,\lvert P \rvert .$$
Let $\sigma$ be a satisfying assignment with polynomial $Q_\sigma$. Populate the tables as intended: $x_{0,p,Q_\sigma(p)} = 1$ for every $p \in P$ (all other global bits zero); for each clause $C$, the restriction $\beta = \sigma|_{I_C}$ lies in $B_C$ because $\sigma$ satisfies $C$, and we set $x_{(C,\beta),p,Q_\sigma(p)} = 1$ for every $p$ (all other clause bits zero, including entire tables for the unselected subtypes).

Check the constraints. (C1): each global fiber has exactly one set bit — odd. (C2): for each clause, exactly one subtype's fiber reproduces the global fiber, and the others are zero, so the mod-2 sum matches bit for bit. (C3): an active table's $j$-th moments are $Q_\sigma(p)^j$, the evaluations of $Q_\sigma(X)^j$ with $\deg \le (m-1)j \le dj$; an inactive table's moments are identically zero, the evaluations of the zero polynomial. (C4): an active subtype has $\beta_i = \sigma_i$, so the shifted moments are evaluations of $\big((Q_\sigma(X) - \sigma_i)/(X - a_i)\big)^j$, of degree $\le (m-2)j \le (d-1)j$ (and at $j = 0$ the constant $1$, degree $0$); inactive subtypes again contribute zero.

Finally the weight: at each of the $\lvert P\rvert$ evaluation points, exactly $\ell + 1$ bits are set — one global, one per clause — giving $\mathrm{wt}(x) = (\ell+1)\lvert P\rvert = R$ (Fig. 21). ∎

7.4 Parameter choices and encoding size

Now fix every dial. With $s$ the encoding length of $\varphi$, set $$N = 100 + s + m + \ell, \qquad d = m, \qquad K = N^4, \qquad T = N^{30}, \qquad c = \tfrac{1}{400},$$ and let $e$ be the least integer with $2^e \ge N^{200}$, so that $$q = 2^e, \qquad N^{200} \le q < 2N^{200}, \qquad K_{\text{field}} = \mathbb{F}_q$$ (built deterministically by Proposition C; at $N = 100$, $e = 1329$). Here $K = N^4$ is the fiber-size cap used from §9 onward — an unfortunate but faithful collision with the field's name, which we disambiguate by context exactly as the paper does. Since $q \ge N^{200} > m$, distinct anchors exist, and $$\lvert P \rvert = q - m, \qquad dT \le N \cdot N^{30} = N^{31} < q - m = \lvert P\rvert,$$ so every Reed–Solomon degree bound above is strictly below the number of evaluation points, as required. The dimension of the system is $$M = \lvert\Theta\rvert\, \lvert P\rvert\, q \le (1 + 8\ell)\, q^2 \le 40 N^{401}, \tag{4}$$ and counting equations — $\lvert P\rvert$ from (C1), $\ell \lvert P\rvert q$ from (C2), and at most $4\lvert\Theta\rvert (T{+}1) \lvert P\rvert e$ binary rows from all Reed–Solomon memberships in (C3)–(C4), using $\sum_C \lvert B_C\rvert\, \lvert I_C\rvert \le 3(\lvert\Theta\rvert - 1)$ — gives $$\#\text{rows} \;=\; O(N^{401}). \tag{5}$$ So the dense binary description of $(H, b)$ occupies $O(N^{802})$ bits: enormous, but a fixed polynomial in the input size, and every entry is computable by field arithmetic and Gaussian elimination in deterministic polynomial time. Fig. 22 collects the dials and what each inequality buys; each is re-verified with exact integers in §17.

8. Interlude: reconstructing unordered data from power sums

We now leave 3SAT completely. This section and the next develop, as a free-standing piece of algebra, the paper's central tool: an inverse problem about unordered sets whose power sums vary polynomially. Nothing here mentions clauses, tables, or binary systems — which is exactly what makes the tool trustworthy when soundness later feeds it hostile data.

8.1 Bounded polynomial-moment families

Let $K$ be a finite field and $P \subseteq K$ a set of evaluation points. Suppose that at each $p \in P$ we are handed an unordered set $S(p) \subseteq K$ — the fiber at $p$ — forming a family $\mathcal{S} = (S(p))_{p\in P}$. No correspondence between the elements at different points is given, and sizes may vary. Write $$m_j(p) = \sum_{w \in S(p)} w^j$$ for the $j$-th moment (power sum) of the fiber, with the convention $w^0 = 1$ even for $w = 0$; thus $m_0(p)$ is $\lvert S(p)\rvert$ as an element of $K$ — in characteristic two, the parity of the size, not the size.

Definition 9 (bounded polynomial-moment family). Let $d, K_{\mathrm{cap}} \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}$ and $T \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}$ with $dT < \lvert P\rvert$. The family $\mathcal{S}$ is a $(d, T, K_{\mathrm{cap}})$-bounded polynomial-moment family if:
  • (M1) every fiber is small: $\lvert S(p)\rvert \le K_{\mathrm{cap}}$ for all $p \in P$ (empty fibers allowed);
  • (M2) the moments vary polynomially: for every $0 \le j \le T$ there is $\mu_j(X) \in K[X]$ with $\deg \mu_j \le dj$ and $\mu_j(p) = m_j(p)$ for all $p \in P$.
Since $dj \le dT < \lvert P\rvert$, each moment polynomial $\mu_j$ is unique (Proposition D). In the paper the cap is written $K$; we write $K_{\mathrm{cap}}$ when the field is nearby.

The condition is subtle precisely because the fibers are unordered: each individual element may jump around wildly with $p$, while the symmetric quantities $m_j$ behave like evaluations of low-degree polynomials. What global structure does that force?

To calibrate, first look at the structured example where the answer is obvious. Take $h$ polynomials $q_1, \dots, q_h \in K[X]$ of degree $\le d$ whose values are pairwise distinct at every $p$, and let $S(p) = \{q_1(p), \dots, q_h(p)\}$. Then $$\mu_j(X) = \sum_{t=1}^{h} q_t(X)^j, \qquad \deg \mu_j \le dj,$$ so the family is bounded polynomial-moment, and everything about it is explained by the single polynomial $$G(Y) = \prod_{t=1}^{h} \big( Y - q_t(X) \big) \in K[X][Y]:$$ its roots are the “label curves” $q_t$, and specializing its coefficients at $p$ gives the monic polynomial with root set exactly $S(p)$. Fig. 23 contrasts this labeled picture with the unlabeled data we actually have.

The goal, stated precisely: writing $F = K(X)$ for the field of rational functions $g/h$ ($g, h \in K[X]$, $h \neq 0$) and $h = \max_p \lvert S(p)\rvert$, produce a monic separable polynomial $G(Y) \in F[Y]$ of degree $h$ whose distinct roots $R = \{\alpha : G(\alpha) = 0\}$, living in a finite separable extension $E/F$, satisfy $$\sum_{\alpha \in R} \alpha^j = \mu_j(X) \quad (0 \le j \le T),$$ and whose coefficients specialize, at every maximum-size fiber, to the monic polynomial vanishing exactly on $S(p)$. (The terms separable and splitting field are defined in §8.4 below.) The roots play the role of the $q_t$ — but they may genuinely fail to be rational functions, as the example in §8.5 shows.

8.2 Encoding a set without labeling it: the Hankel system

The natural label-free encoding of a single finite set $S(p)$ is the monic polynomial that vanishes on it, $$\prod_{w \in S(p)} (Y - w) \in K[Y],$$ whose coefficients are (up to sign, invisible in characteristic two) the elementary symmetric functions of the elements — unchanged under reordering, no labels required; the empty product is $1$. The classical fact (behind Massey's 1969 shift-register decoding of BCH codes) is that the moments determine this polynomial linearly. Suppose $\lvert S(p)\rvert = h > 0$ and write $$\prod_{w\in S(p)}(Y - w) = Y^h + \sum_{l=0}^{h-1} g_{p,l}\, Y^l .$$ Multiply by $w^i$ and sum over $w \in S(p)$: since the product vanishes at each such $w$, $$0 \;=\; \sum_{w \in S(p)} w^i \Big( w^h + \sum_{l=0}^{h-1} g_{p,l} w^l \Big) \;=\; m_{i+h}(p) + \sum_{l=0}^{h-1} g_{p,l}\, m_{i+l}(p) \qquad (0 \le i < h).$$ So the unknown coefficients $g_{p,l}$ satisfy an $h \times h$ linear system whose matrix $\big(m_{i+l}(p)\big)_{0\le i,l<h}$ is a Hankel matrix: entries depend only on $i + l$ (constant anti-diagonals). And this matrix is invertible, by a factorization worth internalizing (Fig. 24): listing $S(p) = \{w_1,\dots,w_h\}$ and forming the Vandermonde matrix $V_p = (w_s^i)_{0\le i<h,\ 1\le s\le h}$, $$\big(m_{i+l}(p)\big)_{i,l} = V_p V_p^{\mathsf T}, \qquad \det V_p = \prod_{1 \le s < t \le h} (w_t - w_s) \ne 0$$ (the Vandermonde determinant formula, proved below as Proposition G), so $\det = (\det V_p)^2 \neq 0$. The moments pin down the whole polynomial without ever identifying an individual element.

Proposition G (Vandermonde determinant). For elements $w_1, \dots, w_h$ of any commutative ring, $\det\big( w_s^{\,i} \big)_{0 \le i < h,\ 1 \le s \le h} = \prod_{1\le s < t \le h} (w_t - w_s)$. In particular over a field it is nonzero iff the $w_s$ are distinct.
Both sides are polynomial identities in the $w_s$, so it suffices to prove them over $\mathbb{Z}[w_1,\dots,w_h]$, i.e. as an identity of integer polynomials, which we do by induction on $h$; the case $h=1$ reads $1 = 1$ (empty product). View the determinant $D(w_h)$ as a polynomial in $w_h$ of degree $\le h-1$ (expand along the last column). It vanishes at $w_h = w_s$ for each $s < h$ (two equal columns), so $D = c \prod_{s<h}(w_h - w_s)$ with $c$ the coefficient of $w_h^{h-1}$, which by cofactor expansion is the $(h-1)$-point Vandermonde determinant of $w_1,\dots,w_{h-1}$; apply the inductive formula to $c$. ∎

8.3 One global polynomial

The pointwise Hankel systems at different $p$ are not independent puzzles: every entry $m_{i+l}(p)$ is the evaluation of the same polynomial $\mu_{i+l}(X)$. So all of them are specializations of a single matrix of polynomials, $\big(\mu_{i+l}(X)\big)_{0\le i,l < h}$, and we can solve one linear system with $X$ left symbolic: $$\sum_{l=0}^{h-1} \mu_{i+l}(X)\, c_l(X) \;=\; -\,\mu_{i+h}(X) \qquad (0 \le i < h). \tag{7}$$ At any point with $\lvert S(p)\rvert = h$ the coefficient matrix specializes to an invertible one, so its determinant $\Delta_h(X)$ is not the zero polynomial, and (7) has a unique solution over the rational function field $F = K(X)$ — by Cramer's rule, $c_l = n_l/\Delta_h$ with polynomial numerators. Division by $\Delta_h$ is exactly why the coefficients live in $F$ rather than $K[X]$. They define one monic polynomial $$G(Y) \;=\; Y^h + \sum_{l=0}^{h-1} c_l(X)\, Y^l \;\in\; F[Y],$$ and at every full-size fiber the coefficients have no pole (the common denominator $\Delta_h(p) \neq 0$), specialize to the pointwise solution, and hence recover $\prod_{w\in S(p)}(Y-w)$. All of this is made precise and quantitative in Lemma 10.

8.4 Separability and splitting fields, in the amount needed

Two definitions before the lemma can even be stated. A splitting field of a nonzero $G \in F[Y]$ is an extension field $E \supseteq F$ in which $G$ factors into linear factors and which is generated over $F$ by the roots; one always exists by the iterated-quotient construction used in Proposition C (adjoin a root of an irreducible factor, repeat), and it is a finite extension. $G$ is separable if its roots in a splitting field are distinct. The workable test:

Proposition F (derivative criterion). A nonzero $G \in F[Y]$ is separable if and only if $\gcd(G, \frac{dG}{dY}) = 1$, where the formal derivative and the gcd are computed in $F[Y]$.
The Euclidean algorithm computes the same monic gcd over $F$ as over any extension $E \supseteq F$ (the algorithm's steps never leave the smaller field), so we may test the gcd in a splitting field $E$. If $\alpha$ is a repeated root, $G = (Y-\alpha)^2 H$, and the product rule gives $G' = 2(Y-\alpha)H + (Y-\alpha)^2 H'$, divisible by $(Y-\alpha)$: gcd $\neq 1$. Conversely if $\gcd \ne 1$ it has a root $\alpha$ in $E$ shared by $G$ and $G'$; writing $G = (Y-\alpha)G_1$ we get $G' = G_1 + (Y-\alpha)G_1'$, so $0 = G'(\alpha) = G_1(\alpha)$, making $\alpha$ a repeated root of $G$. ∎

The criterion has teeth in characteristic two: $\frac{d}{dY}(Y^2 - c) = 2Y = 0$, so $Y^2 - c$ is never separable — it is the square of $Y - \sqrt c$ in a splitting field. Separability is a genuine hypothesis, not a formality, and Lemma 10 will have to earn it for its $G$ (it will fall out of the Hankel determinant). We will also need to split many separable polynomials in one place:

Lemma 3 (common separable splitting field). Let $G_1, \dots, G_r \in F[Y]$ be separable. There is a single finite extension $E/F$, separable over $F$, in which every $G_i$ splits into linear factors.
Take a splitting field $E$ of the product $G_1 \cdots G_r$; it is finite over $F$, and it is generated by the roots of the $G_i$, each of which is a root of a separable polynomial, hence separable over $F$ (its minimal polynomial divides $G_i$; a divisor of a separable polynomial is separable, since its roots are a subset). A finite extension generated by separable elements is separable — this is part of the imported field-theory package, Import 2(a) in §10. Note the product itself need not be separable (two $G_i$ may share a root); only each factor matters. ∎

8.5 A worked example where the roots are genuinely algebraic

Toy (Artin–Schreier fibers in $\mathbb{F}_{16}$, machine-verified). In $K = \mathbb{F}_{16}$, let $P'$ be the set of $p$ with $\operatorname{Tr}(p) = 0$, where $\operatorname{Tr}(a) = a + a^2 + a^4 + a^8$ — there are exactly $8$ such $p$ — and define fibers $$S(p) = \{\, w \in K : w^2 + w = p \,\}.$$ Each such fiber has exactly two elements (if $w$ is a solution so is $w + 1$, and a quadratic has at most 2 roots; solvability for trace-zero $p$ is verified by machine). The computed moment polynomials are $$\mu_0 = 0 \ (\text{parity of } 2), \qquad \mu_1 = 1, \qquad \mu_2 = 1, \qquad \mu_3 = 1 + X, \quad \dots$$ all of degree $\le \lceil j/2\rceil$, comfortably a bounded polynomial-moment family. Solving the $2\times 2$ Hankel system (7) symbolically gives $$c_1 = 1, \qquad c_0 = X, \qquad \text{i.e.}\quad G(Y) = Y^2 + Y + X, \qquad \Delta_2 = 1.$$ This $G$ is separable ($G' = 1$; Proposition F) but has no root in $K(X)$, indeed none in $K[X]$: a polynomial root $y$ would force $\deg(y^2) = 2\deg y = 1$ (machine check: none of the $698{,}896$ candidates of degree $\le 2$ works, and the degree argument rules out the rest, including rational roots after clearing denominators). Its two roots live in a genuine quadratic extension of $K(X)$, differ by $1$, and satisfy $\Delta_2 = (\alpha_2 - \alpha_1)^2 = 1$ — matching equation (8) of Lemma 10. The Newton recurrence $s_j = s_{j-1} + X s_{j-2}$ derived from $G$ reproduces every computed moment polynomial. Fig. 25 shows the real-line shadow of this two-sheeted object and the actual $\mathbb{F}_{16}$ fiber table.

This example is the mental model to carry into the proof: fibers that no polynomial labeling explains, yet one global algebraic equation governs them all. The reconstruction lemma says this is the general situation, and quantifies every degree and every exceptional point.

9. The algebraic reconstruction lemma (Lemma 10), proved in full

First the one classical identity the proof consumes: Newton's identities in the direction that needs no division — from coefficients to power sums. (The familiar direction, recovering coefficients from power sums, divides by factorials and dies in characteristic two; this direction survives everywhere.)

Proposition H (power sums from coefficients, division-free). Let $G(Y) = Y^h + \sum_{l=0}^{h-1} c_l Y^l$ be monic over a commutative ring, with roots $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_h$ (listed with multiplicity, in some extension where $G$ splits), and let $s_j = \sum_{t=1}^h \alpha_t^j$ (so $s_0 = h \cdot 1$). Then for every $j \ge 1$, $$s_j = P_j(c_0, \dots, c_{h-1}),$$ where $P_j$ is a polynomial with integer coefficients, of total degree at most $j$, independent of the ring. Explicitly, writing $g_k$ for the coefficient of $Y^k$ in $G$ (so $g_h = 1$, $g_l = c_l$): $$s_j = -\,j\, g_{h-j} \;-\; \sum_{i=1}^{j-1} g_{h-j+i}\, s_i \quad (1 \le j \le h), \qquad\qquad s_j = -\sum_{l=0}^{h-1} c_l\, s_{j-h+l} \quad (j > h),$$ with the convention $g_k = 0$ for $k < 0$.
For $j > h$: each root satisfies $\alpha_t^{\,j} = \alpha_t^{\,j-h}\,\alpha_t^{\,h} = -\sum_l c_l\, \alpha_t^{\,j-h+l}$ (multiply $G(\alpha_t) = 0$ by $\alpha_t^{\,j-h}$); sum over $t$.

For $1 \le j \le h$ we derive the classical identity from a division-free computation of $G'$. For any $\alpha$, the telescoping identity $Y^n - \alpha^n = (Y - \alpha)(Y^{n-1} + Y^{n-2}\alpha + \dots + \alpha^{n-1})$, summed against the coefficients $g_n$, gives $$G(Y) - G(\alpha) = (Y - \alpha)\, Q_\alpha(Y), \qquad Q_\alpha(Y) = \sum_{k=0}^{h-1} \Big( \sum_{i=0}^{h-1-k} g_{k+1+i}\, \alpha^i \Big) Y^k .$$ When $\alpha = \alpha_t$ is a root, $G(Y) = (Y - \alpha_t) Q_{\alpha_t}(Y)$, so $\prod_{u \ne t} (Y - \alpha_u) = Q_{\alpha_t}(Y)$. Now differentiate the product $G(Y) = \prod_t (Y - \alpha_t)$ by the product rule: $$G'(Y) = \sum_{t=1}^h \prod_{u \ne t}(Y - \alpha_u) = \sum_{t=1}^h Q_{\alpha_t}(Y).$$ Compare the coefficient of $Y^k$ on both sides for $0 \le k \le h-1$: the left side gives $(k+1)\, g_{k+1}$, the right side $\sum_{i=0}^{h-1-k} g_{k+1+i}\, s_i$. Setting $k = h - j$ and isolating the $i = j$ term (whose coefficient is $g_h = 1$): $$(h - j + 1)\,g_{h-j+1}\Big|_{k=h-j}: \qquad (h-j)\,g_{h-j} \;=\; g_{h-j}\, s_0 + \sum_{i=1}^{j-1} g_{h-j+i}\, s_i + s_j \quad\text{at } k = h-j,$$ i.e., using $s_0 = h\cdot 1$: $\; s_j = (h-j)g_{h-j} - h\,g_{h-j} - \sum_{i=1}^{j-1} g_{h-j+i} s_i = -j\,g_{h-j} - \sum_{i=1}^{j-1} g_{h-j+i}\, s_i$. Every step used only polynomial identities with integer coefficients — no division. The degree bound follows by induction: $\deg s_i \le i$ makes each summand of either recurrence have total degree $\le 1 + (j-1) = j$. ∎

Now the main lemma. Recall the standing convention: $K$ is a finite field, $P \subseteq K$, $F = K(X)$, and $K_{\mathrm{cap}}$ is the fiber-size cap (the paper's $K$).

Lemma 10 (algebraic reconstruction of bounded polynomial moments). Let $\mathcal{S} = (S(p))_{p\in P}$ be a $(d, T, K_{\mathrm{cap}})$-bounded polynomial-moment family with moment polynomials $\mu_0, \dots, \mu_T$, and suppose $$T \ge 2K_{\mathrm{cap}} - 1, \qquad \lvert P \rvert - d K_{\mathrm{cap}}(K_{\mathrm{cap}} - 1) > 2 d K_{\mathrm{cap}}^2 T .$$ Define $h = \max_{p\in P} \lvert S(p)\rvert \le K_{\mathrm{cap}}$ and the Hankel determinant $$\Delta_h(X) = \begin{cases} 1, & h = 0,\\[2pt] \det\big( \mu_{i+j}(X) \big)_{0 \le i, j < h}, & h > 0. \end{cases}$$ Then $\Delta_h \neq 0$, and there exist a monic separable polynomial $G(Y) = Y^h + \sum_{l<h} c_l Y^l \in F[Y]$ and a finite separable splitting field $E/F$ of $G$, with root set $R = \{\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_h\} = \{\alpha \in E : G(\alpha) = 0\}$ of exactly $h$ distinct elements, such that:
  • (i) Moment identities: for $0 \le j \le T$, $\;\displaystyle \mu_j(X) = \sum_{\alpha\in R} \alpha^j$. $\tag{6}$
  • (ii) Maximum-size fibers: for every $p \in P$, $\;\Delta_h(p) \ne 0 \iff \lvert S(p)\rvert = h$; hence $\#\{p : \lvert S(p)\rvert = h\} \ge \lvert P\rvert - dh(h-1)$.
  • (iii) Coefficients and discriminant: $\deg \Delta_h \le dh(h-1)$; for $h > 0$ the $c_l$ are the unique solution of the Hankel system $$\sum_{l=0}^{h-1} \mu_{i+l}(X)\, c_l = -\,\mu_{i+h}(X) \qquad (0 \le i < h), \tag{7}$$ each of the form $c_l = n_l / \Delta_h$ with $n_l \in K[X]$, $\deg n_l \le d(h^2 - l) \le dh^2$; and $$\Delta_h = \prod_{1 \le r < s \le h} (\alpha_s - \alpha_r)^2 . \tag{8}$$
  • (iv) Specialization: for every $p$ with $\lvert S(p)\rvert = h$, all $c_l$ are pole-free at $p$ and $$\mathrm{ev}_p(G)(Y) := Y^h + \sum_{l<h} c_l(p)\, Y^l = \prod_{w \in S(p)} (Y - w). \tag{9}$$
When $h = 0$: $G = 1$, $R = \varnothing$, $\Delta_0 = 1$, and $\mu_j = 0$ for all $j$. All conclusions hold in every characteristic, including two.

Case $h = 0$. Every fiber is empty, so each $m_j(p) = 0$: the polynomial $\mu_j$, of degree $\le dT < \lvert P\rvert$, vanishes at all $\lvert P\rvert$ points, hence $\mu_j = 0$ by the root bound (Proposition D). Take $G = 1$, $E = F$, $R = \varnothing$; every conclusion holds by the empty-sum/product conventions.

Setup for $h > 0$. All entries of $\Delta_h$ exist: the largest index used is $2h - 2 \le 2K_{\mathrm{cap}} - 2 \le T - 1 < T$. Expanding the determinant as a signed sum over permutations and using $\deg \mu_{i+j} \le d(i+j)$, every term has degree $\le d \sum_i (i + \pi(i)) = d h(h-1)$, so $$\deg \Delta_h \le d h (h - 1). \tag{10}$$

Step 1: $\Delta_h(p) \neq 0$ exactly at full fibers. At a point with $S(p) = \{w_1, \dots, w_h\}$ (all distinct, being a set), the moment identities give the factorization $$\big( \mu_{i+j}(p) \big)_{0\le i,j<h} = V_p V_p^{\mathsf T}, \qquad (V_p)_{i,s} = w_s^{\,i},$$ since the $(i,j)$ entry of $V_pV_p^{\mathsf T}$ is $\sum_s w_s^i w_s^j = m_{i+j}(p) = \mu_{i+j}(p)$. By Proposition G, $\det V_p = \prod_{s<t}(w_t - w_s) \ne 0$, so $$\Delta_h(p) = \det(V_p)^2 = \prod_{1\le s<t\le h} (w_t - w_s)^2 \neq 0. \tag{11}$$ At a point with $\lvert S(p)\rvert < h$, the same computation runs with the rectangular matrix $W_p = (w^i)_{0\le i < h,\ w \in S(p)}$, which has fewer than $h$ columns: the moment matrix equals $W_p W_p^{\mathsf T}$, of rank $< h$, so $\Delta_h(p) = 0$. This proves the equivalence in (ii). Since $h$ is the maximum fiber size, at least one full-fiber point exists, so $\Delta_h$ is a nonzero polynomial.

Step 2: almost all fibers are full. The nonzero polynomial $\Delta_h$ has at most $dh(h-1)$ roots by (10), and non-full fibers happen only at its roots, so (Fig. 27) $$\#\{p \in P : \lvert S(p)\rvert = h\} \;\ge\; \lvert P\rvert - dh(h-1) \;\ge\; \lvert P\rvert - dK_{\mathrm{cap}}(K_{\mathrm{cap}}-1) \;>\; 2dK_{\mathrm{cap}}^2 T. \tag{12}$$

Step 3: solve the Hankel system symbolically. Since $\Delta_h \ne 0$, the system (7) has a unique solution $(c_l)$ over $F$. Cramer's rule (valid over any field: multiply the system by the adjugate matrix) expresses $c_l$ as the determinant of the coefficient matrix with column $l$ replaced by $(-\mu_{i+h})_i$, divided by $\Delta_h$. The numerator determinant expands with degrees $\le d(i + l')$ per entry except in the replaced column, where the entry has degree $\le d(i + h)$; summing along any permutation gives degree at most $d\big( h(h-1) + (h - l) \big) = d(h^2 - l) \le dh^2$. So $c_l = n_l/\Delta_h$ with the stated degree bound, and $G(Y) = Y^h + \sum_l c_l Y^l$ is defined.

Step 4: specialization at full fibers. Fix $p$ with $S(p) = \{w_1,\dots,w_h\}$. By (11), $\Delta_h(p) \ne 0$, so each $c_l = n_l/\Delta_h$ is pole-free at $p$ and the specialization $\mathrm{ev}_p(G)$ makes sense. Specializing (7) at $X = p$ (legitimate: both sides are rational functions with nonvanishing denominators at $p$, agreeing as elements of $F$) gives, for $0 \le i < h$: $$0 = \mu_{i+h}(p) + \sum_{l<h} \mu_{i+l}(p)\, c_l(p) = \sum_{s=1}^{h} w_s^{\,i} \Big( w_s^{\,h} + \sum_{l<h} c_l(p) w_s^{\,l} \Big) = \sum_{s=1}^{h} w_s^{\,i}\; \mathrm{ev}_p(G)(w_s),$$ where the middle equality regroups the moment sums fiber-element by fiber-element. In matrix form, $V_p \cdot \big(\mathrm{ev}_p(G)(w_s)\big)_s = 0$ with $V_p$ invertible, so $\mathrm{ev}_p(G)$ vanishes at every $w_s$. A monic degree-$h$ polynomial with $h$ distinct prescribed roots equals $\prod_s (Y - w_s)$; this is (9).

Step 5: the moment identities, by zero counting. Let $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_h$ be the roots of $G$ in a splitting field, listed with multiplicity for now. By Proposition H, their power sums are $s_j = P_j(c_0,\dots,c_{h-1})$ with $P_j$ of integer coefficients and total degree $\le j$. Substitute $c_l = n_l/\Delta_h$ and clear denominators: for $1 \le j \le T$, $$D_j(X) := \Delta_h^{\,j}\,\big( \mu_j - P_j(c_0, \dots, c_{h-1}) \big)$$ is a polynomial (each monomial $\prod_l c_l^{m_l}$ with $\sum m_l \le j$ becomes $\prod_l n_l^{m_l} \Delta_h^{\,j - \sum m_l}$), of degree at most $$\max\big( dj + j\,dh(h-1),\ \ j\, dh^2 \big) \;\le\; 2 d h^2 j \;\le\; 2 d K_{\mathrm{cap}}^2 T .$$ Now evaluate at any full-fiber point $p$: by (9), the roots of $\mathrm{ev}_p(G)$ are exactly the elements of $S(p)$, and $P_j$ commutes with specialization (it is a polynomial identity in the coefficients), so $P_j(c(p)) = \sum_{w\in S(p)} w^j = m_j(p) = \mu_j(p)$, giving $D_j(p) = 0$. By (12) there are strictly more than $2dK_{\mathrm{cap}}^2 T$ such points — more zeros than the degree allows — so $D_j \equiv 0$, i.e. $$\mu_j(X) = \sum_{s=1}^{h} \alpha_s^{\,j} \qquad (1 \le j \le T).$$ For $j = 0$: $\mu_0$ has degree $\le 0$, i.e. is a constant; at a full-fiber point it equals $m_0(p) = h \cdot 1_K = \sum_s \alpha_s^0$, proving the $j=0$ identity as well (note this is an identity of field elements — parity, in characteristic two — not of integer cardinalities).

Step 6: separability and the discriminant. Since $2h - 2 \le T$, the identities of Step 5 cover all indices appearing in the Hankel matrix, so that matrix also factors through the roots: $\big(\mu_{i+j}\big)_{i,j} = V_\alpha V_\alpha^{\mathsf T}$ with $(V_\alpha)_{i,s} = \alpha_s^{\,i}$, whence by Proposition G $$\Delta_h = \det(V_\alpha)^2 = \prod_{1 \le r < s \le h} (\alpha_s - \alpha_r)^2,$$ which is (8). Since $\Delta_h \neq 0$ in $F \subseteq E$, all the differences are nonzero: the roots are distinct, $G$ is separable (definition), $\lvert R \rvert = h$, and the power sums over the multiset coincide with sums over the set $R$, completing (6). Finally, Lemma 3's construction (splitting field of a separable polynomial) provides $E/F$ finite and separable. ∎

Relation to Reed–Solomon list recovery (context, not used later). List recovery starts from the same data — small sets $S(p)$ — and asks for all low-degree $f \in K[X]$ with $f(p) \in S(p)$ at many points (Sudan 1997, Guruswami–Sudan 1999, and successors). Lemma 10's output subsumes such lists: the bivariate polynomial $Q(X,Y) = \Delta_h(X) G(Y) = \Delta_h Y^h + \sum_l n_l Y^l \in K[X,Y]$ vanishes on every pair $(p, w)$ with $w \in S(p)$ (at full fibers by specialization; at deficient fibers every $n_l(p)$ and $\Delta_h(p)$ vanish, by the rectangular-factorization argument of Step 1 applied to each Cramer determinant). For $f$ of degree $\le d$, $\deg_X Q(X, f(X)) \le dh^2$, so if $f(p) \in S(p)$ at more than $dh^2$ points then $G(f) = 0$: every list-recovery candidate is a rational root of $G$. But Lemma 10 keeps more — the possibly irrational algebraic roots, and exact whole-fiber moment identities valid in characteristic two — and that surplus is precisely what the soundness argument will spend.

10. The valuation toolbox (Lemmas 2–5)

Soundness will need to evaluate algebraic functions at the anchors — exactly the points the evaluation set $P$ excludes, where the interesting functions may have poles, and where the roots produced by Lemma 10 may not be evaluable at all in the naive sense. The correct tool is the valuation: a bookkeeping device for orders of vanishing that survives passage to extension fields. This section builds it, self-contained except for one precisely-stated import from commutative algebra.

10.1 Orders of vanishing on the rational function field

Let $K$ be any field and $F = K(X)$ its rational function field. Fix $a \in K$ and abbreviate $\ell_a = X - a$. A rational function need not be evaluable at $a$ ($1/\ell_a$ has a pole there), but it always has a well-defined order at $a$. For a nonzero polynomial $g \in K[X]$, let $\mathrm{ord}_{\ell_a}(g)$ be the largest $m \ge 0$ with $\ell_a^m \mid g$; equivalently $g = \ell_a^m g_0$ with $g_0(a) \ne 0$. Extend to nonzero rational functions by $$\mathrm{ord}_{\ell_a}\!\Big(\frac{g}{h}\Big) = \mathrm{ord}_{\ell_a}(g) - \mathrm{ord}_{\ell_a}(h), \qquad \mathrm{ord}_{\ell_a}(0) = +\infty,$$ which is independent of the representation $g/h$ by unique factorization in $K[X]$. Positive order means a zero at $a$, negative a pole, zero a finite nonzero value (Fig. 28); for instance $\mathrm{ord}_{\ell_a}(\ell_a^3) = 3$, $\mathrm{ord}_{\ell_a}(1/\ell_a) = -1$, $\mathrm{ord}_{\ell_a}(c) = 0$ for $c \in K^\times$.

Definition (valuation). An (additive, rational-valued) valuation on a field $E$ is a map $v : E \to \mathbb{Q} \cup \{+\infty\}$ such that for all $x, y \in E$: $$v(x) = +\infty \iff x = 0, \qquad v(xy) = v(x) + v(y), \qquad v(x + y) \ge \min\{v(x), v(y)\}.$$ The third axiom is the ultrametric inequality. Multiplicativity forces $v(1) = 0$ (from $1\cdot 1 = 1$), hence $v(-1) = 0$ (from $(-1)^2 = 1$), $v(-x) = v(x)$, and $v(x^j) = j\,v(x)$ for $x \ne 0$, $j \ge 0$.

The reader should check (immediately, from unique factorization) that $\mathrm{ord}_{\ell_a}$ is a valuation on $F$ with integer values. The one non-obvious — and endlessly useful — consequence of the axioms is that a strict minimum survives addition (Fig. 29):

Lemma 2 (minimum-valuation principle). Let $v$ be a valuation on $E$. If $v(x) \ne v(y)$ then $v(x+y) = \min\{v(x), v(y)\}$. More generally, if $r \ge 2$ and $x_1, \dots, x_r \in E^\times$ are such that exactly one $x_j$ attains the minimum valuation, then $$v\Big( \sum_{i=1}^r x_i \Big) = v(x_j);$$ in particular the sum is not zero.
Say $v(x) < v(y)$. The ultrametric axiom gives $v(x+y) \ge v(x)$. If the inequality were strict, then applying the axiom to $x = (x+y) + (-y)$ would give $$v(x) \ge \min\{v(x+y),\, v(y)\} > v(x),$$ a contradiction (using $v(-y) = v(y)$). For the general case set $y = \sum_{i\ne j} x_i$; repeated use of the axiom gives $v(y) \ge \min_{i \ne j} v(x_i) > v(x_j)$, and the two-term case applies to $x_j + y$. A sum of valuation $v(x_j) < +\infty$ is nonzero. ∎

10.2 Extending a valuation to an algebraic extension

Lemma 10 hands us roots $\alpha$ in a finite separable extension $E/F$. To speak of “the order of $\alpha - \beta_i$ at the anchor $a_i$” we must extend $\mathrm{ord}_{\ell_a}$ from $F$ to $E$: a valuation $v_a$ on $E$ with $v_a(f) = \mathrm{ord}_{\ell_a}(f)$ for all $f \in F^\times$ (normalized so $v_a(\ell_a) = 1$). Such an extension cannot always stay integer-valued: if some $u \in E$ satisfies $u^e = \ell_a$, then $e\,v_a(u) = 1$ forces $v_a(u) = 1/e$. This phenomenon — fractional orders — is ramification (Fig. 30). Existence is real mathematics; we quote the standard commutative-algebra package precisely, and then prove the lemma from it.

Import 2 (field-theory and Dedekind-domain package). The following are standard results of graduate algebra, quoted with precise statements (proofs: e.g. Atiyah–Macdonald ch. 5, 9; Lang, Algebra V–VII; Serre, Local Fields I).
(a) A finite field extension generated by elements whose minimal polynomials are separable is a separable extension (used in Lemma 3).
(b) Let $A_0$ be a principal ideal domain with fraction field $F$ and let $E/F$ be a finite separable extension. The integral closure $A = \{z \in E : z \text{ satisfies a monic polynomial over } A_0\}$ is a finitely generated $A_0$-module and a Dedekind domain with fraction field $E$.
(c) (Lying over) For every nonzero prime ideal $\mathfrak{q} \subset A_0$ there is a prime ideal $\mathfrak{p} \subset A$ with $\mathfrak{p} \cap A_0 = \mathfrak{q}$.
(d) The localization $A_{\mathfrak{p}}$ of a Dedekind domain at a nonzero prime is a discrete valuation ring: its maximal ideal is generated by a single element $t$ (a uniformizer), and every $z \in E^\times$ has a unique expression $z = t^n w$ with $n \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $w$ a unit of $A_{\mathfrak{p}}$.
Lemma 4 (existence and normalization of extended valuations). Let $K$ be a field, $F = K(X)$, $a \in K$, and $E/F$ a finite separable extension. There exists a valuation $v_a : E \to \mathbb{Q}\cup\{+\infty\}$ restricting to $\mathrm{ord}_{X-a}$ on $F^\times$, and a positive integer $e$ (the ramification index) such that $v_a(E^\times) \subseteq \frac{1}{e}\mathbb{Z}$.
Apply Import 2(b) with $A_0 = K[X]$ (a PID) to get the integral closure $A \subseteq E$, a Dedekind domain. By lying over (c) there is a prime $\mathfrak{p} \subset A$ with $\mathfrak{p} \cap K[X] = (\ell_a)$. Localize (d): $A_\mathfrak{p}$ is a discrete valuation ring with uniformizer $t$, and the exponent map $\mathrm{ord}_\mathfrak{p}(z) = n$ from the unique expression $z = t^n w$ is a valuation on $E$ with values in $\mathbb{Z}$ (multiplicativity and the ultrametric inequality follow directly from unique expression: a sum of two elements $t^{n_1}w_1 + t^{n_2}w_2$ with $n_1 \le n_2$ equals $t^{n_1}(w_1 + t^{n_2-n_1}w_2)$, and the bracket lies in $A_\mathfrak{p}$, so its $t$-exponent is $\ge 0$). Set $$e = \mathrm{ord}_\mathfrak{p}(\ell_a) > 0, \qquad v_a(z) = \frac{\mathrm{ord}_\mathfrak{p}(z)}{e}, \qquad v_a(0) = +\infty;$$ positivity of $e$ holds because $\ell_a \in \mathfrak{p}$. It remains to check the restriction property. Let $g \in K[X]\setminus\{0\}$ and factor $g = \ell_a^m g_0$ with $g_0(a) \ne 0$. Then $g_0 \notin (\ell_a) = \mathfrak{p} \cap K[X]$, so $g_0 \notin \mathfrak{p}$: as an element of $A \setminus \mathfrak{p}$ it is a unit of the localization, giving $$\mathrm{ord}_\mathfrak{p}(g) = m\, \mathrm{ord}_\mathfrak{p}(\ell_a) = e\,m, \qquad v_a(g) = m = \mathrm{ord}_{\ell_a}(g).$$ Applying this to numerator and denominator extends the identity to all of $F^\times$; and $v_a(\ell_a) = 1$. Finally $\mathrm{ord}_\mathfrak{p}(E^\times) \subseteq \mathbb{Z}$ gives $v_a(E^\times) \subseteq \frac1e \mathbb{Z}$. ∎

10.3 Residues: evaluation, generalized

For a valuation $v_a$ on $E$, the valuation ring and its maximal ideal are $$\mathcal{O} = \{z : v_a(z) \ge 0\}, \qquad \mathfrak{m} = \{z : v_a(z) > 0\},$$ and the quotient $\kappa = \mathcal{O}/\mathfrak{m}$ is the residue field. Reduction mod $\mathfrak{m}$ generalizes evaluation at $a$: for a polynomial $f \in K[X]$, the factor theorem gives $f(X) - f(a) \in \ell_a K[X]$, so $v_a(f - f(a)) \ge 1 > 0$ and $f$ has the same residue as the constant $f(a)$. Moreover distinct constants stay distinct in the residue field, because $v_a(c) = 0$ for every $c \in K^\times$. That one-line observation has a consequence we will use at the crucial moment of the endgame (§13): an element of $E$ cannot be “close to” two different constants at once.

Lemma 5 (uniqueness of a constant residue). Let $v_a$ extend $\mathrm{ord}_{X-a}$ to $E$, let $\alpha \in E$, and let $b, c \in K$. If $v_a(\alpha - b) > 0$ and $v_a(\alpha - c) > 0$, then $b = c$. (The same holds with both hypotheses strengthened to $\ge 1$, which is the form used later.)
Suppose $b \ne c$. Then $b - c \in K^\times$, so $v_a(b - c) = \mathrm{ord}_{\ell_a}(b-c) = 0$. But $$b - c = (\alpha - c) - (\alpha - b), \qquad\text{so}\qquad v_a(b-c) \ge \min\{v_a(\alpha - c),\, v_a(\alpha - b)\} > 0,$$ a contradiction. ∎

The toolbox is complete: a way to measure vanishing at anchors (Lemma 4), a guarantee that strict minima survive sums (Lemma 2), and a rigidity statement for constants (Lemma 5). Now we can take apart a low-weight solution.

11. Soundness I: support sets, Markov discard, and per-table root sets

We return to the binary system (3) and begin the hard direction. Standing assumption for §11–§13: $x \in \mathbb{F}_2^M$ is any solution of $Hx = b$ with $$\mathrm{wt}(x) \;\le\; 4 M^{1/200} R, \qquad R = (\ell+1)\lvert P\rvert.$$ No structure whatsoever is assumed beyond the constraints: fibers may hold many bits, tables may be nonsense. The goal, reached in Proposition 14, is to extract a satisfying assignment of $\varphi$ anyway.

11.1 Support sets and the Markov discard

Recall the support sets $S_\tau(p) = \{w \in K : x_{\tau,p,w} = 1\}$. Every set bit of $x$ lives in exactly one fiber, so $$\sum_{\tau \in \Theta} \sum_{p \in P} \lvert S_\tau(p) \rvert = \mathrm{wt}(x) \le 4M^{1/200} R .$$ To apply Lemma 10 we need bounded fibers, so for each table we keep only the evaluation points where the fiber is small: $$P_\tau := \{\, p \in P : \lvert S_\tau(p)\rvert \le K_{\mathrm{cap}} \,\}, \qquad K_{\mathrm{cap}} = N^4 .$$ This is analysis-side bookkeeping only: the affine system and its moment polynomials remain indexed by the full $P$; nothing about the instance changes. How many points can a single table lose? A total mass of $\mathrm{wt}(x)$ spread over fibers allows at most $\mathrm{wt}(x)/K_{\mathrm{cap}}$ fibers of size exceeding $K_{\mathrm{cap}}$ — Markov's inequality in its most elementary form (Fig. 31). Quantitatively, using $M \le 40N^{401}$ from (4) and $R = (\ell+1)(q-m) \le Nq$: $$\lvert P \setminus P_\tau \rvert \;\le\; \frac{1}{K_{\mathrm{cap}}} \sum_{p} \lvert S_\tau(p)\rvert \;\le\; \frac{4M^{1/200}R}{N^4} \;\le\; 4 \cdot 40^{1/200}\, q\, N^{401/200 - 3} \;=\; 4 \cdot 40^{1/200}\, q\, N^{-199/200} \;<\; \frac{q}{20}$$ for $N \ge 100$ (at $N = 100$ the middle quantity is $\approx 0.042\,q$; the inequality is verified exactly, without floating point, in §17). Hence every table retains most of the evaluation set: $$\lvert P_\tau \rvert \;>\; \lvert P \rvert - \frac{q}{20} \qquad (\tau \in \Theta). \tag{13}$$ The parameter choices also give the two workhorse estimates $$\lvert P\rvert - \frac{q}{20} - N^9 \;>\; \frac{q}{2}, \qquad\qquad \frac{q}{2} \;>\; 2N^{39}, \tag{14}$$ using $\lvert P\rvert = q - m$, $m \le N$, $q \ge N^{200}$. Note the different tables' retained sets $P_\tau$ may differ; no common retained set is ever needed.

11.2 Each table becomes a root set

Fix $\tau \in \Theta$. Constraint (C3) holds on the full evaluation set: for each $0 \le j \le T$ the moment vector lies in $\mathrm{RS}(P, dj)$, so there is a unique polynomial $\mu_{\tau,j}(X)$, $\deg \le dj$, with $$\mu_{\tau,j}(p) = \sum_{w \in S_\tau(p)} w^j \qquad (p \in P).$$ Restricting attention to $P_\tau \subseteq P$ keeps the same polynomials and verifies condition (M2) of Definition 9 for the family $\mathcal{S}_\tau := (S_\tau(p))_{p \in P_\tau}$; condition (M1) holds by the very definition of $P_\tau$. The numerical hypotheses of Lemma 10 (on the ground set $P_\tau$) hold with room to spare: $$T = N^{30} \ \ge\ 2N^4 - 1 = 2K_{\mathrm{cap}} - 1,$$ $$\lvert P_\tau\rvert - dK_{\mathrm{cap}}(K_{\mathrm{cap}}-1) \;\overset{(13)}{>}\; \lvert P\rvert - \frac{q}{20} - N^9 \;\overset{(14)}{>}\; \frac{q}{2} \;\overset{(14)}{>}\; 2N^{39} \;\ge\; 2dK_{\mathrm{cap}}^2 T,$$ $$dT \le N^{31} < \frac q2 < \lvert P_\tau \rvert$$ (using $d = m \le N$, $dK_{\mathrm{cap}}(K_{\mathrm{cap}}-1) < N\cdot N^8 = N^9$, and $2dK_{\mathrm{cap}}^2T \le 2N\cdot N^8 \cdot N^{30} = 2N^{39}$). So $\mathcal{S}_\tau$ is a $(d, T, K_{\mathrm{cap}})$-bounded polynomial-moment family on $P_\tau$, and Lemma 10 applies separately to each table, yielding a monic separable $$G_\tau(Y) \in F[Y], \qquad h_\tau = \deg_Y G_\tau = \max_{p\in P_\tau} \lvert S_\tau(p)\rvert \le K_{\mathrm{cap}} .$$ By Lemma 3 there is one finite separable extension $E/F$ in which all the polynomials $G_\tau$ ($\tau \in \Theta$) split; fix it once and for all and write $$R_\tau = \{\, \alpha \in E : G_\tau(\alpha) = 0 \,\},$$ a set of exactly $h_\tau$ distinct elements — the root set of table $\tau$ ($R_0$ for the global table, $R_{(C,\beta)}$ for clause subtypes). Lemma 10(i) turns each table's moment polynomials into power sums of its roots: $$\mu_{\tau, j}(X) \;=\; \sum_{\alpha \in R_\tau} \alpha^{j} \qquad (\tau \in \Theta,\ 0 \le j \le T). \tag{15}$$ Keep the logic straight: the polynomials $\mu_{\tau,j}$ come from constraints on all of $P$; only the application of Lemma 10 used the trimmed sets $P_\tau$, and the conclusion (15) is an identity in $K(X)$, free of any evaluation set.

11.3 The global table is nonempty

Corollary 11. The global root set $R_0$ is nonempty (indeed $\lvert R_0\rvert$ is odd).
Constraint (C1) says every global fiber has odd parity: $\mu_{0,0}(p) = 1$ for all $p \in P$. The constant polynomial $1$ interpolates, so $\mu_{0,0} = 1$ identically. Now (15) at $\tau = 0$, $j = 0$ reads $$1 = \sum_{\alpha \in R_0} \alpha^0 = \lvert R_0 \rvert \cdot 1_K \quad\text{in } K,$$ which in characteristic two says exactly that $\lvert R_0\rvert$ is odd — in particular positive. ∎

This is constraint (C1) doing precisely the job it was hired for: parity is the only aspect of “fiber size” that characteristic two can see, and oddness is the property that survives into the root count. The elements of $R_0$ are the candidates for “the assignment polynomial hiding inside the solution” — except they are algebraic functions now, not polynomials, and nothing yet connects them to clauses. That is the work of the next two sections.

12. Soundness II: roots remember clause bits (Lemma 12)

Here is the payoff of the shifted moments (C4). Each clause table $(C,\beta)$ committed to a local assignment $\beta$; the following lemma shows that this commitment is burned into every root of the table's polynomial $G_{(C,\beta)}$: at the anchor $a_i$ of each clause variable, the root is infinitesimally close to the committed bit $\beta_i$. The proof is the chapter's technical summit — a pure valuation-theory argument, using every tool from §10.

Setup: for each variable index $i$, the anchor $a_i$ lies outside $P$, and by Lemma 4 there is a valuation $v_i$ on the common splitting field $E$ extending $\mathrm{ord}_{X - a_i}$. Fix one such $v_i$ per variable, once and for all — the same $v_i$ is used for every clause table involving variable $i$. (This shared choice is what will let different clauses talk to each other in §13.)

Lemma 12 (local valuation extraction). Let $\tau = (C, \beta)$ be a clause subtype, $i \in I_C$, and $E/F$ the common finite separable splitting field of §11. Then for every root $\alpha \in R_\tau$, $$v_i(\alpha - \beta_i) \;\ge\; 1. \tag{16}$$

Write $\ell_i = X - a_i$, $h = h_\tau$, and enumerate the distinct roots as $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_h$; there is nothing to prove if $h = 0$. Abbreviate this table's Hankel determinant $\Delta_h(X) = \det(\mu_{\tau, r+s}(X))_{0\le r,s<h}$.

Step 0: the shifted moments are polynomials tied to the ordinary ones. Recall from (2) that $\eta_{\tau,i,j}(X)$ is the unique polynomial of degree $\le (d-1)j$ interpolating the shifted moment vector. Pointwise, for each $p \in P$ and $w \in S_\tau(p)$, the binomial theorem gives $(w - \beta_i)^j = \sum_{l=0}^j \binom{j}{l} (-\beta_i)^{j-l} w^l$ (an identity with integer coefficients, valid in any characteristic); multiplying the definition of $\eta_{\tau,i,j}(p)$ by $(p - a_i)^j$ and summing over the fiber: $$\ell_i^{\,j}\, \eta_{\tau,i,j}(X) \;=\; \sum_{l=0}^{j} \binom{j}{l} (-\beta_i)^{j-l}\, \mu_{\tau,l}(X) \tag{17}$$ holds at every $p \in P$; both sides are polynomials of degree $\le dj < \lvert P\rvert$, so by the root bound (17) is an identity in $K[X]$ — not merely an agreement on the sampled grid.

Step 1: recentered roots and their power sums. Define the recentered, rescaled roots $$y_s \;=\; \frac{\alpha_s - \beta_i}{\ell_i} \;\in\; E \qquad (1 \le s \le h).$$ Combining (17) with the root-moment identities (15) — expand $(\alpha_s - \beta_i)^j$ binomially, sum over $s$, and compare with (17) — yields $$\sum_{s=1}^{h} y_s^{\,j} \;=\; \eta_{\tau,i,j}(X) \qquad (0 \le j \le T). \tag{18}$$ The right-hand side is a polynomial in $X$, so every displayed power sum has $v_i \ge 0$. The whole game is now: the $y_s$ have nonnegative-valuation power sums, and we must conclude each $y_s$ itself has nonnegative valuation, i.e. $v_i(\alpha_s - \beta_i) = v_i(y_s \ell_i) = v_i(y_s) + 1 \ge 1$.

Step 2: the $y_s$ satisfy a monic equation with controlled coefficients. Write $G_\tau(Y) = Y^h + \sum_{l<h} c_l Y^l$ and $D_0 = dh^2 + h$. Lemma 10(iii) gives $c_l = n_l/\Delta_h$ with polynomial numerator and denominator, so $$v_i(c_l) = \mathrm{ord}_{\ell_i}(n_l) - \mathrm{ord}_{\ell_i}(\Delta_h) \ \ge\ 0 - \deg \Delta_h \ \ge\ -dh(h-1) \ \ge\ -dh^2$$ (a nonzero polynomial's order at a point is between $0$ and its degree). The $y_s$ are precisely the roots of the recentered polynomial $$\ell_i^{-h}\, G_\tau(\ell_i Y + \beta_i) \;=\; Y^h + \sum_{l < h} b_l\, Y^l, \qquad b_l = \ell_i^{\,l-h} \Bigg[ \binom{h}{l} \beta_i^{\,h-l} + \sum_{k'=l}^{h-1} c_{k'} \binom{k'}{l} \beta_i^{\,k'-l} \Bigg] \in F,$$ (substitute $Y \mapsto \ell_i Y + \beta_i$ and expand binomially) and each coefficient obeys, term by term, $$v_i(b_l) \;\ge\; (l - h) + \min(0,\ \min_{k'} v_i(c_{k'})) \;\ge\; -h - dh^2 \;=\; -D_0 . \tag{†}$$ Set $b_h = 1$. If some $y_s = 0$, then $v_i(y_s) = +\infty$ and (16) already holds for that root; so fix $s$ with $y_s \ne 0$ and let $t_s = v_i(y_s) \in \mathbb{Q}$.

Step 3: a floor. If $t_s < -D_0$, then in the vanishing sum $y_s^h + \sum_{l<h} b_l y_s^l = 0$ the leading term would be the unique minimum-valuation summand: for every $l < h$ with $b_l \ne 0$, $$v_i(b_l\, y_s^{\,l}) \ \ge\ -D_0 + l\, t_s \ >\ (h-l)\,t_s + l\,t_s \ =\ v_i(y_s^{\,h}),$$ using $(h-l)t_s \le t_s < -D_0$. By Lemma 2 such a sum cannot vanish — contradiction. Hence $$t_s \ \ge\ -D_0. \tag{19}$$

Step 4: negative means substantially negative. Suppose $t_s < 0$. Since $\sum_{l=0}^{h} b_l y_s^l = 0$ (with $b_h = 1$), Lemma 2 forces the minimum of $v_i(b_l) + l\,t_s$ over the indices with $b_l \neq 0$ to be attained at least twice: say at $l < k'$. The coefficients $b_l$ lie in $F$, where $v_i$ is the integer-valued $\mathrm{ord}_{\ell_i}$, so $$(k' - l)\, t_s \;=\; v_i(b_l) - v_i(b_{k'}) \;\in\; \mathbb{Z}, \qquad 1 \le k' - l \le h,$$ i.e. $t_s$ is a fraction with denominator at most $h$; being negative, it satisfies $$t_s \le -\tfrac1h. \tag{20}$$ This is the pivot of the whole proof (Fig. 32): ramification can shrink valuations, but only down to $1/h$-quanta — a negative $t_s$ cannot sneak arbitrarily close to zero.

Step 5: amplification through the moment window. Let $V = (y_s^{\,j})_{0 \le j < h,\ 1 \le s \le h}$, the Vandermonde matrix of the $y_s$. Since $y_s - y_r = (\alpha_s - \alpha_r)/\ell_i$, Proposition G and equation (8) give $$(\det V)^2 = \prod_{r<s} (y_s - y_r)^2 = \ell_i^{-h(h-1)}\, \Delta_h .$$ As $\Delta_h \in K[X]\setminus\{0\}$, we get $0 \le v_i(\Delta_h) \le \deg \Delta_h \le dh(h-1)$; so $V$ is invertible and $$2\, v_i(\det V) = v_i(\Delta_h) - h(h-1) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_i(\det V) \le \tfrac12 (d-1)h(h-1) \le dh^2 .$$ By (19) every entry of $V$ has $v_i \ge (h-1)(-D_0) \ge -hD_0$, so every cofactor (a sum of products of $h - 1$ entries) has $v_i \ge -h^2 D_0$, and the inverse $V^{-1} = \mathrm{adj}(V)/\det V$ satisfies $$v_i\big( (V^{-1})_{s,j} \big) \ \ge\ -h^2 D_0 - dh^2 \ \ge\ -U_h, \qquad U_h := h^2 D_0 + dh^2 + 1 \ \le\ 4N^{17}. \tag{21}$$ Now choose the amplification exponent $$z = h\,U_h + 1;$$ the required moments exist because $z + h - 1 \le 4N^{21} + N^4 \le 5N^{21} < T = N^{30}$. Equation (18) at exponents $z, z+1, \dots, z+h-1$ reads, in matrix form, $$V \begin{pmatrix} y_1^{\,z} \\ \vdots \\ y_h^{\,z} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \eta_{\tau,i,z} \\ \vdots \\ \eta_{\tau,i,z+h-1} \end{pmatrix},$$ (the $j$-th row is $\sum_s y_s^{\,j}\, y_s^{\,z} = \sum_s y_s^{\,z+j}$). The right-hand entries are polynomials: $v_i \ge 0$. Applying $V^{-1}$ and the ultrametric inequality entrywise with (21): $$v_i\big( y_s^{\,z} \big) \ \ge\ -U_h \qquad (1 \le s \le h).$$ But if $t_s < 0$, then by (20) $$v_i\big( y_s^{\,z} \big) = z\, t_s \ \le\ -\frac{hU_h + 1}{h} \ =\ -U_h - \frac1h \ <\ -U_h,$$ a contradiction. Hence $t_s \ge 0$ for every root, i.e. $v_i(\alpha_s - \beta_i) = t_s + 1 \ge 1$, which is (16). ∎

Pause on what was just proved. The table $(C,\beta)$ was an arbitrary slice of a hostile low-weight solution — yet every one of its algebraic roots evaluates (in the residue sense of §10.3) to the committed bit $\beta_i$ at the anchor $a_i$, for every variable $i$ of the clause. The mechanism to remember: had the “wrong bit” been committed, the shifted moments would carry a hidden pole at the anchor (the secant picture of Fig. 20); constraint (C4) forces them to be polynomials; and the amplification argument converts “power sums are pole-free up to exponent $T$” into “each individual root is pole-free and lands on $\beta_i$” — with the moment budget $T = N^{30}$ sized precisely so that the $z$-th-power window fits inside it.

13. Soundness III: parity matching and the endgame (Lemma 13, Proposition 14)

Two pieces remain. Lemma 12 tells us what the roots of a clause table know; we must still connect the global table's roots to the clause tables (that is constraint (C2)'s job), and then glue the per-clause information into one assignment.

13.1 Every global root joins some clause table

Lemma 13 (parity matching of clause roots). For each clause $C$, every $\alpha \in R_0$ belongs to $R_{(C,\beta)}$ for at least one $\beta \in B_C$.
Constraint (C2) says that at every $p \in P$ and $w \in K$, the bit $x_{0,p,w}$ equals $\sum_{\beta\in B_C} x_{(C,\beta),p,w}$ in $\mathbb{F}_2$. Multiply by $w^j$ and sum over $w$: pointwise, $\mu_{0,j}(p) = \sum_{\beta \in B_C} \mu_{(C,\beta),j}(p)$ for all $p$. Both sides are polynomials of degree $\le dj < \lvert P\rvert$, so by the root bound this is a polynomial identity for each $0 \le j \le T$. Substituting the root-moment identities (15) and moving everything to one side (characteristic two: signs are free): $$\sum_{\alpha \in R_0} \alpha^j \;+\; \sum_{\beta \in B_C} \sum_{\alpha \in R_{(C,\beta)}} \alpha^j \;=\; 0 \qquad (0 \le j \le T).$$ Let $W_C \subseteq E$ be the set of distinct roots occurring an odd number of times across these $1 + \lvert B_C\rvert$ root sets. Pairs cancel in characteristic two, so $$\sum_{\alpha \in W_C} \alpha^j = 0 \qquad (0 \le j \le T), \qquad\quad \lvert W_C \rvert \le (1 + \lvert B_C\rvert) K_{\mathrm{cap}} \le 9K_{\mathrm{cap}} < T + 1 .$$ If $W_C \ne \varnothing$, take just the first $\lvert W_C\rvert$ equations ($j = 0, \dots, \lvert W_C\rvert - 1$): they say that the Vandermonde matrix of the distinct elements of $W_C$, applied to the all-ones vector, gives zero — impossible, since that matrix is invertible (Proposition G) and the all-ones vector is not zero. So $W_C = \varnothing$: every root occurs an even number of times in total. A root $\alpha \in R_0$ occurs exactly once in $R_0$ (root sets are sets), so it must occur at least once among the clause subtype root sets of $C$ (Fig. 33). ∎

Note what the lemma does not say: it does not say the clause tables equal the global table, nor that only one $\beta$ catches $\alpha$. Parity is all that survives characteristic two — and parity is enough.

13.2 The endgame

Proposition 14 (binary nearest-codeword soundness). If the affine system (3) has a binary solution $x$ with $$\mathrm{wt}(x) \le 4M^{1/200} R, \qquad R = (\ell+1)\lvert P\rvert,$$ then $\varphi$ is satisfiable.
Fix such a solution. As verified in §11, each trimmed support family $\mathcal{S}_\tau$ satisfies Definition 9 and the numerical hypotheses of Lemma 10; apply the reconstruction lemma to each table and fix the common finite separable splitting field $E$ (Lemma 3), the root sets $R_\tau$, and — per variable $i$ — the single extended valuation $v_i$ of §12.

By Corollary 11, $R_0 \neq \varnothing$; choose one $\alpha \in R_0$ and keep it for the rest of the proof. For every clause $C$, Lemma 13 supplies a satisfying local assignment $\beta(C) \in B_C$ with $\alpha \in R_{(C,\beta(C))}$. Now the different clauses must agree about shared variables. Let the variable $v_i$ appear in two clauses $C$ and $C'$. Lemma 12, applied to the two tables $(C, \beta(C))$ and $(C', \beta(C'))$ with the same valuation $v_i$ on the same field $E$, gives $$v_i\big( \alpha - \beta(C)_i \big) \ge 1, \qquad v_i\big( \alpha - \beta(C')_i \big) \ge 1 .$$ Both $\beta(C)_i$ and $\beta(C')_i$ are the constants $0$ or $1$ of $K$; by Lemma 5 (uniqueness of a constant residue), they are equal (Fig. 34). So all selected local tuples agree wherever they overlap, and — since preprocessing removed variables occurring in no clause — the definitions $$\sigma_i := \beta(C)_i \quad \text{for any clause } C \text{ containing } v_i$$ are consistent and cover every variable, assembling one global assignment $\sigma \in \{0,1\}^m$. Each clause $C$ is satisfied by $\sigma$, because $\sigma|_{I_C} = \beta(C) \in B_C$ by construction. Hence $\varphi$ is satisfiable. ∎

Together with completeness this closes the binary gap. For every formula $\varphi$ (with the trivial cases deferred to §14): $$\varphi \text{ satisfiable} \;\Longrightarrow\; W(H,b) \le R, \qquad\qquad \varphi \text{ unsatisfiable, } Hx=b \text{ consistent} \;\Longrightarrow\; W(H,b) > 4M^{1/200} R,$$ the second implication being the contrapositive of Proposition 14. One conceptual remark before the transfer: notice how the proof used a single algebraic object — the chosen root $\alpha$ — as a coherent “virtual assignment”. The solution $x$ may be smeared across many spurious table entries, but the reconstruction lemma condenses each table into finitely many algebraic functions, and the valuations force those functions to take Boolean values at the anchors. The root $\alpha$ is, in effect, an assignment polynomial that was never written down.

14. Transfer to CVP: the proof of Theorem 1

All the pieces exist; this section is the final assembly — including the unglamorous but necessary bookkeeping that makes the map total (defined on every formula) and its guarantees strict. Fig. 35 is the algorithm.

Proof of Theorem 1. We describe the map $\varphi \mapsto (B, t, r)$ branch by branch, then verify the two promise implications and the running time.

Degenerate branches. After the preprocessing of §6: if no clauses remain, $\varphi$ is satisfiable; return the fixed YES-instance $(B,t,r) = ((1), (0), 1)$, whose distance is $0 \le 1 = r$. If an empty clause remains, $\varphi$ is unsatisfiable; return $$(B, t, r) = \big( (2),\ (1),\ \tfrac12 \big):$$ the lattice is $2\mathbb{Z}$, the target is $1$, so the distance is exactly $1$, and since here $n = 1$ we have $n^{1/400} r = 1 \cdot \tfrac12 = \tfrac12 < 1$ — a strict NO-instance.

Main branch. Otherwise construct the explicit affine system (3) with the parameters of §7.4, and test consistency by Gaussian elimination over $\mathbb{F}_2$. Note carefully: this tests whether $Hx=b$ has any binary solution, a polynomial-time question; it does not decide whether $\varphi$ is satisfiable, which is about low-weight solutions. If the system is inconsistent, return the fixed NO-instance above — legitimately, because by Lemma 8 a satisfiable formula always yields a consistent system, so inconsistency certifies unsatisfiability.

On the consistent branch, compute a particular solution $u \in \{0,1\}^M$ of $Hu = b$ and the parity-lift basis $B_H$ of Lemma 6 (with the inverse coordinate permutation applied). Output $$(B, t, r) \;=\; \Big( B_H,\ u,\ \big\lceil \sqrt{(\ell+1)\lvert P\rvert}\, \big\rceil \Big).$$ The dimension is $n = M$, the basis is square and nonsingular, and basis, target, and radius are integers in binary.

Completeness. If $\varphi$ is satisfiable, Lemma 8 gives a binary solution of weight $R = (\ell+1)\lvert P\rvert$, and Lemma 7's identity gives $$\mathrm{dist}_2\big(u, L(B_H)\big) = \sqrt{W(H,b)} \le \sqrt{R} \le r .$$

Soundness. Let $\varphi$ be unsatisfiable and suppose, for contradiction, that $\mathrm{dist}_2(u, L(B_H)) \le M^{c} r$ with $c = 1/400$. Lemma 7 converts distance to weight: $$W(H, b) \;=\; \mathrm{dist}_2\big(u, L(B_H)\big)^2 \;\le\; M^{1/200}\, r^2 \;\le\; 4 M^{1/200} R,$$ where $r^2 \le 4R$ because $r = \lceil \sqrt R\,\rceil \le \sqrt R + 1 \le 2\sqrt R$ (using $R \ge 1$). By Proposition 14 the formula would be satisfiable — contradiction. Hence $$\mathrm{dist}_2\big(u, L(B_H)\big) \;>\; M^{c}\, r \;=\; n^{1/400}\, r,$$ and the inequality is strict, as the promise definition requires.

Running time and size. The field construction is deterministic $N^{O(1)}$ (Proposition C, with $e = O(\log N)$); each of the $O(N^{401})$ rows of $H$ (bound (5)) is a field-arithmetic computation over $O(N^{401})$ columns (bound (4)); Gaussian elimination, the systematic form, and $B_H$ are polynomial in the $O(N^{802})$-bit dense description. Every step is deterministic. Since $N \le 100 + 3\cdot(\text{input length})$, the whole map runs in deterministic polynomial time in the length of $\varphi$. With Import 1 (3SAT is NP-complete) and the composition of reductions, $\mathrm{GapCVP}^{(2)}_{n^{1/400}}$ is NP-hard. ∎

Two small subtleties worth savoring. (i) The radius must be part of the instance and rational: $\sqrt R$ itself may be irrational, and rounding up to $\lceil\sqrt R\rceil$ costs at most the benign factor $4$ inside the soundness threshold $4M^{1/200}R$ that Proposition 14 was engineered to tolerate. (ii) The NO-guarantee must be strict ($>$, not $\ge$), and it is, both on the main branch (strict contrapositive) and on the degenerate branch ($1 > \tfrac12$). Promise-problem definitions are picky exactly here, and the paper is careful; so are we.

15. Consequences: decoding problems and every fixed $\ell_p$ norm

The binary gap $W(H,b) \le R$ versus $W(H,b) > 4M^{1/200}R$ is the reduction's real product; Euclidean CVP consumed it through Lemma 7's square root. The same gap feeds two more families of results.

15.1 Coding problems: no square root, better exponent

Corollary 15 (hardness of binary nearest codeword and syndrome decoding). Binary nearest codeword and binary syndrome decoding are NP-hard to approximate within $n^{1/200}$ under deterministic polynomial-time many-one reductions ($n$ = code block length).
Run the reduction of §14 up to the affine system. On the consistent branch, output the system itself as a syndrome-decoding instance — parity-check matrix $H$, syndrome $b$, integer radius $R = (\ell+1)\lvert P\rvert$ — or, for the nearest-codeword formulation, a particular solution $u$ and (via Gaussian elimination, $O(n^2)$ output bits) either a generator matrix or an independent-row parity-check matrix for $\mathcal{C} = \ker_{\mathbb{F}_2} H$, with $d_H(u,\mathcal{C}) = W(H,b)$ as in §4.1. Completeness is Lemma 8: satisfiable $\varphi$ gives $W(H,b) \le R$. Soundness: a binary solution of weight $\le n^{1/200} R$ is in particular of weight $\le 4M^{1/200}R$ (here $n = M$), so Proposition 14 makes $\varphi$ satisfiable; contrapositively, unsatisfiable $\varphi$ forces the strict NO-promise $d_H(u, \mathcal{C}) > n^{1/200} R$. For an inconsistent system (possible only for unsatisfiable $\varphi$, by Lemma 8), output the fixed instance: code $\{00\}$, received word $11$, radius $1$ — its distance $2$ exceeds $2^{1/200}\cdot 1$ strictly. ∎

Notice the factor is $n^{1/200}$, twice the Euclidean exponent: the coding problems are the binary gap, no square root taken. The historical context from §2.5 bears repeating: these are the problems whose exact hardness goes back to 1978, and for which PCP-free approximation hardness was previously known only for constant factors.

15.2 Every fixed rational norm

For $1 \le p < \infty$ define $\lVert x \rVert_p = (\sum_j \lvert x_j\rvert^p)^{1/p}$ and $\mathrm{dist}_p(t, L) = \min_{z \in L} \lVert t - z\rVert_p$; the $\ell_p$ closest-vector promise problem is $\mathrm{GapCVP}$ with this distance. The norm parameter $p$ is fixed (part of the problem definition, not the input). Lemma 7 already gave the exact identity $\min_\lambda \lVert u - \lambda\rVert_p^p = W(H,b)$ for the parity-lift instance; the only work left is choosing a rational radius close to $R^{1/p}$, which is where the restriction to rational $p$ enters.

Corollary 16 (hardness for fixed finite rational norms). Fix a rational $p \ge 1$. There is a deterministic polynomial-time many-one reduction from 3SAT to the full-rank $\ell_p$ closest-vector promise problem with approximation factor $n^{1/(200p)}$; the output basis is square and integral, and target and radius have polynomial-size exact rational encodings.
Write $p = a/b$ in lowest terms and set the fixed integer $A = \lceil 4p \rceil$. Given $R = (\ell+1)\lvert P\rvert$, compute $$j_p = \min\{ j \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0} : j^a \ge A^a R^b \}, \qquad r_p = \frac{j_p}{A}.$$ The defining condition says exactly $j \ge A R^{b/a} = A R^{1/p}$, so $j_p = \lceil A R^{1/p}\rceil$ is computable by fixed-degree integer exponentiation plus binary search, in deterministic polynomial time, and $$R^{1/p} \;\le\; r_p \;\le\; \Big(1 + \frac1A\Big) R^{1/p}, \qquad r_p^{\,p} \;\le\; e^{p/A} R \;\le\; e^{1/4} R \;<\; 2R,$$ using $(1 + 1/A)^p \le e^{p/A}$ and $A \ge 4p$. On the consistent branch output the same $(B_H, u)$ with the $\ell_p$ norm and radius $r_p$. Completeness: satisfiable $\varphi$ gives $\mathrm{dist}_p(u, L(B_H))^p = W(H,b) \le R \le r_p^{\,p}$. Soundness: if $\mathrm{dist}_p(u, L(B_H)) \le n^{1/(200p)}\, r_p$, then Lemma 7 gives $$W(H,b) = \mathrm{dist}_p(u, L(B_H))^p \le n^{1/200}\, r_p^{\,p} < 2\, n^{1/200} R \le 4\, n^{1/200} R,$$ and Proposition 14 (with $n = M$) makes $\varphi$ satisfiable; contrapositively the unsatisfiable case satisfies the strict NO-promise. On an inconsistent system, the fixed output $((2), (1), \tfrac12)$ has $\ell_p$ distance $1 > \tfrac12$ for every finite $p$. ∎

Setting $p = 2$ in Corollary 16 recovers exactly Theorem 1 (with a rational radius rather than an integer one — either form is fine for the promise problem). The uniformity is worth noting: one construction, one soundness proof, and the entire family of norms inherits the gap through the single exact identity of Lemma 7.

16. Scope — what is proved, what is open

A results chapter earns trust by stating its edges. Here they are.

16.1 What the theorem delivers

  • Unconditional, deterministic, PCP-free. The only unproven ingredient is the definition of NP-hardness itself: the reduction is a concrete deterministic algorithm, and the two imports (Cook–Levin; the Dedekind package) are classical theorems with textbook proofs, not conjectures. This distinguishes the result from the earlier conditional fixed-polynomial hardness under the Projection Games Conjecture.
  • Fixed-polynomial factor, honest dimension. The factor $n^{1/400}$ is measured against the rank $n = M$ of the very lattice output by the reduction; the companion factors $n^{1/200}$ (coding) and $n^{1/(200p)}$ ($\ell_p$) are measured the same way.
  • A reusable algebraic tool. Lemma 10 — reconstruction of bounded unordered families from polynomial power sums, in any characteristic, with quantitative degree control — is proved independently of the reduction and strengthens the Reed–Solomon list-recovery toolbox (it retains non-rational algebraic roots and exact whole-fiber identities).

16.2 What it does not deliver

  • No practical statement about lattice cryptography. The instances produced are astronomically large polynomials of the input ($\dim \approx N^{401}$, $\approx N^{802}$ bits): the theorem is an asymptotic classification, not an attack, and NIST-standardized schemes rest on structured, average-case problems that worst-case NP-hardness neither breaks nor certifies. The connection is directional: it maps which approximation regimes are intractable in the worst case.
  • The exponent is not optimized. $1/400$ is the output of a deliberately slack budget cascade ($K = N^4$, $T = N^{30}$, $q \approx N^{200}$); the paper makes no optimality claim, and tightening the cascade would improve the constant without new ideas — but reaching materially larger exponents likely needs new techniques.
  • The window to $1/2$ is wide open. Between $1/400$ and the Aharonov–Regev barrier (NP-hardness at $C\sqrt n$ would give $\mathsf{NP} = \mathsf{coNP}$) nothing is known (Fig. 37). Determining $\sup\{c : \mathrm{GapCVP}^{(2)}_{n^c} \text{ NP-hard}\}$ is the natural next question.
  • No shortest-vector consequence. The reduction leans on the affine structure — a target, a coset $u + \mathcal{C}$, a particular solution. The homogeneous shortest vector problem has no target; Bennett–Peikert's Reed–Solomon-based SVP hardness remains randomized, and derandomizing it is explicitly open (their paper discusses the obstacles).
  • Norms: fixed and rational only. Corollary 16 needs $p$ fixed (the constant $A = \lceil 4p\rceil$ and the exponent enter the problem definition) and rational (to compute the radius exactly); $\ell_\infty$ is not covered here, and the factor $n^{1/(200p)}$ degrades as $p$ grows.
  • Orthogonal to fine-grained lower bounds. Recent ETH-based results (Aggarwal–Gupta–Morolia–Zhang; Huang–Ko–Wang, both 2026) bound running times for constant-factor approximation; this chapter bounds nothing about running time but places a fixed-polynomial factor beyond polynomial-time reach unless $\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{NP}$. The two kinds of statement complement each other.

16.3 Where a skeptic should audit

Everything in this document is proved except two boxed imports, so a verification effort concentrates on: (i) Import 1 — Cook–Levin, standard; (ii) Import 2 — the separability and Dedekind-domain package feeding Lemmas 3 and 4, standard commutative algebra; (iii) the parameter inequalities, which are finitely many integer comparisons — and those we verify by machine, next section, alongside an end-to-end execution of the construction on the toy formula. The toy runs at parameters far below the theorem's regime ($q = 16$, $T = 4$; the honest thresholds need $q \approx N^{200}$), so it validates the mechanics — every identity, every constraint, every conversion — not the asymptotic inequalities, which get their own exact-arithmetic checks at $N = 100$.
